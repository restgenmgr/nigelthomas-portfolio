Why Every F&B Professional Must Master These Basics

In the fast‑paced world of hospitality, the difference between an average employee and an exceptional one often comes down to a solid grasp of fundamental concepts. Whether you’re a fresh graduate preparing for your first restaurant interview, a seasoned waiter aiming for a promotion, or a hiring manager looking to benchmark candidates, knowing the core questions—and the best answers—is non‑negotiable.

This guide compiles 26 of the most frequently asked Food & Beverage interview questions, drawn from real‑world recruitment across hotels, fine‑dining restaurants, and catering companies. But we don’t just give you the textbook answer. For each question, we break down why it’s asked, what interviewers really want to hear, and how to deliver a response that sets you apart. By the end of this article, you’ll have a complete repertoire that demonstrates technical knowledge, customer‑centric thinking, and operational awareness.

26 Essential Questions – Expanded with Expert Insights

Below, each question is followed by the core answer (as in the poster) and then a deeper dive into the reasoning, common pitfalls, and pro tips that turn a good answer into a great one.

1What is Food & Beverage service?
Core Answer: Food & Beverage service involves preparing, presenting, and serving food and drinks to guests in hotels, restaurants, and catering establishments.
Why it matters: This question tests your understanding of the entire service cycle—not just the serving part. Interviewers want to see that you view F&B as an integrated process that starts with menu planning, passes through kitchen preparation, and ends with guest satisfaction. Mention that service includes pre‑prep (mise en place), actual service, and post‑service (clearing, billing, guest feedback). A strong answer shows you appreciate the team effort involved.
2What are the types of F&B service?
Core Answer: Table service, Buffet service, Room service, Self-service, and Takeaway service.
Why it matters: This is a knowledge check. But a great candidate will elaborate: for example, table service can be further broken into French, American, Russian, or English service. Buffet may be hot/cold or themed. Room service requires specific logistics. Mentioning these nuances shows you’ve worked in diverse environments or have studied the trade. Also, acknowledge that hybrid models (e.g., counter service + table delivery) are becoming popular.
3What is mise en place?
Core Answer: “Everything in place” – preparing and organizing all items before service begins.
Why it matters: Mise en place is the backbone of kitchen and service efficiency. Interviewers love this question because it separates the organised from the chaotic. Give a concrete example: “Before a shift, I check my station has enough clean cutlery, glassware, napkins, condiments, and that the sideboard is stocked. I also verify that the POS system is working and that I have the day’s specials memorised.” This shows you don’t just know the term—you live it.
4What is a menu?
Core Answer: A list of food and beverages offered to guests along with their prices.
Why it matters: Simple, but you can elevate it by mentioning different types of menus (à la carte, table d’hôte, cycle menu, tasting menu) and how menu engineering affects sales. You could add: “A menu is also a marketing tool—it should be descriptive, appealing, and organised to guide the guest toward profitable items.” That demonstrates commercial awareness.
5What is the role of a waiter?
Core Answer: Takes orders, serves food and beverages, ensures guest satisfaction, and maintains service standards.
Why it matters: This is your chance to show you see beyond the basic duties. Talk about being an ambassador for the restaurant, anticipating guest needs, upselling, handling complaints gracefully, and collaborating with the kitchen. Mention that a great waiter also educates guests about the menu, suggests pairings, and creates a memorable experience—not just a transactional one.
6What is customer service in F&B?
Core Answer: Providing polite, efficient, and friendly service to ensure a positive dining experience.
Why it matters: This is a values question. Go beyond the textbook and describe how you personalise service: remembering regular guests’ preferences, offering a genuine smile, handling special requests with a “can‑do” attitude. Interviewers want to see empathy and emotional intelligence. Mention that customer service isn’t just about the guest—it’s also about supporting colleagues to create a seamless experience.
7What is table setting?
Core Answer: Arranging cutlery, crockery, glassware, and napkins properly on the table before service.
Why it matters: Precision matters in fine dining. You can impress by describing the standard cover layout (e.g., fork left, knife right, spoon right, glassware above the knives). Also, mention that table setting varies by cuisine (e.g., Asian settings) and that attention to detail—like ensuring all logos face the guest—reflects the establishment’s standards.
8What is order taking?
Core Answer: The process of accurately noting guests’ food and beverage requests.
Why it matters: Accuracy is non‑negotiable. Share your method: repeat the order back to the guest, note any modifications (e.g., “no onion, extra sauce”), use a system to avoid confusion (e.g., seat numbers), and double‑check with the kitchen. Mention that order taking is also a moment to upsell—you can suggest a starter or a wine that pairs with the main course.
9What is upselling?
Core Answer: Encouraging guests to purchase higher‑value items or additional products.
Why it matters: This is a revenue‑driving skill. A strong answer gives examples: “If a guest orders a pasta, I might suggest adding a prawn topping or a side salad. For drinks, I might recommend a premium spirit or a bottle of wine instead of a glass.” But emphasize that upselling must feel natural and guest‑centric—it’s about enhancing their experience, not being pushy.
10What is hygiene in F&B?
Core Answer: Maintaining cleanliness in food handling, personal grooming, and the workplace to ensure safety.
Why it matters: Hygiene is a legal and ethical cornerstone. Don’t just list the basics—talk about temperature control, cross‑contamination prevention, handwashing protocols, and the importance of HACCP. Mention that personal hygiene (clean uniform, tied hair, no jewellery) is as important as kitchen cleanliness. This shows you take food safety seriously.
11What is a cover?
Core Answer: A cover refers to one guest served in a restaurant.
Why it matters: This is operational vocabulary. You can add: “Covers are used to measure occupancy and productivity—for example, a restaurant might aim for 100 covers per dinner service.” This shows you understand the business side of hospitality.
12What is billing?
Core Answer: Preparing and presenting the guest’s bill after service.
Why it matters: Billing is more than just adding numbers. It involves accuracy, clarity, and handling payment methods (cash, cards, vouchers). Mention that you always double‑check the order against the bill and offer to explain any charges if the guest asks. Also, it’s a final touchpoint—a smile and a thank‑you can leave a lasting positive impression.
13What is room service?
Core Answer: Serving food and beverages to guests in their hotel rooms.
Why it matters: This is a specialised service. Describe the process: taking orders via phone or in‑room tablet, preparing trays with all necessary items (cutlery, condiments, napkins), delivering promptly, and setting up the table. Also, mention that room service requires extra care with presentation and temperature maintenance. It’s a key revenue stream for hotels.
14What is a steward?
Core Answer: A staff member responsible for serving food and maintaining cleanliness in the restaurant.
Why it matters: The term can vary—some places use “steward” for back‑of‑house (dishwashers, cleaning), while others use it for front‑of‑house assistants. Clarify your understanding: “In my experience, a steward supports the waitstaff by clearing tables, polishing glassware, and ensuring the sideboard is fully stocked.” This shows adaptability and awareness of team roles.
15Why is communication important in F&B?
Core Answer: It helps understand guest needs, avoid mistakes, and deliver better service.
Why it matters: Communication is the glue. Give a concrete example: “During a busy service, clear communication between the kitchen and waitstaff prevents order errors. Also, active listening with guests ensures we meet their expectations.” Mention both verbal and non‑verbal communication (body language, eye contact). Show that you’re a team player who values feedback.
16What is a restaurant?
Core Answer: A place where food and beverages are prepared and served to customers.
Why it matters: Simple, but you can add depth: “A restaurant is not just a physical space—it’s a hospitality experience that combines ambiance, service, and food quality. It can be a quick‑service outlet, a fine‑dining establishment, or anything in between.” This shows you see the bigger picture.
17What is fine dining?
Core Answer: A formal dining experience with high‑quality food, elegant ambiance, and professional service.
Why it matters: This tests your knowledge of service levels. Elaborate on the characteristics: multiple courses, elaborate table settings, extensive wine list, highly trained staff, and often a dress code. Mention that fine dining requires meticulous attention to detail and a passion for hospitality. If you have experience, share a brief anecdote.
18What is casual dining?
Core Answer: A relaxed dining style with moderate pricing and informal service.
Why it matters: Contrast with fine dining. Casual dining still demands good service and quality food, but the atmosphere is more laid‑back. Mention that you understand the different expectations—guests in casual dining may prefer quicker service and less formal interaction, but friendliness is still key.
19What is a buffet?
Core Answer: A self‑service style where guests serve themselves from a variety of dishes.
Why it matters: Buffets require different operational planning: food replenishment, temperature control, and guest flow management. Show that you can think about logistics—how to keep food fresh, avoid overcrowding, and maintain cleanliness. Also, mention that buffets are popular for events and hotels.
20What is à la carte?
Core Answer: A menu where each item is priced and ordered separately.
Why it matters: This is a classic menu type. You can add: “À la carte offers guests maximum flexibility, and it allows the restaurant to showcase a wide range of dishes. It also means waitstaff need to be knowledgeable about every dish to answer questions.” This shows you understand the operational implications.
21What is table d’hôte?
Core Answer: A fixed menu with a set price and limited choices.
Why it matters: Explain that table d’hôte is often used for banquets, set lunches, or group events. It simplifies kitchen operations and inventory planning. A good answer might note that it can be more cost‑effective for guests and allows for better portion control.
22What is a cover charge?
Core Answer: A fixed fee charged per guest, sometimes including basic items or entertainment.
Why it matters: This is a pricing strategy. Mention that cover charges are common in fine dining or clubs, and they may cover bread, amuse‑bouche, or live music. You can also note that some guests may expect it, so transparency is important.
23What is a cocktail?
Core Answer: An alcoholic mixed drink made with spirits, mixers, and flavorings.
Why it matters: This is a basic beverage knowledge question. Show you know the difference between classic cocktails (Martini, Margarita) and modern craft cocktails. Mention that cocktails are an art form and that understanding flavor profiles helps in recommending drinks to guests.
24What is a mocktail?
Core Answer: A non‑alcoholic mixed drink.
Why it matters: Mocktails are increasingly popular—they cater to designated drivers, minors, and health‑conscious guests. A good answer might mention that mocktails should be as creative and well‑presented as cocktails, often using fresh juices, syrups, and garnishes.
25What is a sideboard?
Core Answer: A service station used to store extra cutlery, napkins, and service items.
Why it matters: This is about operational efficiency. Explain that a well‑organised sideboard reduces the need to run to the kitchen constantly, speeding up service. Mention that you keep it restocked and tidy throughout the shift.
26What is a KOT?
Core Answer: KOT (Kitchen Order Ticket) is used to communicate food orders to the kitchen.
Why it matters: This is a practical, system‑based term. Mention that in modern restaurants, KOTs are often digital (via POS systems), but the principle remains—accuracy and clarity are vital. A mistake on a KOT can lead to wrong dishes, wastage, and unhappy guests. Show that you understand the importance of double‑checking before sending.

Beyond the Answers: What Interviewers Really Look For

While knowing the correct answers is essential, the best candidates go further. They demonstrate:

  • Confidence without arrogance – they speak clearly and maintain eye contact.
  • Real‑world examples – they don’t just recite definitions; they share stories from past shifts.
  • Adaptability – they acknowledge that every restaurant has its own way of doing things and express willingness to learn.
  • Passion for hospitality – they convey genuine enthusiasm for serving people and creating great experiences.

During the interview, listen carefully to the question and tailor your answer to the specific role and establishment. For instance, if applying to a luxury hotel, emphasise fine‑dining etiquette; if it’s a high‑volume casual place, highlight speed and teamwork.

Mastering These Questions = Career Growth

Whether you’re aiming for a front‑of‑house position, a supervisory role, or a corporate F&B management job, these 26 questions are the foundation. But remember—interviewers are not just testing your knowledge; they’re testing your attitude, your problem‑solving ability, and your fit with their team culture.

Practice your answers out loud. Record yourself and listen back. Prepare follow‑up questions to ask the interviewer—this shows engagement. And most importantly, be yourself. Authenticity combined with solid knowledge is a winning formula.
