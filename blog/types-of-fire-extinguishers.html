Types of Fire Extinguishers

A hospitality manager's guide to classes A, B, C, D & K

Types of Fire Extinguishers: A Hospitality Manager's Guide

Hospitality Safety Series · By Nigel A. Thomas

Walk into any well-run hotel, restaurant, or cruise galley and you will find a red cylinder mounted at regular intervals along the wall — often glanced at, rarely thought about, until the one moment it matters more than anything else in the building. A fire extinguisher is one of the cheapest pieces of safety equipment a property will ever buy, and one of the most misunderstood. Most staff know it is "for putting out fires." Very few know that the wrong extinguisher on the wrong fire can make things dramatically worse — turning a contained flare-up into a spreading, sometimes explosive, emergency.

In hospitality operations specifically, this matters more than in almost any other industry. A single property can contain every major fire hazard category at once: wood and paper in the back office, cleaning solvents in housekeeping, LPG cylinders in the kitchen, deep fryers running at high temperature during service, and electrical panels feeding everything from walk-in chillers to guest-room air conditioning. Understanding fire extinguisher classification is not a compliance box to tick once a year for an audit — it is operational knowledge that every department head, shift supervisor, and long-serving line staff member should be able to explain without hesitation.

This guide breaks down the five extinguisher classes most relevant to hotel, restaurant, and resort operations, explains why each one exists, where it belongs on your property, and how to build the training and inspection habits that keep the equipment ready when it is actually needed.

It also matters for reasons beyond the obvious safety argument. Fire insurance underwriters routinely review extinguisher coverage, placement, and inspection records when assessing a property's risk rating and premium. Local fire departments and municipal licensing authorities inspect for the same details before renewing a hotel's operating licence. And increasingly, corporate brand standards for international hotel chains build fire safety compliance directly into their quality audit scorecards — meaning a poorly classified or poorly maintained extinguisher fleet can cost a property points on an audit long before it ever costs anyone an actual fire.

Why Classification Exists in the First Place

Fire is not a single phenomenon — it behaves differently depending on what is burning. A extinguishing agent that works brilliantly on burning paper can be actively dangerous on a grease fire, and an agent designed for flammable liquids may do nothing useful against burning metal. Extinguisher classification exists to match the extinguishing agent to the fuel source, because the chemistry of putting out a fire changes completely depending on what is feeding it.

Regulatory bodies such as the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) in the United States, and equivalent fire safety codes in India, the Gulf, and Europe, all use a version of the same lettered system. A property manager does not need to memorize the chemistry behind each agent, but does need to know, instantly, which extinguisher goes where — and which one to grab first in an emergency.

Class A — Ordinary Combustibles

Class A covers what most people picture when they imagine an accidental fire: wood, paper, cardboard, fabric, and general trash. In a hotel, this is the category most likely to be triggered by an unattended candle, a discarded cigarette, an overloaded extension cord under a stack of linen, or a wastebasket fire in a back office or storeroom.

Class A extinguishers typically use a water-based or multi-purpose dry chemical agent that cools the fuel below its ignition temperature and soaks into fibrous material to prevent re-ignition. These are the extinguishers you will most often see in corridors, guest floors, offices, laundry rooms, and storage areas — anywhere paper, cardboard, or textile waste accumulates.

A useful habit for department heads: any area where you regularly see cardboard boxes, cleaning rags, or paper stacked against a wall is a Class A risk zone, and should have a Class A (or ABC multi-purpose) extinguisher within a short, unobstructed walk.

Class B — Flammable Liquids

Class B fires involve flammable and combustible liquids — petrol, paint, solvents, thinners, and liquefied fuels. In a hospitality setting, this risk shows up in maintenance workshops, generator rooms, paint storage for property upkeep, and anywhere fuel-powered equipment such as pool pumps, groundskeeping machinery, or backup generators is stored or serviced.

Because these fires spread across the surface of a pooling liquid rather than burning through a solid fuel source, Class B extinguishers rely on smothering the fire — cutting off its oxygen supply — rather than simply cooling it. Common agents include foam, carbon dioxide (CO2), and dry chemical powder. Water is specifically dangerous on a Class B fire: it can scatter burning liquid and spread the fire rather than putting it out, which is exactly the kind of mistake that untrained staff make under panic.

Every engineering and maintenance area, generator room, and fuel storage point on a property should have a clearly marked Class B (or ABC combination) extinguisher, and staff working in those areas should specifically be told never to use water on a liquid-fuel fire.

Class C — Electrical Equipment

Class C fires involve energized electrical equipment — wiring, switchboards, control panels, outlets, and any appliance still connected to a live power source. This is one of the highest-risk categories in any hotel, given the sheer volume of wiring, transformers, server racks, kitchen equipment, and guest-room electronics running continuously across a property.

The defining rule for Class C is simple and unforgiving: never use water or a water-based extinguisher on a fire involving live electricity. Water conducts electricity, and doing so risks electrocuting the person holding the extinguisher. Class C extinguishers use non-conductive agents — typically CO2 or dry chemical powder — that can be discharged safely near live equipment.

Practically, this means electrical rooms, IT server closets, laundry equipment areas, back-of-house electrical panels, and kitchen line equipment should all have a Class C-rated extinguisher (most commercial kitchens use a combination unit rated for multiple classes) within immediate reach. It is worth noting that once power to the equipment is cut, a fire that started as electrical can behave like an ordinary Class A fire — but the golden rule during the live phase remains: no water, ever.

Class D — Combustible Metals

Class D is the category hospitality managers encounter least often, but it is critical in any property with an on-site engineering, workshop, or fabrication area — metal beams, sheet metal, shavings, swarf, and machine parts used in maintenance and repair work. Certain metals, particularly in shaving or powder form, burn at extremely high temperatures and react violently with water or standard extinguishing agents, sometimes causing explosive flare-ups.

Class D extinguishers use specialized dry powder agents designed to smother the burning metal and absorb heat without triggering a chemical reaction. These units are far less common than A, B, C, or K extinguishers, and are typically only required in engineering workshops, welding bays, or any area where metal fabrication or grinding regularly takes place. If your property runs its own maintenance workshop with grinding or welding equipment, this is worth a specific conversation with your fire safety consultant — a generic multi-purpose extinguisher will not do the job here.

Class K — Kitchen Fires

For hospitality operators, Class K deserves the most attention of all, because it governs the single highest-risk fire zone on almost every property: the commercial kitchen. Class K covers fires involving cooking oils and fats — the kind of fire that starts in a deep fryer, a flat-top griddle, or a wok station, and burns at temperatures far higher than an ordinary combustible fire.

This is precisely why Class K exists as its own category, separate from Class B. A standard flammable-liquid extinguisher can actually cause a grease fire to erupt further, because the force of discharge can splash burning oil outward. Class K extinguishers use a wet chemical agent, typically a potassium-based solution, that reacts with the hot oil to form a soapy, foam-like layer across the surface — a process called saponification. This layer smothers the fire and, critically, cools the oil below its ignition point so it does not simply reignite once the immediate flame is out.

Every commercial kitchen, whether it is a five-star hotel's main production kitchen or a small property's staff canteen, should have a Class K extinguisher mounted near — but not directly beside — the fryer and cooking line, along with an automatic kitchen suppression system over the hood where local fire code requires it. Line cooks and kitchen supervisors should be trained specifically on Class K use, since the instinct to grab whatever extinguisher is nearest can lead to the wrong agent being used on a grease fire during the exact moment when speed and correctness both matter most.

Kitchen Safety Reminder: Never throw water on a grease or deep-fryer fire. Water is denser than hot oil, sinks beneath it, and instantly flashes to steam — throwing burning oil into the air in every direction. If a stovetop pan fire starts and it is small and contained, smothering it with a metal lid (never a wet towel) can work as a first response while someone retrieves the Class K unit.

Multi-Purpose Units — Where "ABC" Extinguishers Fit In

Walk any hotel corridor and the extinguisher you are most likely to see is not labelled with a single letter at all — it is labelled "ABC," meaning it is rated for ordinary combustibles, flammable liquids, and electrical equipment in one unit. These multi-purpose dry chemical extinguishers exist because most general circulation areas — corridors, lobbies, back offices, storage rooms — face a mix of possible fire sources rather than one specific hazard, and it is neither practical nor necessary to mount three separate units side by side.

ABC units are the right default for guest floors, administrative offices, storerooms, laundry areas, and general circulation space. They are not, however, a substitute for the specialised units this guide covers elsewhere: an ABC extinguisher does not replace a Class K unit at the fryer line, and it is not rated for the concentrated metal fires covered under Class D. The rule of thumb worth teaching every department head is simple — ABC covers the general building, but high-hazard zones still need their specific class on top of, not instead of, general coverage.

It is also worth knowing that different countries and regions use slightly different labelling conventions. The letter-based A/B/C/D/K system described here is the American NFPA standard and is widely recognised across the Gulf and much of Asia. Europe uses a similar but not identical system (A, B, C, D, and F, where F corresponds closely to the American Class K for cooking oils and fats), and extinguisher colour-coding on the cylinder itself can vary by country as well. A property operating internationally, or training staff who have worked across different markets, should confirm which standard the local fire code actually requires rather than assuming the labelling they learned elsewhere applies unchanged.

The PASS Technique — How to Actually Use One

Owning the correct extinguisher only matters if staff know how to operate it under pressure. The industry-standard method, taught in almost every fire safety course worldwide, is the PASS technique:

  • Pull the safety pin at the top of the extinguisher, breaking the tamper seal.
  • Aim the nozzle or hose low, at the base of the fire — not at the flames themselves.
  • Squeeze the handle slowly and evenly to release the extinguishing agent.
  • Sweep the nozzle from side to side across the base of the fire until it is fully out.

Every new hire in a role with fire exposure — kitchen, engineering, housekeeping, front office — should physically practice this motion at least once during onboarding, ideally with a training extinguisher or during a scheduled fire drill. Reading about PASS in an induction manual is not the same as having done it once with your own hands; muscle memory is what holds up when adrenaline takes over.

Placement, Signage & Inspection Standards

Classification tells you which extinguisher to use. Placement and maintenance determine whether it will actually work when the moment comes. A few standards worth building into every property's Standard Operating Procedures:

  • Visibility and access: Extinguishers should be mounted in clearly visible, unobstructed locations, never blocked by stacked stock, trolleys, or décor — a common audit finding in busy back-of-house corridors.
  • Mounting height: Most fire codes specify a maximum mounting height (commonly around 1.2 metres / 4 feet to the top of the unit) so that any staff member can retrieve it without needing a step stool.
  • Signage: A clearly visible sign or marker above the extinguisher — not just the unit itself — helps staff locate it quickly in low visibility or smoke conditions.
  • Monthly visual checks: Someone on shift should confirm the pressure gauge reads in the operable range, the pin and tamper seal are intact, and the unit is free of physical damage or corrosion.
  • Annual professional inspection: A licensed fire safety contractor should inspect, tag, and service every unit annually, and hydrostatically test or recharge units on the schedule required by local code.
  • Logbook discipline: Just as a strong pest control programme depends on a logbook auditors can review, a Fire Extinguisher Inspection Log — dates, locations, inspector initials, and any corrective action — is one of the first things a fire marshal or insurance auditor will ask to see.

Common Mistakes Seen on Hospitality Properties

Having audited and trained on this topic across multiple properties, a handful of mistakes show up again and again:

  • Wrong class for the zone: A general-purpose ABC extinguisher placed near the fryer line instead of a Class K unit — technically better than nothing, but not designed for the intensity of a deep-fat fire.
  • Expired or discharged units left in place: A red cylinder on the wall gives false confidence to staff and guests alike if nobody has checked whether it is actually charged.
  • No trained "first responder" per shift: Every shift, in every department, should have at least one person who knows exactly where the nearest extinguisher is and has practiced using one.
  • Extinguishers blocked by storage: Housekeeping trolleys, folded banquet tables, or seasonal decor boxes frequently end up stacked in front of wall-mounted units — invisible during daily operations, catastrophic during an actual fire.
  • Treating fire safety as a once-a-year training slide: Fire safety retention drops sharply within weeks of a single annual briefing. Short, repeated refreshers — a five-minute PASS demonstration at a pre-shift briefing — build far more durable readiness than an annual slideshow.

Building a Genuine Fire Safety Culture

The properties that handle fire incidents well are rarely the ones with the newest equipment — they are the ones where fire safety has become part of daily operating culture rather than an annual compliance exercise. That culture is built through a few consistent habits:

  • Include extinguisher class and PASS technique in every new-hire induction, not just for kitchen and engineering staff, but front office, housekeeping, and F&B service as well.
  • Run unannounced fire drills at varying times of day, including overnight shifts, so response is not just rehearsed for the convenient scenario.
  • Rotate responsibility for the monthly visual extinguisher check across shift leaders, so ownership does not sit with a single person who might be on leave when it matters.
  • Debrief every real incident, however minor — a scorched pan, a tripped breaker, a smoking motor — as a genuine learning opportunity rather than something to quietly forget.
  • Keep the inspection log visible and current, and treat it the same way a strong HACCP programme treats a temperature log: a living document, not paperwork produced only for an audit.
  • Assign a named fire safety champion per shift or per department, whose job includes flagging blocked access points, expired tags, or missing signage the moment they notice them — rather than waiting for the next scheduled inspection to surface the problem.
  • Tie fire safety knowledge into performance conversations for supervisory roles, the same way food safety competency is tied into a chef de partie's review — treating it as a core operational skill rather than an HR compliance checkbox.

None of this requires a large budget. It requires consistency — the same quality that separates a property with an excellent HACCP record from one that merely has the paperwork to look compliant on the day of an audit. Fire safety habits decay quietly when nobody is watching, which is exactly why they need to be built into the rhythm of daily operations rather than revisited once a year.

Manager's Checklist: Can every department head on your property name, without checking, which extinguisher class sits nearest their station — and when it was last inspected? If the answer is not an immediate yes, that is this month's training priority, not next quarter's.

Conclusion

Fire extinguisher classification is a small piece of technical knowledge with an outsized impact on guest and staff safety. A, B, C, D, and K each exist because a single "one size fits all" approach to fire simply does not work — the wrong response can turn a minor, containable incident into a serious emergency within seconds. For hospitality leaders, the job is not just to have the right equipment mounted on the wall in the right place, but to make sure every person on shift — from a first-week trainee to a twenty-year department head — knows exactly which extinguisher to reach for, and precisely how to use it, before they ever need to find out under pressure.

Fire safety, like food safety, is not a certificate you earn once. It is a standard you maintain every single shift. The poster above is designed to be printed and displayed in staff corridors, back-of-house noticeboards, and training rooms as a quick visual reference — but a poster on a wall only does its job if it starts a conversation, and that conversation only matters if it turns into a habit every team member carries with them long after the induction session ends.
