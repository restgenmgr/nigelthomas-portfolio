Brandy vs Cognac & Armagnac

All Cognac and Armagnac are brandy — but not all brandy is Cognac or Armagnac. What every F&B professional should know before recommending one.

By Nigel A. Thomas · Hospitality Executive · 30+ Years in F&B Operations

Ask most guests what the difference is between brandy and Cognac, and you'll get a shrug or, more often, a guess that treats the two words as synonyms. Ask a server the same question and you'll frequently get the same shrug. That gap in understanding costs restaurants and bars real money and real credibility — a guest who is confidently, correctly guided toward the right bottle for their palate and budget becomes a repeat guest; one who is served a vague answer, or worse, an incorrect one, quietly notices.

I've trained bar and service teams across hotels, resorts and cruise lines in India, the Middle East and the United States, and brandy terminology is one of the areas I return to most often, because the category is genuinely confusing even to people who work in it every day. Brandy is not one product. It's an entire family of spirits, and Cognac and Armagnac are just two of its most famous, most tightly regulated members. Understanding the family tree — and the real production differences behind the labels — is what separates a server who can upsell confidently from one who's just repeating a bottle's back-label copy.

What Is Brandy, Really?

At its simplest, brandy is any spirit produced by distilling wine or fermented fruit. That's a broad definition on purpose — it has to cover everything from a French Cognac aged for forty years in a cellar in Charente to a bottle of unaged, mass-produced grape spirit sold at a fraction of the price. The word "brandy" itself likely comes from the Dutch brandewijn, meaning "burnt wine," a reference to the heating process of distillation applied to wine that merchants wanted to transport more efficiently.

Because the category is so broad, almost anything distilled from fermented fruit can technically call itself a brandy, provided local labeling laws allow it. This includes:

  • Grape brandy — the largest category, made from distilled wine (Cognac, Armagnac, and most generic "brandy" fall here)
  • Fruit brandy — distilled from fruits other than grapes: apple (Calvados), cherry (Kirsch), pear (Poire Williams), plum (Slivovitz)
  • Pomace brandy — distilled from the leftover skins, seeds, and stems after winemaking (Grappa in Italy, Marc in France)

This piece focuses primarily on grape brandy, since that's where Cognac and Armagnac sit, and where most of the confusion in a bar or restaurant setting actually happens.

What Makes Cognac Different From Brandy in General

Cognac is, legally and technically, a brandy. But it is a brandy that can only earn the name if it satisfies a long list of production requirements enforced under French Appellation d'Origine Contrôlée (AOC) rules. If any one of these requirements isn't met, the spirit cannot legally be labeled Cognac, no matter how similar it tastes or how it was made.

Geography

Cognac must be produced in the Cognac region of France, centered around the town of the same name in the Charente and Charente-Maritime départements. The region is further divided into six growing zones (crus), with Grande Champagne and Petite Champagne generally considered to produce the most prized eaux-de-vie.

Grape Variety

Cognac is made overwhelmingly from Ugni Blanc grapes — a high-acid, low-alcohol white grape that is poorly suited to drinking as wine but ideal for distillation, since the acidity and lower sugar content produce a cleaner, more aromatic base spirit.

Distillation Method

Cognac must be distilled twice in a copper pot still, known as an alambic charentais. This double-distillation is mandatory and produces a purer, more refined spirit than single distillation, at the cost of stripping away some of the more rustic, characterful notes a single pass would retain.

Aging Requirements

Cognac must age for a minimum of two years in French oak barrels, typically from the Limousin or Tronçais forests, before it can be sold as Cognac at all. The age statements most guests will recognize on a bottle correspond to minimum aging requirements for the youngest eau-de-vie in the blend:

  • VS (Very Special) — minimum 2 years
  • VSOP (Very Superior Old Pale) — minimum 4 years
  • XO (Extra Old) — minimum 10 years

Because Cognac houses blend eaux-de-vie from many different years and casks to achieve a consistent house style, these age statements refer to the youngest component in the blend, not the average age — a detail worth knowing, because many premium blends contain far older spirit than the label technically requires.

The simplest way to remember it: Brandy is the family name. Cognac is one specific, tightly regulated member of that family, defined by where it's made, what grape it's made from, how it's distilled, and how long it's aged.

What Is Armagnac?

If Cognac is the brandy most guests have heard of, Armagnac is the one most have not — and that's a genuine loss, because it's actually the older of the two. Armagnac has been distilled in Gascony, in the far south-west of France, since at least the 15th century, predating Cognac's rise to international fame by roughly two hundred years.

Armagnac diverges from Cognac in several concrete, tasteable ways:

Single Distillation

Where Cognac is double-distilled in a pot still, Armagnac is traditionally distilled just once, in a continuous column still known as an alambic armagnacais. Single distillation retains more of the original wine's character and congeners — the compounds responsible for flavor and aroma — producing a spirit that is generally more rustic, more textured, and less polished than Cognac straight off the still.

Grape Variety Flexibility

Armagnac permits a wider range of grape varieties than Cognac, including Ugni Blanc, Baco, Folle Blanche, and Colombard, giving individual producers more room to express a distinct house style or vintage character.

Vintage Bottling

While Cognac is almost always a blend of multiple vintages designed for house-style consistency, Armagnac is frequently bottled as a millésime — a single-vintage expression from one specific year's harvest. This makes Armagnac closer in spirit (pun intended) to vintage wine or vintage Port than to most Cognac, and it's part of why Armagnac collectors often chase specific birth-year bottlings as gifts.

Scale of Production

Armagnac remains a smaller, more fragmented industry than Cognac, dominated by family estates rather than the large international houses (Hennessy, Rémy Martin, Martell, Courvoisier) that define Cognac's global reach. This has kept Armagnac somewhat under the radar commercially, even among spirits enthusiasts, despite its arguably more distinctive character.

For the floor: If a guest enjoys a robust, characterful whisky and is curious about brandy, Armagnac is often a better recommendation than Cognac — the single distillation and varied grape blend give it a rougher, more layered profile that whisky drinkers tend to find more familiar than Cognac's polished consistency.

Old World Brandy: Beyond France

France dominates the conversation, but it is far from the only country with a serious, centuries-old brandy tradition. Understanding the wider Old World picture helps put Cognac and Armagnac's strictness in context — they're not unusual for the region, they're representative of it.

Spain: Brandy de Jerez

Produced around the sherry-making region of Jerez in Andalusia, Brandy de Jerez is aged using the solera system — the same fractional blending method used for sherry, where younger spirit is progressively blended through a series of barrels containing older spirit, so that no single bottling is ever a single vintage, but rather a continuously refreshed blend carrying character from years or decades of accumulated stock. Brandy de Jerez is typically aged in ex-sherry casks, giving it a noticeably sweeter, richer, more raisined profile than French brandies aged in neutral oak.

Italy: Grappa and Brandy Italiano

Italy produces both a true grape brandy (Brandy Italiano, distilled from wine) and the more internationally famous Grappa, which is technically a pomace brandy, distilled from the leftover skins, seeds, and stems after winemaking rather than from wine itself. Grappa is usually unaged or lightly aged and prized for its intense, sometimes fiery aromatic character rather than the smoothness French brandies pursue.

Germany and Central Europe

Germany produces Weinbrand, a wine-based brandy with its own aging categories, though it rarely reaches international markets with the same prestige as Cognac or Armagnac. Central and Eastern Europe maintain strong fruit brandy traditions — Slivovitz (plum), Rakia (various fruits) — that sit adjacent to grape brandy in the broader category but follow entirely separate production customs.

New World Brandy: A Different Philosophy

Where Old World brandy production is defined by centuries of regional law, tradition, and terroir-driven identity, New World brandy production — broadly, brandy made outside of Europe's historic producing regions — operates under a very different philosophy: fewer inherited rules, and much more room for a producer to experiment.

United States

American brandy production is centered heavily in California, where producers benefit from a climate well suited to the grape varieties used in both Cognac and Armagnac production, without being bound by AOC-style restrictions on grape variety, still type, or aging vessel. This has produced everything from large-scale, accessible brandies to small-batch, pot-still expressions clearly modeled on Cognac technique but finished in American oak or unconventional casks (bourbon barrels, for example) that no French AOC would permit.

South Africa

South African brandy has a genuinely serious pedigree, closely tied to the country's Cape wine industry. Cape Brandy regulations actually require pot-still distillation and minimum oak aging for the "Potstill Brandy" designation, making South Africa one of the few New World producers to have voluntarily adopted regulation resembling the Old World model, while still exploring grape varieties and blends French law wouldn't allow.

Australia and Mexico

Australian brandy production, concentrated in regions like the Riverland, tends toward warmer-climate, riper fruit character. Mexican brandy, produced largely in regions surrounding Jerez-trained expertise brought over historically, has built a substantial domestic market, with some producers exporting internationally, though Mexican brandy is more associated in most markets with everyday mixing than with sipping-grade prestige.

The real distinction isn't quality — it's philosophy. Old World brandy is built on the idea that a region's specific combination of grape, climate, still type and cask defines an unchangeable identity. New World brandy is built on the idea that none of those variables should be fixed, and that experimentation is itself the point. Neither approach is inherently better; they simply optimize for different things.

Serving and Tasting: What Actually Changes the Experience

A guest's experience of any brandy is shaped by more than the liquid itself. A few service fundamentals worth reinforcing with any team pouring brandy:

  • Glassware — a tulip-shaped snifter, held slightly warmed by the hand, concentrates aromatics far better than a straight-sided rocks glass
  • Temperature — brandy should never be served ice-cold; room temperature or very slightly warmed allows the full aromatic range to open up
  • Dilution — a few drops of water can open up a cask-strength or particularly hot young brandy, revealing aromatics that alcohol heat otherwise masks
  • Pacing — brandy, especially older Cognac or Armagnac, is a sipping spirit; guests unfamiliar with the category sometimes treat it like a shot, and a well-trained server can gently redirect that expectation

Common Misconceptions Worth Correcting

"Cognac Is Just Fancy Brandy"

Not quite — Cognac is regulated brandy, not simply brandy with a higher price tag. There are excellent, expensive brandies made outside the Cognac region that are not, and legally cannot be, called Cognac, no matter how they're priced or marketed.

"Older Is Always Better"

Age statements indicate a minimum, not a guarantee of quality, and a well-made VS or VSOP can outperform a poorly blended XO. Age is one input into quality, not a substitute for it.

"Armagnac Is a Cheaper Version of Cognac"

Armagnac is often more affordable than comparable-age Cognac, largely because it lacks the same global marketing infrastructure and brand recognition — not because it's a lesser product. Many spirits professionals consider well-made Armagnac more characterful, not less, than Cognac at a similar price point.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is all Cognac brandy, but not all brandy Cognac?

Yes — this is the single most useful sentence to remember. Cognac must meet every one of the AOC requirements described above to use the name; brandy is the broader category that includes Cognac along with everything else distilled from fermented fruit.

Can Armagnac be made anywhere else in France?

No. Just as Cognac is restricted to the Cognac region, Armagnac can only be produced in the Armagnac region of Gascony, in south-west France, and is governed by its own separate set of AOC rules.

Why does Cognac taste smoother than Armagnac?

Largely because of double distillation. Passing the spirit through the still twice removes more of the heavier congeners responsible for texture and rustic flavor notes, producing a cleaner, more refined result. Armagnac's single distillation preserves more of those compounds, which is exactly what gives it its more textured character.

Is New World brandy inferior to Old World brandy?

Not inherently. Some New World producers, particularly in South Africa and parts of California, are making brandy that rivals mid-tier Cognac and Armagnac in quality, built on genuine craft rather than regulation. What New World brandy generally lacks is the centuries of brand-building and regional prestige that Old World producers benefit from, not necessarily the underlying quality of the spirit in the bottle.

What is the best way to introduce a guest to brandy for the first time?

Start with a VS or VSOP Cognac, or an entry-level Armagnac, served in a proper snifter at room temperature, and describe what to expect before they taste — dried fruit, vanilla, oak, and a warming finish. Guests who are told what they're about to experience tend to enjoy unfamiliar spirits far more than guests who are simply handed a glass with no context.

Food and Cigar Pairing Fundamentals

Brandy's role at the end of a meal is as much about pairing as it is about the pour itself, and a server who can suggest a pairing with confidence adds real value to the check and to the guest's experience.

Cheese

Aged, hard cheeses generally pair best with brandy's dried-fruit and oak notes — a well-aged Comté or Gruyère works beautifully with VSOP Cognac, while a pungent blue cheese can stand up to the more intense, rustic character of Armagnac.

Chocolate and Dessert

Dark chocolate, especially anything above 70% cacao, mirrors brandy's own bitterness and dried-fruit complexity without competing for sweetness. Caramel and toffee-based desserts echo the vanilla and butterscotch notes that oak aging contributes to both Cognac and Armagnac.

Cigars

The classic pairing of Cognac and cigars exists for a structural reason, not just tradition: a medium to full-bodied cigar's smoke intensity needs a spirit with enough weight and sweetness to stand alongside it without being overwhelmed. XO Cognac, with its longer aging and rounder profile, is the traditional choice, though a well-aged Armagnac performs the same role with a more rustic edge that some smokers actually prefer.

A Simple Buyer's Guide by Price Tier

Guests asking "what should I buy" or "what should I order" usually want a recommendation calibrated to what they're willing to spend, not a lecture on production method. A tiered mental model helps:

Entry Level

VS Cognac and younger Armagnac bottlings offer an accessible, food-friendly introduction to the category, typically priced for everyday after-dinner drinking rather than special-occasion sipping. These are excellent both neat and in classic cocktails like a Sidecar or Brandy Alexander, where their brighter, less complex profile doesn't get lost behind other ingredients.

Mid-Tier

VSOP Cognac and mid-aged, single-estate Armagnac sit in the range most guests associate with a "proper" after-dinner brandy — enough complexity to reward slow sipping, without the price tag of the oldest expressions. This tier is also where well-made South African Potstill Brandy and premium Brandy de Jerez become genuinely competitive alternatives worth recommending.

Premium and Collector Tier

XO Cognac, vintage-dated Armagnac, and rare single-cask bottlings belong here — purchases driven as much by occasion, gifting, and collecting as by everyday drinking. A guest's own birth-year Armagnac, when available, is one of the more memorable gift recommendations a knowledgeable server can make, precisely because vintage bottling is something Cognac generally cannot offer in the same way.

Brandy in Classic Cocktails

Beyond the snifter, brandy has a long history behind the bar, and it's worth knowing which style suits which drink. A Sidecar — Cognac, orange liqueur, lemon juice — wants a VS or young VSOP with enough brightness to cut through the citrus without being wasted on subtlety a shaken drink will mask anyway. A Brandy Alexander, built on cream and crème de cacao, similarly doesn't need an expensive pour, since the dairy dominates the palate. A Sazerac, by contrast, traditionally built on Cognac before rye whiskey became the modern standard, rewards a slightly more characterful spirit, since the absinthe rinse and bitters are designed to complement complexity rather than bury it. Knowing not to pour a guest's treasured XO into a shaken cocktail — and knowing which bottle on the well is actually meant for exactly that purpose — is a small distinction that separates a knowledgeable bar team from one that's simply following a recipe card.

The Bottom Line

Brandy is a family, not a single product, and Cognac and Armagnac are its two most famous, most rigorously defined members — each shaped by a specific region, grape, distillation method, and aging tradition that cannot legally be replicated anywhere else. Old World producers built their reputations on strict, inherited rules; New World producers are building theirs on the freedom to break from them. Neither story is finished, and a guest curious enough to ask the difference deserves a server who actually knows it.
