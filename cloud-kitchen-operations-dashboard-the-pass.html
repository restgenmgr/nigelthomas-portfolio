Cloud Kitchen Operations Dashboard: The Brief, the Prompt and a Live Prototype

By Nigel A. Thomas  |  21 September 2026  |  Hospitality technology  |  9 min read, plus a working demo

Ask any multi-outlet operator how their morning starts and the answer is usually the same: messages to managers, waiting for checklist photos, chasing last night's numbers, and only then the real work of fixing problems. This article puts a wish list for a better way next to something you can click: a working prototype called The Pass, built directly from that brief.

It is written for owners, area managers and outlet managers in cloud kitchens and multi-outlet F&B, and for anyone curious about how far a plain-English brief can now go toward a real operations tool. You will find the brief exactly as written, the working demo, how each request was answered, the rules behind every alert, and an honest list of what a production version would still need.

The brief: what the operator asked for

This is the brief I gave the AI, word for word. I pasted it in as-is and asked one question: “Can you build this for me?”

🚨 Looking for a Hospitality-Tech Solution | AI + Operations

I’m currently looking for a hospitality industry-specific application/dashboard that can make day-to-day cloud kitchen operations more automated, trackable and less dependent on manual follow-ups.

The objective is simple:

  • 👉 Reduce the daily load of chasing people for updates
  • 👉 Automate operational follow-ups
  • 👉 Get real-time visibility across outlets
  • 👉 Identify pending tasks and deviations automatically
  • 👉 Use AI to analyse operational data and highlight what needs attention
  • 👉 Make managers focus on solving problems rather than collecting updates

Ideally, the solution should help manage areas such as:

  • Daily outlet operations
  • Task & checklist management
  • Staff accountability
  • Sales & performance tracking
  • Inventory / wastage monitoring
  • Online ordering & platform performance
  • Complaints & issue tracking
  • Maintenance & escalation
  • Compliance & audits
  • Automated reminders & follow-ups
  • AI-generated MIS / daily summaries
  • Exception-based alerts — “What needs my attention today?”

I’m not looking for another generic task-management software. I’m specifically looking for something built around the real operational challenges of the hospitality/F&B industry.

Why a generic task app is not enough

The last line of the brief is the important one. A general task manager treats every task the same. Food and beverage operations do not work that way, for five reasons:

  • Time is the deadline. A temperature log due at 10:00 is a food-safety record, not a to-do. A missed prep sign-off costs you at the lunch peak.
  • Performance is measured by the hour. Ending the day 20% short of target is too late to act. What matters is whether you are on pace at 2 pm.
  • Delivery platforms are a second front door. An outlet can go offline on an aggregator, or slip on rating and acceptance rate, without anyone in the kitchen noticing.
  • Stock is perishable. The useful question is not “are we below par?” but “will we last until close at today’s demand?”
  • Compliance runs on a calendar. Licences, pest control and hygiene audits all expire on fixed dates, and missing one is expensive.

Try The Pass: the live demo

The Pass is the prototype built from the brief. It runs four sample kitchens in Mumbai with made-up data, so you can click freely. Nothing you do is saved to a server.

On a phone, use Full screen for the best view.

Five things to try in the demo

  1. Read the Attention screen first. Every problem hangs on the rail as a chit, most urgent first. This is the answer to “What needs my attention today?”
  2. Press “Move on 30 min” three times. Watch new exceptions appear as tasks go overdue, and watch the follow-up log on the right fill with reminders and escalations.
  3. Open Checklists and tick off an overdue task. Go back to Attention and its chit has gone. Nobody had to close it manually.
  4. Log a complaint or a wastage entry. The complaint starts an SLA clock. The wastage entry changes the outlet’s share of sales and can trigger an alert.
  5. Open Daily MIS and copy it. The report writes itself from the live figures, formatted for WhatsApp.

The rules behind the alerts

“Exception-based” only works if the definition of an exception is explicit. Here are the sample thresholds used in the prototype. Every operator should replace them with their own standards.

AreaWhen it is flagged
ChecklistsPast its due time. Food-safety records such as temperature logs are urgent immediately. Other tasks become urgent after 3 hours.
SalesBelow 92% of the expected pace for the time of day to watch, below 80% urgent.
Delivery platformsOffline; rating below 4.2 (urgent below 4.0); acceptance below 92% (urgent below 85%); prep time above 22 minutes against a 20 minute target (urgent above 26); cancellations above 5% (urgent above 8%).
StockLess than 1.25 times what is still needed until close, urgent if it will run short. Also flagged if the current batch expires today.
WastageAbove 2.5% of sales to watch, above 5% urgent.
Complaints60 minute response target; 30 minutes for hygiene, which is always urgent. Three of the same type at one outlet in a day are grouped as a pattern.
MaintenanceResponse targets of 2 hours for P1, 8 hours for P2 and 48 hours for P3. A breach goes to the area manager, double the target goes to the ops head.
Licences and auditsLicences flag at 30 days and turn urgent at 14. Pest-control service and hygiene audits flag when overdue.

The follow-up ladder

The brief’s first goal was to reduce the daily load of chasing people. The Pass replaces the chase with a fixed ladder. When something goes overdue, the owner gets a reminder at the due time. If there is still no update after 30 minutes (15 for food-safety records), the outlet manager is told. After 60 minutes (30 for food-safety records) it goes to the area manager. Managers hear about the exception, not the reminder, and only if it survives the first two steps.

What this prototype is, and what production would still need

A prototype proves the idea and the screens. It is not yet a system you should run a business on. To be clear about where the line is:

  • The data is sample data. Four fictional kitchens, invented staff and figures. Nothing is connected to a real business.
  • Reminders are simulated. The log shows what would be sent. No messages leave the page.
  • The alert thresholds are placeholders. Your standards for prep time, wastage and acceptance rate will differ.

A production version would need:

  • Staff logins and roles, so a store keeper, kitchen manager and area manager each see the right screens.
  • A shared database, so everyone works from one live picture instead of one browser.
  • Real messaging, for example through the WhatsApp Business platform, for reminders and escalations.
  • Data feeds from your POS, inventory system and delivery aggregators. Access to aggregator data is controlled by the platforms, so this usually means partner APIs or an integration provider, and it is normally the slowest part of any build.
  • Photo or sensor evidence for safety-critical checks, so a temperature log is proof and not just a tick.
  • Careful handling of staff and customer data, including who can see what.
The AI read-out that can rewrite the MIS and answer questions about the day’s figures is available in the version that runs inside Claude. This page runs it without the AI step, and the rule-based MIS and alerts work in full.

How to brief an AI for a tool like this

The brief above worked well because of how it was written. If you want to try the same approach for your own operation, these habits made the difference:

  1. Start with the outcome, not the features. “Reduce the daily load of chasing people” tells the builder what success looks like.
  2. List the areas you want covered. A checklist of twelve modules leaves nothing to guess.
  3. Say what you do not want. “Not another generic task manager” steered every design decision toward F&B.
  4. Ask for a prototype on sample data first. You learn in minutes whether the idea holds, before spending on integrations.
  5. Then refine one screen at a time, using your own thresholds, outlet names and checklists.
  6. Check the rules against your operation. AI builds what you describe. Whether a 2.5% wastage ceiling or a 60 minute complaint response is right for you is your call.

Where to go from here

If you run cloud kitchens or multi-outlet F&B, the useful exercise is to write your own version of the brief: what you chase people for every day, what you want flagged automatically, and what you never want to find out too late. That list is the specification. Everything else is engineering.
