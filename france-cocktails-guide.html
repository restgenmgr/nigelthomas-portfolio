France Cocktails: 6 Classic French Cocktail Recipes

France gave the world wine, Champagne, Cognac and a whole culture of drinking well. It also gave the bar trade some of its most elegant cocktails. Walk into any serious cocktail bar from Mumbai to Manhattan and you will find at least one of them on the menu: a French 75 sparkling in a flute, a Sidecar glowing in a coupe, a Boulevardier slowly dissolving its ice. These drinks have lasted for a century because they are balanced, simple to make and easy to sell.

This guide covers six of them in one place: the French 75, Kir Royale, Sidecar, Boulevardier, French Martini and Champs-Élysées. For each one you get the exact recipe from our poster, the story behind it, the method I teach bar teams, the glass and garnish, common mistakes and ideas for service. At the bottom of the page you can open the infographic and download the poster for your bar, your training room or your own notes.

The six recipes at a glance
  • French 75: 30 ml Gin, 15 ml Lemon juice, 15 ml Simple syrup, top with Champagne
  • Kir Royale: 15 ml Crème de Cassis, top with Champagne
  • Sidecar: 45 ml Cognac, 20 ml Triple sec, 20 ml Lemon juice, orange twist or sugar rim
  • Boulevardier: 45 ml Bourbon, 30 ml Campari, 30 ml Sweet vermouth, orange twist
  • French Martini: 45 ml Vodka, 15 ml Chambord (raspberry liqueur), 45 ml Pineapple juice
  • Champs-Élysées: 45 ml Cognac, 15 ml Green Chartreuse, 15 ml Lemon juice, 15 ml Simple syrup, 1 dash Angostura bitters

Why French Cocktails Matter to a Bar Programme

When people say “French cocktail,” they usually mean one of two things: a drink built on a French ingredient such as Cognac, Champagne, Chartreuse or crème de cassis, or a drink created in Paris during the golden age of the American bar abroad, roughly the 1910s to the 1930s. Paris in those years was full of writers, artists and travellers, and bars like Harry’s New York Bar and the bar of the Ritz became meeting points where recipes were swapped, published and carried home. Many of the cocktails in this guide come from that world. A few, like the French Martini, are far more recent but follow the same French logic of fruit, liqueur and elegance.

For a hotel or restaurant bar, French cocktails solve several problems at once. They use few ingredients, so training is quick. They rely on products with strong brand recognition, which helps guests understand the menu. They suit the sparkling-wine moment at the start of an evening, the digestif moment at the end, and everything in between. And because several of them use only 15 to 45 ml of premium spirit, you can control cost per serve more easily than with large tall drinks.

1. French 75

Recipe

30 ml Gin, 15 ml Lemon juice, 15 ml Simple syrup, topped with Champagne. Serve in a chilled flute with a long lemon twist.

The story

The French 75 takes its name from the French 75 mm field gun of the First World War, a fast and accurate piece of artillery. The nickname was meant to suggest a drink with a real kick under a smooth surface. Gin, lemon and sugar make a classic sour, and Champagne lifts it into something celebratory. The drink was popular in the Paris and New York bar scenes of the 1920s and it has never really gone away. Some old recipes use Cognac instead of gin, and you will still see both versions today, but the gin version is the one most bars serve.

Method

Chill the flute with ice and water or keep it in the freezer. Add the gin, lemon juice and syrup to a shaker with ice and shake hard for about ten seconds until the outside of the shaker is frosted. Discard the ice from the flute and strain the mix in. Slowly top with cold Champagne, tilting the glass slightly as you pour so that the bubbles stay lively. Express a lemon twist over the top and drop it in.

Service notes

  • Always use freshly squeezed lemon juice. Bottled juice flattens the drink.
  • Do not shake with the Champagne. The carbonation will escape and the shaker can pop open.
  • If Champagne is too costly for volume service, a good dry sparkling wine made by the traditional method is a sensible house alternative. Label it honestly on the menu.
  • Use a London Dry style gin so that the juniper is clearly present behind the lemon.

2. Kir Royale

Recipe

15 ml Crème de Cassis, topped with Champagne (Champagne optional, as shown on the poster, when you serve the simpler Kir). Serve in a flute.

The story

The Kir is named after Canon Félix Kir, a priest, Resistance figure and long-serving mayor of Dijon in Burgundy. He promoted the local blackcurrant liqueur, crème de cassis, by serving it with the region’s white wine, usually Aligoté. That drink became the Kir. When the white wine is replaced by Champagne or another sparkling wine, it becomes the Kir Royale. The deep ruby colour under a pale froth makes it one of the most photogenic drinks in any bar, and the sweetness of the cassis softens the acidity of the wine, which makes it a friendly aperitif for guests who find dry sparkling wine too sharp.

Method

Pour 15 ml of crème de cassis into the bottom of a chilled flute. Tilt the glass and top slowly with cold Champagne. Do not stir with a spoon, because the bubbles will vanish. The natural movement of the pour is enough to blend the two layers. No garnish is needed, although a single blackberry or a thin lemon twist is acceptable on a special menu.

Service notes

  • Pour the liqueur first. It is heavier than the wine and mixes by itself as the Champagne goes in.
  • Quality of cassis matters more than most staff expect. A good Dijon cassis tastes of real blackcurrant. A cheap one tastes of sugar.
  • Keep the bottle of cassis refrigerated after opening to protect the colour and freshness.
  • This is an excellent welcome drink for weddings and conferences because it is quick to build in volume.

3. Sidecar

Recipe

45 ml Cognac, 20 ml Triple sec, 20 ml Lemon juice. Garnish with an orange twist, or rim the glass with sugar. Serve straight up in a chilled coupe.

The story

The Sidecar is a true sour: a base spirit, a citrus and a sweetener, here supplied by the orange liqueur. It appeared around the end of the First World War and several bars claim it, among them the Ritz in Paris and Buck’s Club in London. Nobody can prove who was first, and the debate is part of the drink’s charm. The name is often linked to a motorcycle sidecar in which a regular guest supposedly arrived at the bar. What matters for bartenders is that the drink is an excellent test of skill. With only three ingredients there is nowhere to hide a poorly balanced shake.

Method

Chill a coupe. Add Cognac, triple sec and fresh lemon juice to a shaker filled with good, hard ice. Shake for about twelve seconds. Strain into the chilled coupe through a fine strainer to catch ice chips. If using a sugar rim, moisten only the outer edge of the glass with a lemon wedge, dip it lightly into fine sugar and let it set before you pour. Finish with an orange twist expressed over the surface.

Service notes

  • Offer the sugar rim as a choice. Some guests love the contrast of sweet rim and sharp drink, others find it too sweet.
  • A VS or VSOP Cognac is correct here. Save XO for sipping.
  • Taste the mix before service if your triple sec is sweeter than usual. A small change in lemon can rescue the balance.
  • Keep the rim on the outside only so that the sugar does not fall into the drink.

4. Boulevardier

Recipe

45 ml Bourbon, 30 ml Campari, 30 ml Sweet vermouth. Stir with ice, strain over a large ice cube in a rocks glass and garnish with an orange twist.

The story

The Boulevardier is usually described as the whiskey cousin of the Negroni, with bourbon replacing gin. The name is tied to Erskine Gwynne, an American writer who published a magazine called Boulevardier in Paris in the late 1920s. Harry MacElhone of Harry’s New York Bar recorded the drink in his book of that period. The word itself means a man about town, someone who strolls the boulevards, and that image fits the drink perfectly: bitter, warm, confident and unhurried. It has returned strongly in the modern cocktail revival and now appears on bar lists across the world.

Method

Add bourbon, Campari and sweet vermouth to a mixing glass filled with ice. Stir for about 25 to 30 seconds until the glass is cold and the liquid has gained a little water. Strain into a rocks glass over one large cube. Cut a wide strip of orange peel, squeeze it over the glass so the oils land on the surface, rub the rim, and place the twist in the drink.

Service notes

  • Stir, never shake. This is a spirit-only drink and shaking would cloud it and over-dilute it.
  • Choose a bourbon with enough proof and spice to stand up to Campari. A soft, weak bourbon disappears.
  • Store opened vermouth in the refrigerator and use it within a few weeks. Oxidised vermouth is the most common reason a Boulevardier tastes flat.
  • If a guest finds it too bitter, a short bar-spoon of extra vermouth is a quick and polite adjustment.

5. French Martini

Recipe

45 ml Vodka, 15 ml Chambord (raspberry liqueur), 45 ml Pineapple juice. Shake hard and strain into a chilled martini glass, garnished with a raspberry.

The story

The French Martini is the youngest drink in this guide. It rose to popularity in the 1980s and 1990s, in the era of fruit-led “martinis” served in a V-shaped glass. The French element is Chambord, a black raspberry liqueur from the Loire Valley. The pineapple juice brings tropical sweetness and, when the drink is shaken hard, a soft foam on top. Purists point out that it has little in common with a true martini apart from the glass, but guests love it and it is among the easiest cocktails to teach a new bartender, which makes it valuable on a busy night.

Method

Chill a martini glass. Add vodka, Chambord and pineapple juice to a shaker with plenty of ice. Shake vigorously for 12 to 15 seconds. The pineapple juice needs a strong shake to create its signature foam. Double strain into the glass and float a fresh raspberry on the foam.

Service notes

  • Use fresh or good-quality chilled pineapple juice. Canned juice with added sugar will make the drink cloying.
  • The foam is part of the presentation. Pour with confidence and do not wait before serving, because it settles within a few minutes.
  • A fresh raspberry is the correct garnish. Use a frozen one only if you can place it just before service.
  • This drink travels well on a party menu because it appeals to guests who are new to cocktails.

6. Champs-Élysées

Recipe

45 ml Cognac, 15 ml Green Chartreuse, 15 ml Lemon juice, 15 ml Simple syrup, 1 dash Angostura bitters. Shake, strain into a chilled coupe and garnish with a lemon or orange twist.

The story

The Champs-Élysées is named for the famous avenue in Paris and was published in cocktail books of the early twentieth century. It is a close relative of the Sidecar, but the orange liqueur is swapped for Green Chartreuse, a herbal liqueur made by Carthusian monks from a closely guarded blend of many plants. The result is a drink of real depth: grape and oak from the Cognac, herbs and spice from the Chartreuse, and a clean lemon finish. A single dash of Angostura bitters ties these flavours together. It is the most “grown-up” drink in this guide, and a rewarding one for guests who enjoy complexity.

Method

Chill a coupe. Add Cognac, Green Chartreuse, lemon juice, syrup and one dash of Angostura bitters to a shaker with ice. Shake for about twelve seconds and strain into the coupe through a fine strainer. Express a twist over the surface and place it on the rim or in the drink.

Service notes

  • Measure Chartreuse carefully. At 15 ml it is a supporting voice, and a heavy hand will take over the drink.
  • Chartreuse can be expensive and hard to source in some markets. Check availability and local excise rules before you commit it to a printed menu.
  • Use only one dash of bitters. More will push the drink toward bitterness.
  • Pair with dark chocolate, roasted nuts or a soft cheese plate.

The Essential French Cocktail Toolkit

You do not need a large inventory to serve all six of these drinks. The core list is short: a London Dry style gin, a VS or VSOP Cognac, a bourbon with good proof, a clean vodka, triple sec, Campari, sweet vermouth, crème de cassis, Chambord, Green Chartreuse, Angostura bitters and a bottle of Champagne or good traditional-method sparkling wine. Add fresh lemons, oranges, raspberries and pineapple juice, plus a bottle of simple syrup, and you are ready.

Simple syrup is made from equal parts sugar and water by weight. Dissolve the sugar in warm water, cool it, bottle it and keep it refrigerated. It will stay fresh for about two weeks, or longer with a small measure of neutral spirit added. Label every bottle with the date.

The glassware is just as important as the ingredients. You need champagne flutes, coupes, a rocks glass and a martini glass. The three drinks served straight up should all be served in chilled glasses. Keep a few coupes in the freezer during service and rotate them regularly.

For equipment, a Boston shaker, a Hawthorne strainer and a fine tea strainer for double straining cover almost every need. Add a mixing glass and long bar spoon for the Boulevardier, a jigger marked in 15, 30 and 45 ml for accurate pouring, and a sharp peeler for citrus twists.

Technique: Shake, Stir or Build?

A reliable rule is that drinks containing citrus juice, juice or cream are shaken, and drinks made only of spirits, liqueurs and fortified wine are stirred. The French 75, Sidecar, French Martini and Champs-Élysées all contain juice, so they are shaken. The Boulevardier contains only spirits and vermouth, so it is stirred. The Kir Royale is built directly in the glass because its two ingredients mix on their own.

Shaking chills, dilutes and aerates. Stirring chills and dilutes more gently and keeps the drink silky and clear. Building preserves carbonation. Teach the reason for each method, not only the rule, and bartenders will remember it and apply it to new recipes.

Dilution is an ingredient. A drink that is shaken or stirred for too short a time tastes hot and sharp, while one that is overworked tastes weak. Train your team to taste every new batch of ice and every new recipe, and to adjust shaking time to suit their equipment.

Menu Planning and Costing

A French cocktail list works best as a short, deliberate section rather than as a long list. Six drinks cover the spectrum: two sparkling aperitifs (French 75, Kir Royale), two sours (Sidecar, Champs-Élysées), one stirred bitter drink (Boulevardier) and one fruit-led crowd pleaser (French Martini).

To cost a drink, add the cost of each pour. Take the price of the bottle, divide by the number of millilitres in it and multiply by the measure used. Include fresh juice, syrup, garnish and a small allowance for spillage and dilution. Compare the total to your selling price and aim for the beverage cost percentage your property targets. The Sidecar and Champs-Élysées carry the highest ingredient cost because of Cognac and Chartreuse, while the French Martini and Kir Royale usually deliver the best margin, so balance the list and price each drink on its own merits.

Seasonal and local touches help. A Kir Royale can be offered with a house-made blackberry garnish. A French 75 can carry a locally grown herb or a citrus twist from a regional fruit. Small details like these add character without adding much cost.

Food Pairing Ideas

  • French 75: smoked salmon canapés, oysters, fried appetisers and salted nuts.
  • Kir Royale: gougères, light cheese puffs, strawberries and canapés with cream cheese.
  • Sidecar: roast chicken, duck, citrus tarts and dried fruit.
  • Boulevardier: charcuterie, aged cheddar, grilled red meat and dark chocolate.
  • French Martini: raspberry desserts, light pastries and fresh fruit platters.
  • Champs-Élysées: washed-rind cheeses, truffle bites and roasted almonds.

Training Your Team: A Simple Plan

Use the poster as your daily training aid. In the first session, give each trainee the six recipes and let them measure each ingredient on their own. In the second session, demonstrate the three techniques: shaking, stirring and building. In the third session, have the trainees make every drink, then taste and compare them against a reference version prepared by a senior bartender. In the fourth session, practise garnishes, glassware and the guest conversation: how to describe each drink in one sentence and how to suggest alternatives.

Examples of one-sentence descriptions: the French 75 is a crisp gin sour lifted with Champagne; the Kir Royale is Champagne softened with blackcurrant; the Sidecar is a Cognac sour with orange; the Boulevardier is a bittersweet whiskey classic; the French Martini is a fruity, silky raspberry and pineapple drink; and the Champs-Élysées is a herbal, elegant Cognac sour. Staff who can say this clearly sell more drinks and answer questions with confidence.

Finish training with a short practical test. Ask each trainee to make three random drinks from the list within a set time, with correct measures, correct glass and correct garnish. Record the results and repeat the exercise every month.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using bottled lemon juice instead of fresh juice.
  • Pouring by eye rather than with a jigger, which makes drinks inconsistent from one shift to the next.
  • Serving straight-up drinks in warm glasses.
  • Shaking sparkling wine or stirring a flute drink and losing the bubbles.
  • Letting vermouth, crème de cassis and juices stand open at room temperature.
  • Over-garnishing. French drinks are meant to look refined and uncluttered.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need real Champagne for a French 75 or Kir Royale?

Real Champagne is the classic choice and gives the best flavour. If cost is a concern, use a good dry sparkling wine made by the traditional method and describe it honestly on the menu.

Can I make the Sidecar without triple sec?

Triple sec supplies the orange sweetness and is part of the recipe. Another good orange liqueur can be used, but adjust the lemon juice if it is sweeter.

What is the difference between a Boulevardier and a Negroni?

A Negroni uses gin, Campari and sweet vermouth. A Boulevardier replaces the gin with bourbon, which makes the drink warmer and rounder.

Why is my French Martini not foamy?

Usually the shake was too short or the pineapple juice was flat. Shake harder with plenty of ice and use fresh, cold juice.

Can I batch these drinks for a party?

The Boulevardier batches very well because it is stirred. The sours can be pre-mixed without ice and chilled, then shaken to order. Never pre-mix drinks containing sparkling wine.

Final Thoughts

French cocktails reward discipline. They use a few excellent ingredients, correct measures and careful technique, and they ask the bartender to respect the drink rather than decorate it. Learn these six and you will have a repertoire that works in a luxury hotel, a neighbourhood bistro, a banquet hall or your own home bar.
