Types of Cheese Used in Hotels

Quality Ingredients. Better Flavor. Memorable Guest Experience. — A professional breakdown of the 15 cheeses every hospitality team should recognize, store correctly, and serve with confidence.

By Nigel A. Thomas
Hospitality Executive & Corporate Trainer

Good Cheese, Great Experience

Cheese shows up across almost every part of a hotel operation — breakfast buffets, à la carte dishes, banquets and catering, room service, and the dessert trolley. Guests notice the difference between a cheese plate that was thrown together and one that was chosen, stored and presented with real knowledge behind it.

Knowing your cheeses isn't just a culinary nicety. It's what allows a chef to build a balanced cheese board, a server to answer a guest's question with confidence, and a kitchen team to store and rotate stock correctly so nothing is wasted or served past its best.

What You Will Learn

  • The 15 cheeses most commonly used in hotel kitchens.
  • Their texture, flavor profile and where each is typically used.
  • Correct storage temperatures and hygiene practices.
  • How to present cheese for a better guest experience.
  • Quick answers to common questions from trainees and new hires.

Why Cheese Knowledge Matters in Hospitality

Cheese appears at breakfast, on the à la carte menu, on the banquet table, and on the dessert cart — often on the same day, in completely different forms. A team that can tell a guest why a cheese board pairs Brie with grapes, or why Feta belongs in a Greek salad rather than a lasagna, is a team that has been properly trained.

Hospitality Insight

Cheese adds not just flavor, but also enhances the texture, appearance and overall guest experience. Store it correctly, rotate it on a strict first-in, first-out basis, and present it well — the details are what guests remember.

1. Mozzarella

Soft, mild and creamy, with a gentle milky flavor. Fresh mozzarella melts beautifully, which is exactly why it's a kitchen workhorse across so many menus.

Used In

Pizza, pasta, lasagna, sandwiches, salads.

Service Tip

Fresh mozzarella is best served at room temperature to bring out its flavor — take it out of refrigeration shortly before plating rather than serving it cold.

2. Cheddar

Firm, rich and slightly sharp, Cheddar ranges from mild to extra-mature depending on how long it's aged. It's one of the most versatile cheeses in any kitchen.

Used In

Burgers, sandwiches, omelets, breakfast buffet.

Service Tip

For a breakfast buffet, offer both a mild and a mature Cheddar side by side — guests notice the choice, and it costs very little extra to provide.

3. Parmesan

Hard, nutty and full of flavor, Parmesan is a finishing cheese as much as a cooking one — a little goes a long way.

Used In

Pasta, Caesar salad, soups, risotto.

Service Tip

Grate Parmesan to order wherever possible. Pre-grated cheese loses aroma quickly and never has the same impact tableside as a fresh shaving.

4. Brie

Soft, creamy and buttery, with an edible bloomy rind. Brie is a cheese board staple that also works beautifully baked.

Used In

Cheese platters, appetizers, fine dining.

Service Tip

Bring Brie to room temperature for around 30–45 minutes before serving — it needs to soften to release its full flavor and texture.

5. Camembert

Soft, earthy and mild in its youth, growing stronger and more pungent as it ripens. Closely related to Brie but typically smaller and more intense.

Used In

Cheese boards, baked cheese dishes.

Service Tip

Baked Camembert is a popular shared starter — score the rind lightly before baking so it opens evenly and stays presentable at the table.

6. Gouda

Semi-hard, creamy and slightly sweet. Young Gouda is mild and smooth; aged Gouda develops a firmer texture and deeper, caramel-like notes.

Used In

Sandwiches, buffets, cheese platters.

Service Tip

Cube or slice Gouda evenly for buffet platters — consistent portion size keeps presentation neat as guests serve themselves.

9. Feta

Crumbly, salty and tangy, brined for preservation and flavor. A defining ingredient of Mediterranean and Greek-inspired menus.

Used In

Greek salads, Mediterranean dishes.

Service Tip

Keep Feta in its brine until just before use — it dries out and loses texture quickly once exposed to air.

10. Ricotta

Soft, mild and granular, made from whey rather than curds. Its light texture makes it a favorite in both savory and sweet Italian dishes.

Used In

Lasagna, ravioli, cannoli, desserts.

Service Tip

Drain Ricotta well before using it in fillings — excess moisture is the most common cause of a soggy lasagna or split cannoli filling.

11. Mascarpone

Rich, creamy and indulgent, with a high fat content and almost no tang. Closer to a dessert component than a table cheese.

Used In

Tiramisu, cheesecakes, mousses.

Service Tip

Never over-whip Mascarpone — its high fat content means it can split and turn grainy far more easily than cream.

12. Swiss (Emmental)

Mild, nutty and smooth, instantly recognizable by its characteristic holes. A reliable, crowd-pleasing melting cheese.

Used In

Sandwiches, gratins, burgers.

Service Tip

Slice Emmental thinly for sandwiches — its firm texture melts more evenly and folds better over a hot filling when cut thin.

13. Provolone

Smooth, mild and slightly smoky, especially in its aged form. A classic melting cheese for hot sandwiches.

Used In

Paninis, grilled sandwiches.

Service Tip

For paninis, use Provolone at room temperature before grilling — it melts more evenly and avoids the greasy separation that cold cheese can cause under high heat.

14. Monterey Jack

Mild, creamy and melts extremely well, making it a natural fit for Mexican-inspired and comfort-food menus.

Used In

Mexican dishes, nachos, burgers.

Service Tip

Shred Monterey Jack fresh for nachos rather than using pre-shredded cheese — it melts more smoothly and avoids the powdery anti-caking coating found on packaged shreds.

Hospitality Tip: The Right Cheese, The Right Dish

Choosing the right cheese for the right dish isn't guesswork — it's knowledge that shows up on the plate. The right cheese, the right dish, the right impression.

Storage & Handling Tips for Hoteliers

  • Store cheese at the right temperature — most cheeses hold best between 2°C and 5°C.
  • Use clean utensils and boards to maintain hygiene and prevent cross-contamination between cheeses.
  • Always check expiry dates and rotate stock on a strict FIFO (First In, First Out) basis.
  • Wrap cut cheese properly to prevent it drying out or absorbing odors from other items in the fridge.
  • Present cheese beautifully on platters with fruits and accompaniments to lift the guest experience.
  • Label cheese with receipt and use-by dates so kitchen and service staff can track freshness at a glance.

Hotel Manager's Note

Cheese is used across breakfast buffets, à la carte dishes, banquets and catering, room service, and desserts and bakeries. A single storage mistake can affect every one of those touchpoints in the same day — which is exactly why correct handling matters as much as correct sourcing.

Interesting Cheese Facts

  • Parmesan is traditionally aged for a minimum of 12 months, and often much longer.
  • The holes in Swiss (Emmental) cheese are formed by carbon dioxide released during fermentation.
  • Mascarpone isn't technically a cheese in the traditional sense — it's made by curdling cream rather than milk.
  • Brie and Camembert are both bloomy-rind cheeses, but Camembert is typically smaller and more intensely flavored.
  • Feta must be aged in brine for at least a few weeks to develop its characteristic tang.
  • Goat cheese is naturally lower in lactose than most cow's-milk cheeses, which is why some lactose-sensitive guests tolerate it better.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What temperature should cheese be stored at in a hotel kitchen?

Most cheeses hold best between 2°C and 5°C. Always check the supplier's specific guidance, as softer, fresher cheeses can be more temperature-sensitive than hard, aged ones.

2. Which cheese is best for a breakfast buffet?

Cheddar and Gouda are both reliable buffet choices — they hold their texture well at room temperature for short service periods and appeal to a broad range of guests.

3. What's the difference between Brie and Camembert?

Both are soft, bloomy-rind cheeses, but Camembert is typically smaller, denser and more strongly flavored, while Brie tends to be milder and buttery.

4. Which cheeses melt best for hot sandwiches and paninis?

Provolone, Monterey Jack, Swiss (Emmental) and Mozzarella all melt smoothly and are the standard choices for hot sandwich and panini service.

5. Which cheese should never be substituted in a Greek salad?

Feta. Its brined, crumbly, salty character is central to the dish and cannot be replicated by a substitute without changing the dish entirely.

6. How should a cheese board be presented for fine dining?

Combine a range of textures and intensities — for example Brie, Blue Cheese and an aged Cheddar or Gouda — alongside fruits, nuts and accompaniments, and always take soft cheeses out of refrigeration ahead of service so they can soften properly.

Conclusion

From the mozzarella on a pizza to the Mascarpone in a tiramisu, cheese touches nearly every part of a hotel's food and beverage operation. Once a hospitality professional understands the texture, flavor and correct use of each of these 15 cheeses, choosing, storing and presenting them becomes second nature.

Good cheese, great experience — it happens by choice: the choice to source quality ingredients, store them correctly, and present them with care every single time.

Professional Takeaway

Cheese knowledge is guest-facing knowledge. A team that can confidently identify, store and serve all 15 of these cheeses delivers a noticeably better guest experience — one plate at a time.
