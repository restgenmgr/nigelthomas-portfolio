Fish Cuts Poster: A Visual Guide to Anatomy & Culinary Use

By Nigel Thomas Visual Design & Information Architecture Project Portfolio

Walk into any professional kitchen, and you will notice something immediately: the cooks do not guess. They know exactly which part of the fish they are holding, what it is called, and precisely how it should be treated. The fillet that gets a hard sear on the skin side is not the same fillet that gets sliced thin for crudo. The belly that melts under a blowtorch is not the tail that gets simmered into stock. This knowledge is not intuitive—it is learned, practiced, and reinforced daily.

For the home cook, however, this knowledge is often fragmented. A recipe calls for "cod loin," and the fishmonger hands over a thick, uniform block. Another recipe asks for "halibut cheek," and the search begins. The gap between what a recipe demands and what a home cook can confidently identify is where confusion lives. It is also where this project began.

The Problem With "Just Buy a Fillet"

Most supermarket fish counters operate on a model of convenience. Fillets are pre-cut, skinned, and trimmed into anonymous rectangular portions. This is efficient, but it strips away the geography of the animal. A cook who only ever sees a skinless, boneless square of white fish never learns that the same fish yields a firm, meaty collar perfect for grilling, a delicate belly suited for gentle poaching, and a bony frame that makes the richest stock. The information exists, but it is invisible.

I wanted to make that information visible again. Not in a dense textbook, not in a wall of text, but in a single visual reference that could answer three questions at once: Where is this cut? What is it called? What should I do with it?

The answer was a poster. But not a decorative one. An informational one.

Designing for Clarity, Not Nostalgia

The vintage educational chart style is having a moment. Warm paper tones, fine line work, and serif typography evoke a sense of authority and craftsmanship. It is a seductive aesthetic. But it is also a trap. Many contemporary "vintage" posters prioritize mood over information. Labels are decorative rather than functional. The anatomy is suggestive rather than accurate. The viewer admires the poster but learns nothing from it.

I set a strict rule for this project: every visual decision must serve the information. The paper texture exists to separate the background from the illustration, not to simulate age. The line weight is calibrated for print legibility at A3 and A2 sizes, not for screen viewing. The serif typeface was chosen because it reads clearly at small sizes in print, not because it looks antique.

This distinction matters. A poster that looks informational is not the same as a poster that functions informationally. The test is simple: can a person stand in front of it and find what they need without frustration?

The Anatomy of a Cut

Before designing anything, I had to settle the taxonomy. Fish anatomy is not standardized across species, but the major culinary cuts are consistent enough to warrant a general guide. I focused on the cuts most relevant to common cooking fish: salmon, cod, halibut, tuna, and sea bass.

The loin is the primary fillet, running along the back. It is thick, meaty, and relatively uniform. For most cooking methods—searing, roasting, grilling—the loin is the default choice. It holds together well and cooks evenly.

The belly is the fatty, rich section along the lower edge. It is prized for raw preparations (think toro in sushi) and for gentle cooking where the fat can render slowly. It is also the first part to spoil, which is why it is often removed and sold separately.

The tail tapers thin and is often tougher. It works well for dishes where the fish is broken down or flaked—fish cakes, curries, or stock. It is not the cut you sear and serve whole unless you enjoy chewing.

The collar (or cheek) is the area around the gills and jaw. It is dark, fatty, and intensely flavorful. In Japanese cuisine, hamachi collar grilled with salt is a delicacy. In Western kitchens, it is often discarded. This is a loss. The collar is one of the best parts of the fish for high-heat cooking.

The frame (or carcass) is what remains after filleting. It is not a cut in the culinary sense, but it is the foundation of fish stock. A poster that ignores the frame ignores the most economical use of the animal.

Why Labels Fail (and How to Fix Them)

Most fish cut posters fail at the labeling stage. The illustration is beautiful, the anatomy is accurate, and then the labels are a mess. They float in white space, connected to the illustration by thin lines that cross each other and force the eye to travel in circles. The reader spends more time tracing lines than absorbing information.

I tested three labeling approaches.

Approach One: Radial Layout. Labels arranged in a circle around the fish, with lines pointing inward. Visually striking. Functionally terrible. The reading order is chaotic. The eye jumps from label to label without a clear path.

Approach Two: Left-Right Split. The fish on the left, a list of cuts on the right. Clean. But it separates the information from the referent. The reader looks at the text, then looks back at the fish, then back at the text. The physical distance creates cognitive friction.

Approach Three: Anchored Left-Aligned Labels. Each label is positioned near the cut it describes, aligned to the left edge of the cut area, with a single thin connector line. The labels are short: the name, and one line of use case. The reading order follows the fish from head to tail, then down to the cooking reference section.

I chose the third approach. It is less dramatic than the radial layout, but it respects the way people actually read. The eye moves naturally from the illustration to the adjacent text, then to the next cut. The result is a poster that can be scanned in seconds, not studied for minutes.

Color as Information, Not Decoration

Color is the most overused tool in information design. Designers reach for color to make things "pop," forgetting that color carries semantic weight. A red cut and a blue cut imply a difference that may not exist. A gradient implies a continuum that may be misleading.

I restricted the palette to four values: a warm gray for the background, a light ochre for the primary cuts, a pale pink for the fatty sections (belly, collar), and a single dark blue used only for the cooking method icons. The colors are subtle. They distinguish adjacent cuts without shouting. They suggest categories without imposing a rigid system.

Critically, I did not use color to encode cooking method. It would have been intuitive to color-code "raw," "pan-sear," and "stock," but that would have overloaded the illustration. The anatomy section is about anatomy. The cooking section is about cooking. Mixing the two would have created a poster that is good at neither.

The Cooking Method Quick Reference

The lower third of the poster is a quick reference table. It answers the question that prompts most fish-related searches: I have this cut. What do I do with it?

The first version of this section was comprehensive. Twelve cuts, each with an icon and a short description. It looked thorough. When printed at A3, it was unreadable. The icons were too small to distinguish. The text was too tight to scan. The section became a wall of information that repelled the eye.

I cut it to six. Six cuts that cover the majority of home cooking scenarios: loin (sear, roast), belly (raw, gentle poach), tail (curry, cake), collar (grill, broil), cheek (pan-fry), frame (stock). The remaining cuts are still labeled in the anatomy section, but they are not repeated in the quick reference.

This was a difficult decision. "Comprehensive" feels safer than "curated." But a poster that is too dense to use is not comprehensive—it is abandoned. The goal was never to replace a textbook. It was to give a cook standing in a kitchen one useful piece of information in thirty seconds. Six entries achieve that. Twelve do not.

What I Would Do Differently

No project is perfect, and a portfolio that claims otherwise is not honest. Two things would change in a second iteration.

First, the cooking method icons. I designed six simple icons: a pan, a pot, a knife, a flame, a snowflake, and a plate. The pan and the pot are clear. The knife is clear. The flame is clear. The snowflake (intended to mean "suitable for freezing") and the plate (intended to mean "suitable for plating") are not. They require a legend that does not exist on the poster. A reader should not have to guess what an icon means. Either the icons need to be more literal, or they need labels. The current version assumes too much.

Second, the versioning. What exists now is a compromise between two use cases: a teaching poster for culinary students and a decorative reference for home kitchens. A teaching poster can be denser. A kitchen poster needs to be more restrained. Splitting the project into two versions—a full-information A2 for classrooms and a streamlined A3 for home use—would have produced better outcomes for both audiences. The single version is good, but it is not optimal for either.

What This Project Demonstrates

This is not a project about drawing fish. It is a project about making complex information accessible without simplifying it into uselessness. The skills on display are not illustration skills alone, but the ability to define a problem, research the domain, make design decisions with reasons, reject attractive but flawed solutions, and iterate based on how people actually use the result.

The poster works. It is readable. It is printable. It answers real questions for real cooks. And it is honest about its limitations. That combination—functional, thoughtful, and self-aware—is what I bring to every project.
