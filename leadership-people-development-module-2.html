Leadership & People Development Academy Module 2

Trainer Skills & Instructional Design

A practical guide to identifying learning needs, designing useful sessions, facilitating participation and checking whether training improves workplace performance.

Designed for workplace trainers, hospitality leaders, supervisors, customer-experience teams and people-development professionals.

Build a 60-minute lesson plan Take the knowledge check

1. Learning outcomes

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish a training need from a process, equipment, staffing or management problem.
  • Write a measurable learning objective using observable workplace behaviour.
  • Choose suitable methods for the learner, task, risk and available time.
  • Structure a session with practice, feedback and a clear assessment.
  • Facilitate questions, demonstrations, role-plays and learner reflection.
  • Evaluate learning and plan follow-up on-the-job application.

2. Start with training needs analysis

Effective instructional design begins with a performance gap, not with a slide deck. Identify the expected standard, the current performance and the evidence of the gap. Then investigate what is causing it.

Expected standard

What should the employee know or do? Define the observable standard, procedure, service behaviour or quality target.

Current performance

Use observation, checklists, guest feedback, error records, audits, interviews or practical demonstrations to understand what happens now.

Root cause

Check whether the gap comes from knowledge, skill, unclear procedures, unavailable equipment, workload, incentives or supervision.

Example: inconsistent restaurant order-taking

If servers repeatedly omit modifiers, first review the ordering workflow, POS layout, staffing pressure and written standards. If the process is workable but employees cannot follow it, a demonstration and supervised practice may address the skill gap. Training alone will not fix a confusing POS or an understaffed shift.

Output: a concise statement of the gap, its evidence, its likely cause and the performance change required.

3. Write measurable learning objectives

An objective states what learners will be able to do after training and the standard they must meet. Prefer action verbs that can be observed or assessed.

Weak objective

Understand food safety.

Understanding is difficult to verify from the statement alone.

Stronger objective

Demonstrate the handwashing procedure in the correct sequence and meet every critical point on the approved checklist.

The action and assessment standard are visible.

Useful verbs include identify, demonstrate, prepare, calculate, inspect, compare, complete, explain and resolve. Choose the verb that matches the actual task and the level of performance required.

Objective-writing template

By the end of the session, the learner will be able to [observable action] under [conditions] to [standard or criteria].

Example: By the end of the session, the supervisor will conduct a five-minute pre-shift briefing using the team checklist, covering all required safety and service points.

4. Select methods that fit adult learners

Adults bring experience, expectations and practical problems to training. Make the relevance clear, invite participation and connect concepts to tasks learners actually perform. Respect existing expertise while correcting unsafe or ineffective practice.

Do not rely on a lecture when learners need physical practice. For safety-critical tasks, explain the risk, demonstrate the approved procedure, supervise practice and verify competence before independent work.

6. Facilitation, questioning and feedback

Explain clearly

Use short explanations, plain language, relevant examples and a logical sequence. Introduce essential terms before using them in exercises.

Ask useful questions

Use open questions to explore reasoning and specific questions to verify critical facts. Give learners time to think before answering.

Make practice safe

Set respectful ground rules. Correct errors promptly where safety or guest welfare is involved, without humiliating the learner.

Give actionable feedback

Describe the observed behaviour, explain the standard, identify what worked and agree on one concrete improvement to practise.

Example feedback

Instead of You need to be more professional, say: You confirmed the main order but did not repeat the allergy information. Before sending the order, repeat the allergy requirement and confirm the correct procedure. Follow workplace policy for allergy communication and escalation.

7. Assess learning and evaluate training

Assessment checks whether an individual has achieved the learning objective. Evaluation examines whether the learning intervention was useful and whether performance improved.

  • Knowledge check: questions, short written responses or explanation in the learners own words.
  • Skills check: observation against a task checklist or a practical demonstration.
  • Scenario assessment: a realistic decision or role-play scored against defined criteria.
  • Workplace follow-up: a supervisor observes the task after training and records whether the standard is sustained.
  • Business indicator: review a relevant measure, such as order errors, audit findings, complaints or task completion time, while considering other factors that may influence the result.

Attendance and learner satisfaction can be useful information, but neither proves competence. Establish the assessment criteria before the session and document any required retraining or sign-off under your organisations procedures.
