Food Handling Guide

A quick-reference poster covering the 12 core food handling practices every hospitality and F&B team should follow — from hand hygiene to safe hot and cold holding temperatures.

Why Food Handling Discipline Matters

In any kitchen, restaurant, hotel outlet, or catering operation, food handling discipline is the single biggest lever a team has over guest safety, food cost, and audit readiness. A single lapse in hand hygiene, temperature control, or cross-contamination prevention can turn into a guest illness, a failed inspection, or a costly product recall. The 12 rules on this poster are not arbitrary — each one maps directly to the control points that HACCP plans and food safety inspectors check first, and each one is something a shift supervisor can actually observe and coach in real time.

The 12 Rules at a Glance

  • Wash hands before handling food — the single most effective barrier against transferring contamination from hands to food.
  • Wear a clean uniform and hair net — reduces the risk of physical and biological contaminants reaching food.
  • Use clean and sanitized equipment — boards, knives, and utensils that carry residue from a previous task are a direct contamination pathway.
  • Separate raw and cooked food — prevents cross-contamination between items at different stages of preparation.
  • Store food at safe temperature — protects both quality and safety across the storage chain.
  • Follow FIFO and FEFO — first-in-first-out and first-expired-first-out rotation keeps waste down and stock fresh.
  • Use clean gloves when required — especially critical for ready-to-eat food that will not be cooked again before service.
  • Cover food properly — a simple, low-cost control against dust, pests, and airborne contamination.
  • Check expiry dates — a daily discipline that prevents spoiled or expired product from reaching a guest.
  • Reheat food above 74°C — the core temperature needed to destroy the harmful bacteria that survive refrigeration.
  • Keep hot food above 60°C — anything held below this threshold moves into the temperature danger zone.
  • Keep cold food below 5°C — slows bacterial growth and keeps perishable product safe through service.

Where This Poster Belongs

Print or display this poster in staff areas, at the kitchen pass, near handwash stations, and in pantry or storeroom areas as a constant visual reminder. Posting the rules is useful, but the real value comes from pairing the poster with a short toolbox talk during shift briefings — walk the team through one or two rules a day, connect each one to a real example from the operation, and rotate through the full list weekly. Teams retain food safety habits far better when a rule is explained with a "why" rather than simply enforced as a "must."

Building a Culture Around These Rules

Posters and checklists set the standard, but culture is what makes the standard stick on a busy Friday night service when the pass is backed up and everyone is under pressure. A few practices that reinforce this poster in daily operations:

  • Assign a rotating "food safety champion" per shift who spot-checks two or three rules and reports back at the pre-shift briefing.
  • Log hot-holding and cold-holding temperatures at fixed intervals rather than only when an inspector is expected.
  • Treat every near-miss — a mislabeled container, a raw board used for garnish — as a coaching moment, not a blame exercise.
  • Review expiry-date and FIFO/FEFO compliance as part of the weekly stock take, not as a separate audit.
  • Recognize staff publicly when they catch and correct a food safety issue before it becomes a problem.

Food handling is one of the few areas of hospitality operations where the cost of prevention is almost always lower than the cost of a single incident — in guest trust, in regulatory standing, and in the wellbeing of the people you serve. Keeping these 12 rules visible, understood, and practiced consistently is one of the most effective things any F&B leader can do for their operation.
