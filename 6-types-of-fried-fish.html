6 Types of Fried Fish: Indian Fish Fry Recipes, Methods & Kitchen Tips

By Nigel A. Thomas, Hospitality Executive • Recipes & Culinary Series

Walk into any good Indian kitchen, from a Mumbai fish market restaurant to a five-star coastal specialty outlet, and you will find one dish that never leaves the menu: fried fish. It is quick, it is generous, it sells itself on aroma alone, and it rewards a cook who understands heat, timing and restraint. Over thirty years in luxury hotels, resorts and cruise operations across India, the Middle East and the USA, I have watched fried fish hold its place on breakfast buffets, bar snack menus, banquet stations and à la carte tasting flights alike.

This guide covers six distinct Indian fried fish styles: Crispy Rava Fish Fry, Amritsari Crispy Fish Fry, Surmai Tawa Masala Fry, Kerala Style Fish Fry, Spiced Pomfret Tawa Fry and Bengali Machher Chop Fry. Each one uses a different coating, a different fat and a different cooking method, which makes them a perfect training set for anyone learning how fish behaves in a pan or a fryer. You will find the ingredients and method for every recipe, followed by practical chef's notes drawn from real kitchen service.

Why Fried Fish Deserves a Place on Every Menu

Fish is one of the few proteins that can be sold at every price point without losing its dignity. A humble small fish fried whole with pepper and curry leaves can anchor a rustic Kerala thali, while a whole pomfret seared on a hot griddle can be the centrepiece of a premium seafood platter. The technique is the same family of skills: dry the surface, season boldly, apply a light coating, and cook fast in the right amount of fat.

From a business point of view, fried fish has three big advantages. First, the preparation is front-loaded. Marination and coating can be done in the morning during mise en place, and final cooking takes only minutes on order. Second, the flavour range is enormous while the ingredient list stays short, so a kitchen can offer six different dishes from a handful of spices, pastes and flours. Third, guests understand it instantly. Nobody needs a long menu description to know what a crisp, golden fish fry will taste like.

The six styles in this article also show how regional Indian cooking solves the same problem in different ways. Semolina, gram flour, rice flour, breadcrumbs, spice paste alone and mustard paste each create a different texture, and each pairs with a different fat, from coconut oil in the south to neutral oil for deep frying in the north.

Choosing the Right Fish Before You Cook

Good frying starts at the fish counter. A perfect recipe cannot rescue tired fish, and a strong-flavoured spice paste cannot hide a fish that has been handled badly. When receiving fish in a hotel kitchen, I train teams to check five things every single time: clear, slightly bulging eyes; bright red or pink gills; firm flesh that springs back when pressed; a clean sea smell rather than a sharp ammonia note; and a delivery temperature close to melting ice.

The cut matters as much as the species. Here is how it works across the six recipes:

  • Fillets (rava fry, Bengali chop): fast to cook and easy to portion, but delicate. Choose firm white fish such as basa, sole or a boneless fillet of seer, and handle them gently once coated.
  • Cubes (Amritsari): boneless chunks about 3 cm square that stay juicy inside a crisp batter. Firm river or sea fish that does not flake apart works best.
  • Steaks (surmai tawa masala): thick cross-cut slices of kingfish or seer fish, with a central bone that keeps the flesh moist and gives the fish structure on the tawa.
  • Whole small fish (Kerala style): sardines, mackerel or similar. Scoring the skin lets the marinade reach the flesh and helps heat move evenly.
  • Whole pomfret: a prized Indian fish with sweet, delicate flesh. Slits along the body are essential so the spice paste and heat penetrate.

Frozen fish is perfectly acceptable when it has been frozen properly and thawed slowly under refrigeration, never at room temperature. Pat every piece dry with a clean towel before marinating. Surface water is the enemy of crispness, because the pan must first boil off that water before any browning can begin.

The Science Behind a Perfect Fish Fry

Every great fried fish balances three things: a crisp exterior, a moist interior and clean flavour. The crust forms when moisture on the surface evaporates and the coating dehydrates and browns, a process linked to the Maillard reaction. The interior stays moist because the coating and the fast cooking seal in juices while the fish steams gently from its own water content.

Oil temperature is the control that decides everything. For deep frying, work in the range of about 175 to 180°C. If the oil is too cool, the coating absorbs fat and turns greasy and heavy. If it is too hot, the outside burns before the centre is cooked. For shallow frying and tawa cooking, a steady medium heat is the goal, hot enough to sizzle the moment the fish touches the surface but never smoking.

  • Dry the fish. Moisture on the surface creates steam and a soggy crust.
  • Do not overcrowd. Too many pieces drop the oil temperature sharply and the fish steams instead of frying.
  • Turn once. Let a crust form and release naturally before flipping. Fish that sticks is fish that is not ready to turn.
  • Drain on a wire rack. Paper towels trap steam underneath and soften the crust. A rack keeps air moving.
  • Season at the right time. Salt draws water, so marinate briefly and salt just before cooking when a very crisp result is needed.

A note on marination with lemon juice or other acids: a short marination of 15 to 30 minutes in the refrigerator adds brightness and lightly firms the flesh. Leave fish too long in acid and the texture turns chalky and dry, so watch the clock.

The Six Fried Fish Recipes

1. Crispy Rava Fish Fry

Rava fry is the friendliest way into fried fish. Popular along the Konkan and Karnataka coast, it uses coarse semolina as the coating, which fries into a sandy, golden crust that shatters at the first bite. It is a menu favourite because it looks impressive, holds crispness well and needs no batter.

Ingredients

  • 2 Fish Fillets
  • 2 tbsp Semolina (Rava)
  • 1 tsp Red Chili Powder
  • 1/2 tsp Turmeric
  • 1 tbsp Lemon Juice

Method

  1. Marinate fish with spices and lemon juice.
  2. Coat thoroughly with coarse semolina.
  3. Shallow fry on medium heat until golden.

Chef’s notes: Press the semolina firmly onto both sides so it grips the moist surface. Rest the coated fillets for five minutes before frying, which helps the crust stay attached. Use coarse rava, not fine, for the best crunch. In a banquet setting, coat in advance and hold on a tray in the chiller, then fry to order.

Serve with: Onion rings, lemon wedges, green chutney or a simple mint dip.

2. Amritsari Crispy Fish Fry

Amritsari fish is a Punjabi classic, celebrated for its light, spicy gram-flour batter and the warm, earthy notes of carom seeds. Traditionally deep fried, it delivers a crisp jacket around tender boneless cubes and is a natural fit for bar menus and cocktail service.

Ingredients

  • 200g Fish Cubes
  • 2 tbsp Besan (Gram Flour)
  • 1 tsp Carom Seeds (Ajwain)
  • 1 tsp Ginger Paste
  • Oil for Deep Frying

Method

  1. Make batter with besan, ajwain, and spices.
  2. Coat fish pieces evenly in batter.
  3. Deep fry until crispy and golden brown.

Chef’s notes: The batter should coat the back of a spoon without running off. Too thin and it fries flat; too thick and it turns bready. Crush the ajwain lightly between your palms to release its aroma before adding. Fry in small batches and keep the oil steady, then finish with a pinch of chaat masala and a squeeze of lemon.

Serve with: Mint chutney, sliced onions with lemon, and a cold beer or a spiced lime soda.

3. Surmai Tawa Masala Fry

Surmai, known as kingfish or seer fish, is a beloved Mumbai and Konkan fish with firm, meaty flesh that stands up to bold masala. Cooked on a seasoned iron tawa with coconut oil, it develops a deep red, seared crust and a slightly smoky flavour that is hard to replicate in a non-stick pan.

Ingredients

  • 2 Kingfish Steaks
  • 1 tbsp Red Chili Paste
  • 1 tsp Garlic Paste
  • 1/2 tsp Coriander Powder
  • 2 tbsp Coconut Oil

Method

  1. Apply garlic and chili paste onto fish.
  2. Heat coconut oil on an iron tawa.
  3. Pan fry each side for 4 minutes until seared.

Chef’s notes: Heat the tawa properly before adding oil, and let the oil shimmer before the fish goes down. The four-minute side is a guide, so adjust for steak thickness and look for opaque flesh right through to the bone. Do not move the steak while it sears. Once it releases cleanly, it is ready to turn.

Serve with: Sliced onion rings, lime, and steamed rice with a light coconut curry.

4. Kerala Style Fish Fry

Kerala fish fry, or meen varuthathu, is defined by shallots, black pepper and fresh curry leaves, all shallow fried until fragrant. It is usually made with whole small fish, and the aroma of curry leaves crackling in hot oil is one of the most recognisable smells in South Indian cooking.

Ingredients

  • 2 Whole Small Fish
  • 1 tbsp Shallot Paste
  • 1 tsp Black Pepper
  • 1 tsp Fennel Powder
  • Curry Leaves

Method

  1. Score fish and rub with shallot pepper mix.
  2. Shallow fry with fresh curry leaves.
  3. Cook until skin is fragrant and crispy.

Chef’s notes: Score the skin on both sides with two or three diagonal cuts so the paste reaches the flesh. Add the curry leaves to the oil just before the fish goes in, so they crisp without burning. Whole fish suits a moderate flame, because a high one will scorch the skin before the centre is done.

Serve with: Kerala red rice, sliced red onion, and a simple coconut chutney.

5. Spiced Pomfret Tawa Fry

Pomfret is one of India's most treasured fish, especially in Mumbai, where a whole fried pomfret is a status dish on many restaurant tables. Its flesh is sweet and delicate, so the spice paste should support it rather than overwhelm it. A light dusting of rice flour gives a thin, glassy crispness.

Ingredients

  • 1 Whole Pomfret
  • 1 tbsp Ginger Garlic Paste
  • 1 tsp Lemon Juice
  • 1/2 tsp Garam Masala
  • 1 tbsp Rice Flour

Method

  1. Slit pomfret and marinate with spice paste.
  2. Dust lightly with rice flour for crispiness.
  3. Sear on hot griddle with minimal oil.

Chef’s notes: Make deep, even slits on both sides so the marinade penetrates and the fish cooks evenly. Dust the rice flour on only just before cooking, and shake off the excess, since damp flour turns pasty. Use a very hot griddle and just enough oil to prevent sticking. Pomfret cooks quickly, so watch it closely.

Serve with: Lemon, a fresh green salad and light lemon rice.

6. Bengali Machher Chop Fry

Machher chop is Bengal's answer to the fish croquette, with mustard paste giving a sharp, nose-tingling heat that Bengali kitchens are known for. This version uses fish fillets seasoned with mustard and panch phoron, then crumbed and shallow fried, which gives a golden crust with an aromatic centre.

Ingredients

  • 200g Fish Fillets
  • 1 tbsp Mustard Paste
  • 1/2 tsp Panch Phoron Powder
  • 1/2 cup Breadcrumbs
  • 1 Egg (for Dip)

Method

  1. Season fish with mustard paste and spices.
  2. Dip in egg wash and roll in breadcrumbs.
  3. Shallow fry until golden and crispy.

Chef’s notes: Use the standard breading order: seasoned fish, egg wash, then crumbs, pressing firmly for full coverage. Chill the crumbed fish for 15 minutes before frying, since the coating sets and stays put in the pan. Add mustard paste in moderation, as too much turns bitter when heated. Treat the egg, gluten and mustard as declared allergens on the menu.

Serve with: Kasundi mustard, a wedge of lime and sliced onion salad.

Food Safety Standards for Fried Fish

Fish is a high-risk food, and a busy kitchen has no room for shortcuts. The principles below apply whether you are cooking at home or running a banquet for two hundred guests. These are the same habits emphasised in ServSafe and HACCP-based training.

  • Cold chain: keep raw fish between 0 and 4°C, ideally on crushed ice in a drained tray, and never leave it on a counter during preparation.
  • Core temperature: cook fish until the centre reaches at least 63°C (145°F), and check with a probe thermometer rather than guessing.
  • Hot holding: if fried fish must be held, keep it above 60°C (140°F) and never for longer than the quality standard allows. Crisp coatings soften quickly under lids.
  • Cross-contamination: use dedicated boards, knives and containers for raw fish, and keep raw seafood below ready-to-eat food in the chiller.
  • Frying oil: filter between services, replace when it darkens, foams or smells, and never top up old oil indefinitely.
  • Allergens: fish is a major allergen. These recipes also involve gluten (semolina and breadcrumbs), egg and mustard, so declare them clearly on menus and to service staff.

When marinating, always keep fish covered in the refrigerator, and discard leftover marinade that has touched raw fish. Never reuse it as a sauce unless it has been fully boiled first.

Menu Planning, Costing and Service Tips

For hotel and restaurant operators, fried fish is an ideal candidate for menu engineering. The core protein cost is easy to calculate, spice and coating costs are low, and the perceived value is high. As a rule of thumb, price the dish on the protein cost plus a fair contribution for labour and oil, then compare margins against other starters on the same menu.

  • Portion control: weigh fillets and cubes in advance, for example 80 to 100g per starter portion, so cost stays consistent.
  • Menu placement: a signature fish fry works well as a starter, a bar snack or part of a mixed seafood platter.
  • Batch prep: marinate and coat in morning mise en place, hold chilled on labelled trays, and fry to order for the best texture.
  • Yield awareness: whole fish loses weight to bones, head and trimmings, so cost per edible portion is higher than the purchase price suggests.
  • Garnish discipline: onion rings, lemon wedges and fresh herbs cost little but lift plate appeal enormously.
  • Timing at the pass: fried fish waits for nobody. Coordinate with the expeditor so it leaves the kitchen within moments of coming out of the oil.

Plating deserves attention too. Serve fried fish on a warm plate, place the crispest side up, and add a sharp, fresh element such as lemon, raw onion or a herb chutney. Contrast is the secret: crisp against soft, hot against cool, spicy against fresh.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

  • Wet fish going into the pan. Always pat dry before marinating and again before coating.
  • Overcrowding the pan or fryer. Cook in batches to keep the temperature steady.
  • Impatient turning. Let the crust form. If the fish sticks, it needs another moment.
  • Over-marinating in acid. Lemon juice and lime firm and dry the fish. Keep the time short.
  • Cooking straight from the refrigerator. Let fish rest a few minutes so the centre cooks in step with the outside, while staying within safe holding rules.
  • Skipping the rest on crumbed fish. A short chill sets the coating and prevents it from falling off.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which fish is best for frying? Firm, mild fish such as seer fish (surmai), pomfret, basa, sole and mackerel suit these recipes. Match the cut to the technique: fillets for coating, steaks for the tawa, and whole fish for scoring and marinating.

How do I keep the coating crisp? Dry the fish thoroughly, coat just before frying, keep the oil at the right temperature and drain on a rack instead of paper towels.

Can I bake or air fry these instead? Yes, for a lighter result. Brush the coating lightly with oil and cook at a high temperature. The texture will be drier than pan frying, but the flavours carry through.

What oil should I use? Coconut oil suits the south Indian styles and adds a characteristic aroma. For deep frying, use a neutral oil with a high smoke point, and filter it regularly.

How do I know the fish is cooked? The flesh turns opaque and flakes easily, and a probe thermometer reads at least 63°C in the thickest part.

Final Thoughts

India’s fried fish traditions prove that great cooking does not depend on complexity. A handful of spices, the right coating and disciplined heat control can produce six completely different dishes, each with its own regional voice. Whether you are a home cook building confidence or a hospitality professional designing a seafood menu, mastering these six recipes will sharpen the fundamentals that matter most: sourcing, seasoning, timing and food safety.
