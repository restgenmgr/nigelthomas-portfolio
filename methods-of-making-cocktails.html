7 Methods of Making Cocktails Building to Throwing

Every classic cocktail is built on one of seven core techniques. Knowing which method a drink calls for — and why — is the difference between a bartender and someone who just follows a recipe card.

By Nigel A. Thomas September 2026 ~6 min read Beverage Service

Ask a guest what makes a Martini different from a Margarita and most will say the glass, or the garnish. Ask a trained bartender, and the real answer is the method: one is stirred, the other is shaken. The technique behind a cocktail is not a style choice — it changes dilution, texture, temperature and clarity in ways a recipe card alone cannot fix.

These seven methods cover essentially every cocktail a bar will ever be asked to make. Learning which method belongs to which drink — and why — is one of the fastest ways a new bartender earns the confidence of a busy service.

"A recipe tells you the ingredients. The method tells you whether the drink will actually taste like it's supposed to."

01 Building

Straight into the glass

Ingredients are added directly into the serving glass, usually over ice, and gently mixed if required. No separate mixing vessel is used at all.

Best For
  • Gin & Tonic
  • Cuba Libre
  • Vodka Soda

02 Stirring

Chilled without dilution or cloudiness

Ingredients are combined, chilled and diluted by stirring with ice in a mixing glass, then strained into the serving glass. Best for spirit-forward cocktails where clarity and a smooth, silky texture are the goal.

Best For
  • Martini
  • Manhattan
  • Negroni

03 Shaking

Vigorous, cold, and fully combined

Ingredients are vigorously shaken with ice to chill, dilute and thoroughly combine them — essential for anything containing juice, syrup, egg white, or other ingredients that need real integration, not just chilling.

Best For
  • Daiquiri
  • Whiskey Sour
  • Margarita

04 Blending

Smooth, frozen, and fully emulsified

Ingredients are combined in an electric blender, usually with ice or frozen fruit, to create a smooth or frozen drink with a consistent texture throughout the glass.

Best For
  • Frozen Margarita
  • Piña Colada
  • Frozen Daiquiri

05 Layering

Distinct bands by density

Ingredients are carefully poured in stages so they remain in distinct visible layers, relying on differences in density and sugar content rather than any mixing at all.

Best For
  • B-52
  • Pousse-Café
  • Baby Guinness

06 Rolling

Gentle mixing without aeration

The drink is gently poured back and forth between two vessels to mix and chill it while minimising aeration — used when a shaken texture would break the drink's structure.

Best For
  • Bloody Mary
  • Red Snapper
  • Bloody Maria

07 Throwing

Height, aeration and theatre

The drink is poured between two vessels, often from a height, to mix, chill and aerate it — part technique, part showmanship, and a genuine test of a bartender's control.

Best For
  • Blue Blazer
  • Classic Cocktail Throwing

08 Quick Reference

Training a New Bartender

Start with building and stirring: the lowest-risk methods to teach ratio and pour control before introducing shaking.

Shaking is a skill, not just motion: the seal, the grip, and the shake duration all affect dilution — train it deliberately, not by imitation alone.

Layering and throwing are advanced: both demand precision most new hires only develop after several months behind the bar.

The Real Test

If a bartender can explain why a Martini is stirred and a Daiquiri is shaken — not just which glass each goes in — the technique has actually been understood, not memorised.

None of these seven methods is more "advanced" than the others in isolation — each exists because a specific combination of ingredients demands it. The bartenders guests remember are the ones who know instantly which method a drink calls for, and execute it the same way, every single time.
