4 Types of Homemade Mayonnaise: Classic, Garlic, Spicy and Herb

By Nigel A. Thomas, Hospitality Executive · October 17, 2026 · Food and Beverage Series

Mayonnaise is one of the most useful sauces in any kitchen. It dresses salads, binds fillings, spreads on sandwiches, tops burgers and turns into a dozen other sauces with one or two additions. It is also one of the cheapest sauces to make from scratch, and a freshly made batch tastes far better than most jars. This guide covers four classic versions, Classic, Garlic, Spicy and Herb, with exact ingredients, method, uses, troubleshooting and the food safety rules every cook should know.

What Mayonnaise Actually Is

Mayonnaise is an emulsion, which means a stable mixture of two things that normally refuse to mix: oil and a water-based liquid. In this case the liquid is lemon juice or vinegar plus the water in the egg yolk. The egg yolk contains lecithin, a natural emulsifier that sits between the oil and the water and holds tiny droplets of oil suspended in the liquid. When you whisk oil in slowly, those droplets stay small and evenly spread, and the sauce thickens into a smooth, creamy cream.

Understanding this one idea solves nearly every mayonnaise problem. If you add the oil too quickly, the droplets are too large and merge back together, and the sauce splits. If the ingredients are too cold, the emulsion is slower to form. If you add enough oil to a small amount of yolk and liquid, the mixture thickens to the texture you want. Once you know the rules, you can make it by hand, with a stick blender or in a food processor, and fix it when it goes wrong.

The Base Ingredients and Why They Matter

Every version on the poster starts from the same base: one egg, one cup of neutral oil, one tablespoon of lemon juice or vinegar, half a teaspoon of salt and, if you like, a quarter teaspoon of sugar. Each ingredient has a job.

  • Egg at room temperature. A room-temperature egg blends more easily and emulsifies faster than a cold one. Take it out of the refrigerator about half an hour before you start, or place it in warm water for a few minutes. For food safety, use a fresh, clean, uncracked egg, or better, a pasteurised one.
  • Neutral oil. Sunflower, rapeseed (canola) or a light vegetable oil gives a clean flavour that lets the other ingredients speak. Strong extra virgin olive oil turns bitter when whisked at speed, so if you want olive oil flavour, use a mild one or blend a small amount with neutral oil.
  • Lemon juice or vinegar. The acid adds brightness, balances the richness of the oil and helps the emulsion. It also slightly lowers the pH, which contributes to the keeping quality of the sauce.
  • Mustard (optional). Mustard contains natural emulsifiers of its own and gives the sauce extra stability and a gentle tang. A teaspoon is enough.
  • Salt. Salt seasons the sauce, and it matters more than people expect, because unsalted mayonnaise tastes flat and greasy.
  • Sugar (optional). A small pinch rounds off sharp acidity. It is a matter of taste, and many cooks leave it out.

The base recipe makes a little over a cup of mayonnaise, roughly 300 ml, which is enough for a small family meal or a few days of sandwiches. Double or triple it for a catering batch, but keep the ratio the same.

The Basic Method: Three Ways to Make It

By Hand with a Whisk

Place the egg yolk (or the whole egg, as in the poster recipe), lemon juice or vinegar, mustard and salt in a bowl and whisk until pale and slightly thick. Begin adding the oil drop by drop, whisking constantly. After the first few tablespoons, when the mixture begins to thicken and look creamy, you can pour the oil in a very thin steady stream. Keep whisking until all the oil is in and the sauce is thick and glossy. Taste, adjust the salt and acid, and thin with a teaspoon of water if it is too thick.

With a Stick Blender

Put everything in a tall jar that fits the blender head closely, with the oil on top. Place the blender head flat on the bottom, switch it on and hold it still for about ten seconds while the mayonnaise forms at the base. Then slowly lift the blender upward to pull the rest of the oil into the emulsion. This takes about a minute and gives a thick, stable result. It is the best method for beginners.

In a Food Processor

Blend the egg, acid, mustard and salt for twenty seconds. With the motor running, add the oil in a thin stream through the feed tube, slowly at first and then a little faster as the sauce thickens. Stop as soon as it is thick and smooth, because over-processing can thin it.

1. Classic Mayonnaise

Ingredients: 1 egg (room temperature), 1 cup neutral oil such as sunflower, 1 tablespoon lemon juice or vinegar, 1 teaspoon mustard (optional), ½ teaspoon salt, ¼ teaspoon sugar (optional).

This is the foundation for every other version. It should taste clean, lightly tangy and rich, with a smooth, spoonable texture that holds a peak. Use it in sandwiches, potato salad, coleslaw, egg salad, tuna and chicken salad, and as the binder for cold appetizer fillings such as stuffed tomatoes and deviled eggs. For a lighter flavour, use lemon juice. For a sharper flavour, use white wine or white vinegar.

Once you have the classic version in hand, taste and adjust it before you move on to the variations, because everything else builds on it. If it is too thick, whisk in a teaspoon of water. If it is bland, add salt first and acid second. If it tastes oily, it needs more acid or salt rather than more mustard.

2. Garlic Mayonnaise

Ingredients: 1 egg (room temperature), 1 cup neutral oil, 1 tablespoon lemon juice or vinegar, 3 to 4 garlic cloves, ½ teaspoon salt, ¼ teaspoon sugar (optional).

Garlic mayonnaise is the classic partner for fried and grilled food. It works on fries, grilled chicken, fish, roast vegetables, burgers and sandwiches. Peel the garlic and crush it to a smooth paste with the salt, using the flat of a knife or a mortar and pestle, so there are no hard pieces in the finished sauce. Add the paste to the egg and lemon at the start, then whisk in the oil as in the basic method.

Use fewer cloves for a gentle flavour and more for a bold one. Raw garlic grows stronger as the sauce stands, so make it a little milder than you want it to taste on the day. A note on safety: garlic in oil is a known concern for botulism when it is stored at room temperature, so keep garlic mayonnaise refrigerated at all times and use it within two to three days.

3. Spicy Mayonnaise

Ingredients: 1 egg (room temperature), 1 cup neutral oil, 1 tablespoon lemon juice or vinegar, 2 to 3 tablespoons hot sauce or chili sauce, ½ teaspoon salt, ¼ teaspoon sugar (optional).

Spicy mayonnaise is the sauce behind many modern sandwiches, burgers, wraps and sushi rolls. Make the classic base, then whisk in the hot sauce at the end so the heat is even and you can adjust it. Start with two tablespoons, taste, then add more. A sweet chili sauce gives a milder, sweeter result, while a sharp hot sauce gives a cleaner heat. A pinch of smoked paprika gives colour and a gentle smoky depth.

The hot sauce loosens the mayonnaise, so if you want a thick sauce, use a little less lemon juice in the base or reduce the quantity of liquid sauce. Use it on burgers, chicken sandwiches, fried seafood, fish tacos, rice bowls and as a dipping sauce for crispy snacks.

4. Herb Mayonnaise

Ingredients: 1 egg (room temperature), 1 cup neutral oil, 1 tablespoon lemon juice or vinegar, 2 tablespoons fresh herbs (parsley, dill, chives and so on), ½ teaspoon salt, ¼ teaspoon sugar (optional).

Herb mayonnaise is fresh, green and elegant. It suits poached or grilled fish, cold chicken, boiled eggs, asparagus, potatoes and cold platters. Chop the herbs very finely and fold them into the finished classic mayonnaise rather than blending them in at the start, which keeps the flecks of green visible and the herbs bright. Choose herbs to match the dish: dill for salmon, chives for eggs and potatoes, parsley for general use, tarragon for chicken and a little basil for tomato dishes.

Make herb mayonnaise close to the time you plan to use it. The herbs release moisture and darken over time, so the sauce looks best on the day it is made.

How to Fix Mayonnaise That Has Split

If your mayonnaise looks oily, grainy or runny, it has split, which means the emulsion has broken. It can almost always be rescued. Put a fresh egg yolk or a teaspoon of mustard in a clean bowl. Then whisk the broken mixture into it very slowly, a spoonful at a time, until it comes together again. Once the emulsion has re-formed, you can add the rest more quickly. If the sauce is too thick, loosen it with a few drops of water or lemon juice.

The usual causes of splitting are oil added too fast at the start, ingredients that are too cold, too much oil for the amount of egg, or a bowl that moved around while you were whisking. Place a damp cloth under the bowl, begin drop by drop, and the problem usually disappears.

Food Safety: What Every Cook Must Know

Traditional homemade mayonnaise contains raw egg, and that carries a risk of salmonella. The risk is small with fresh, clean, good-quality eggs, but it is real, and it matters more in professional kitchens and for vulnerable guests such as young children, pregnant women, older people and anyone with a weakened immune system.

  • Use pasteurised eggs when serving at-risk guests, in commercial service, or whenever you are unsure of the source. Pasteurised liquid egg and pasteurised shell eggs are widely available and work well.
  • Keep it cold. Refrigerate immediately after making, at 5°C (41°F) or below, and do not leave it out on the counter or buffet for more than a short time.
  • Use it quickly. A good rule is to use homemade mayonnaise within three days, and to discard it if it smells off, separates badly or has been left at room temperature.
  • Keep everything clean. Use clean equipment, wash hands, and store in a clean, covered, labelled and dated container.
  • Do not top up old batches. Never mix fresh mayonnaise into an older batch.
  • Label allergens. Mayonnaise contains egg, and mustard where used, and spicy or herb versions may carry further allergens.

Scaling Up for a Restaurant or Catering Kitchen

In a professional kitchen, mayonnaise is usually made in a food processor or a mixer, in batches of a litre or more. The method stays the same, but discipline matters more. Use pasteurised egg product, weigh the ingredients instead of measuring by volume, and make a base batch of classic mayonnaise that you then divide into smaller containers to flavour as needed. That way a single production gives you garlic, spicy and herb versions with very little extra work.

Label every container with the product name, date made and use-by date, store at the correct temperature, and use a first-in, first-out rotation. Taste every batch before service, because small differences in lemon, salt or garlic strength affect the final product. If you sell dishes that contain mayonnaise, record the sauce in your standard recipe so that the cost per portion stays accurate.

Serving Ideas for Each Version

  • Classic: sandwiches, club sandwiches, potato salad, coleslaw, egg salad, deviled eggs, tuna and chicken salad, and as a base for tartare sauce.
  • Garlic: fries, grilled chicken, grilled fish, kebabs, roast vegetables, burgers and bread.
  • Spicy: burgers, fried chicken sandwiches, wraps, fish tacos, rice bowls and fried seafood.
  • Herb: poached salmon, cold chicken, boiled eggs, asparagus, new potatoes and finger sandwiches.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding the oil too fast. This is the leading cause of split mayonnaise. Go slowly at the start.
  • Using cold ingredients. Room-temperature eggs and acid emulsify far more easily.
  • Using strong olive oil. It can turn bitter. Use a neutral or mild oil.
  • Under-seasoning. Taste at the end and adjust the salt and acid.
  • Storing it too long. Fresh homemade mayonnaise is not a long-life product. Label, date and discard on time.
  • Leaving it out. Mayonnaise served at a buffet should be kept chilled and replaced in small amounts.

Quick Reference: The Four Types at a Glance

  1. Classic: egg, neutral oil, lemon juice or vinegar, optional mustard, salt and optional sugar.
  2. Garlic: the classic base with 3 to 4 garlic cloves crushed to a paste.
  3. Spicy: the classic base with 2 to 3 tablespoons hot sauce or chili sauce.
  4. Herb: the classic base with 2 tablespoons of finely chopped fresh herbs.

Final Thoughts

Once you can make a good classic mayonnaise, you have four sauces in your hands, and many more besides. The skill is simple: room-temperature ingredients, a slow start with the oil and a clean, cold, careful approach to storage. Practise the classic version until it comes together without thinking, then build the garlic, spicy and herb variations on top of it.
