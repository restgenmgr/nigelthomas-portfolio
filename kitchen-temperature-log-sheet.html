🌡️ Kitchen Temperature Log Sheet

A printable temperature log for refrigeration, freezers, cooking, and hot/cold holding — for daily HACCP record keeping.

Why Kitchen Temperature Logging Matters

Temperature control is one of the few food safety hazards a commercial kitchen can measure directly and objectively — a thermometer either confirms an item is safe or it doesn't. That is exactly why regulators, auditors, and HACCP systems treat refrigeration, cooking, and holding temperatures as Critical Control Points (CCPs): the specific steps in a food's journey where a hazard can be prevented, eliminated, or reduced to a safe level, and where the outcome must be checked and recorded every time.

Most foodborne illness outbreaks traced back to a commercial kitchen come down to one of three failures: food held too long in the temperature danger zone, undercooked high-risk items such as poultry or ground meat, or refrigeration equipment that quietly drifted out of range without anyone noticing. A consistent, dated, signed-off log sheet is the paper trail that proves those checks actually happened — which is what a health inspector, an internal auditor, or your own kitchen team needs to see during a review or, worse, after a complaint.

What This Tool Does

This page gives you three ready-to-use logs — refrigeration & freezers, cooking & reheating, and hot/cold holding — with built-in target ranges for each. Fill it in on screen and print it at the end of shift, or print it blank and complete it by hand at the pass. Every log includes a corrective action column, because recording an out-of-range reading is only half the requirement — what you did about it matters just as much to an auditor.

How to Use This Log Sheet

  1. Set it up once per shift. Fill in the property name, date, and the name of the person recording readings at the top of the sheet.
  2. Check refrigeration at opening and closing. Walk-in fridges, freezers, and reach-ins should be checked at minimum twice a day; busier kitchens often check every four hours.
  3. Log cooking temperatures at the point of service, not before. Take the reading from the thickest part of the item, away from bone, immediately before it leaves the kitchen.
  4. Check hot and cold holding every two to four hours during active buffet or banquet service, since holding equipment can drift once doors are opened repeatedly.
  5. Record the corrective action immediately if any reading falls outside the target range — don't wait until end of shift to remember what was done.
  6. Have a supervisor review and sign off at the end of each day, then file the printed sheet with your HACCP records.

Understanding the Danger Zone

The temperature danger zone — 5°C to 60°C (41°F to 140°F) — is the range in which bacteria such as Salmonella, E. coli, and Listeria multiply fastest. Under ideal conditions, bacterial populations can double in as little as 20 minutes within this range. That is why most food safety systems apply a strict "2-hour / 4-hour rule": food left in the danger zone for under 2 hours (cumulative, not per instance) can usually be returned to safe temperature or used immediately; between 2 and 4 hours it must be used immediately with no further temperature abuse; beyond 4 hours it should be discarded. Cumulative means every minute the item has spent in the danger zone across its whole life in your kitchen — from delivery, to storage, to prep, to service — adds up toward that limit.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should kitchen temperatures be logged?

Refrigeration and freezers at least twice daily; cooking and reheating every time a high-risk item is prepared; hot/cold holding every two to four hours during active service.

What temperature range is considered the danger zone?

5°C to 60°C (41°F to 140°F). Food should not spend more than a combined two hours in this range before it is discarded.

Does a temperature log replace a certified HACCP plan?

No. This log sheet supports a HACCP plan by documenting checks at critical control points, but it does not replace a certified plan or your local health authority's specific requirements.

Who should be responsible for filling in the temperature log?

A trained team member on each shift — typically a chef de partie, sous chef, or shift supervisor — with the outlet manager or executive chef reviewing and signing off daily.

What should happen if a reading falls outside the safe range?

Record it immediately, take corrective action (adjust the equipment, move stock, or discard affected food), note the action taken, and escalate to a supervisor if it recurs.
