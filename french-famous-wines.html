Wine & Beverage Knowledge Series

France Famous Wines

A legacy in every sip: ten famous French wines, their grapes, regions, tasting notes and how to serve them.

Why French wine still sets the reference point

France is one of the largest wine producers in the world, but its real influence is not about volume. It is about reference. When a winemaker in Chile, Australia or Maharashtra describes a wine as Bordeaux-style, Burgundian or Champagne-method, they are borrowing a vocabulary that French regions spent centuries defining. For a hotel or restaurant professional, understanding that vocabulary is not a luxury. It is the foundation for writing a wine list, training a service team and advising a guest with confidence.

This guide follows the ten wines on the poster below: Château Margaux, Château Lafite Rothschild, Dom Pérignon, Moët & Chandon, Chablis, Côtes du Rhône, Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Sancerre, Alsace Riesling and Sauternes. Between them they cover the major French styles: structured Bordeaux reds, prestige and house Champagne, crisp white wines from Burgundy, the Loire and Alsace, warm-climate Rhône reds and one of the world's great sweet wines. Learn these ten and you can place most of what a guest will ask about on a French list.

The information here is written from an operations point of view. Alongside grapes, regions and tasting notes, each wine includes practical service guidance: serving temperature, glassware and food pairing. Details such as blends and styles can vary by producer and vintage, so always check the label and the producer's technical sheet before you train your team on a specific bottle.

How to read a French wine label

The first thing to understand about French wine is that most labels name a place, not a grape. A bottle of Sancerre does not say Sauvignon Blanc on the front, because the appellation itself tells an informed buyer what to expect. A bottle of Châteauneuf-du-Pape does not list Grenache, Syrah or Mourvèdre. The place name is the promise. This is the opposite of many New World labels, and it is the single biggest reason French wine can feel intimidating to guests and to new staff.

The system behind it is the Appellation d'Origine Contrôlée, usually shortened to AOC, which in the European Union framework is now also described as AOP. An appellation defines the area where the grapes must be grown, which varieties are permitted, how the vines are managed and, in many cases, the minimum alcohol level and the winemaking rules. Within an appellation there are often further tiers. In Burgundy and Chablis the words Premier Cru and Grand Cru refer to specific vineyard sites. In Bordeaux, classifications such as the famous 1855 ranking of Médoc châteaux refer to estates rather than plots of land. In Alsace, Grand Cru identifies named vineyards. The same words mean different things in different regions, which is why a service team needs to learn them region by region.

A practical habit for any manager is to teach staff to read a label in a fixed order: the producer or château, the appellation, the vintage, the classification if any, and only then the extras. That sequence turns a confusing label into five quick facts a server can repeat at the table.

Château Margaux

Bordeaux, Margaux. Red.

  • Grapes: Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Cabernet Franc, Petit Verdot
  • Region: Margaux (Bordeaux)
  • Tasting notes: Elegant, complex, silky tannins, black fruit, floral, long finish

Château Margaux is one of the best-known names in the wine world and one of the original first growths of the 1855 Bordeaux classification, along with Lafite, Latour and Haut-Brion. Mouton Rothschild joined that top group in 1973. The estate sits on the Left Bank of the Gironde in the commune of Margaux, in the Médoc, where gravelly soils suit Cabernet Sauvignon.

What distinguishes Margaux within Bordeaux is its character. The Margaux appellation is generally associated with perfume and finesse rather than raw power, which is why tasters often describe floral notes alongside blackcurrant and dark berry fruit, with tannins that feel silky rather than severe. The blend is led by Cabernet Sauvignon, with Merlot adding roundness, Cabernet Franc adding lift and Petit Verdot contributing colour and spice in small amounts.

For a hospitality manager, a wine at this level is usually a special-occasion bottle with a high price and a long list of caveats: vintage, storage history and provenance all matter. The more useful teaching point for staff is the appellation. Guests who like the style can often be introduced to other wines from the Margaux appellation or to well-made Médoc blends at much friendlier prices.

Service and pairing: Serve at about 16 to 18°C in a large red wine glass. Older vintages should be handled gently and decanted only to separate sediment, while younger wines benefit from an hour or two of air. It pairs naturally with roast lamb, beef, duck and mild aged cheeses.

Château Lafite Rothschild

Bordeaux, Pauillac. Red.

  • Grapes: Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Cabernet Franc
  • Region: Pauillac (Bordeaux)
  • Tasting notes: Powerful, structured, rich fruit, excellent aging potential

Lafite Rothschild is the other great Left Bank name on the poster, from the neighbouring Pauillac appellation. Like Margaux it was a first growth in 1855, and it has been owned by the Rothschild family since the 1860s. Pauillac as a whole is known for firm structure and the aromas of cedar, pencil shavings and blackcurrant that Cabernet Sauvignon develops with age, and Lafite is often described as the most refined and restrained expression of that style.

The practical lesson in comparing Lafite and Margaux is how two famous neighbours differ. Margaux tends to emphasise perfume and a silky texture. Pauillac tends to emphasise structure, depth and longevity. Both are built on Cabernet Sauvignon, and both depend heavily on the vintage, which is why a service team should never assume that two bottles of the same château taste alike.

Wines like this are built for ageing. The poster's reference to excellent aging potential is not marketing language. In strong vintages the tannins and acidity allow these wines to develop for decades. That also means a young bottle on a list may be several years away from its best, and a good sommelier or manager will say so honestly when advising a guest.

Service and pairing: Serve at 16 to 18°C and, for a young bottle, decant an hour or two before service. Classic matches are roast beef, rack of lamb, venison and hard aged cheese.

Dom Pérignon

Champagne. Prestige sparkling.

  • Grapes: Chardonnay, Pinot Noir
  • Region: Champagne
  • Tasting notes: Fine bubbles, crisp, fresh, complex, notes of citrus, brioche

Dom Pérignon is the prestige cuvée of the Champagne house Moët & Chandon, part of the Moët Hennessy group, and it carries the name of a Benedictine monk who was cellar master at the abbey of Hautvillers in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. It is made as a vintage Champagne, which means the wine is produced only from the grapes of a single declared year rather than blended across years.

All Champagne is made by the traditional method. A base wine is made first, then bottled with a small addition of yeast and sugar so a second fermentation takes place inside the bottle, which creates the bubbles. The wine is then aged on its lees, the spent yeast, before disgorgement. That extended ageing is the source of the toasty, brioche and biscuit notes the poster mentions, sitting beside citrus freshness and a very fine bead of bubbles. Chardonnay and Pinot Noir are the two grapes in this blend, bringing freshness and structure respectively.

For service teams, the key point is value: a prestige cuvée is a celebration wine and guests usually order it for an occasion. Staff should be able to explain, in a sentence, why it costs more than a non-vintage Champagne: single-vintage selection, long ageing and smaller production.

Service and pairing: Serve at about 8 to 10°C in a tulip-shaped glass rather than a wide coupe, so aromas and bubbles are preserved. Open the bottle slowly with a towel, holding it at an angle. Pair with oysters, caviar, sushi, roast chicken and aged hard cheese.

Moët & Chandon

Champagne. House sparkling.

  • Grapes: Chardonnay, Pinot Noir, Meunier
  • Region: Champagne
  • Tasting notes: Fresh, elegant, fruity, with fine bubbles and a smooth finish

Moët & Chandon was founded in 1743 and is one of the best-known Champagne houses in the world. The Impérial Brut on the poster is the house's flagship non-vintage wine, blended from Pinot Noir, Meunier and Chardonnay across several harvests so that the style stays consistent from one year to the next. Consistency is the entire point of a non-vintage blend: reserve wines from earlier years are used to match the house style.

The three-grape blend explains the profile. Pinot Noir adds body and red-fruit character, Meunier adds roundness and fruitiness, and Chardonnay adds freshness and finesse. The result is the fresh, elegant, fruity wine with a smooth finish that the poster describes. Brut is a style label that indicates a dry wine with a low level of added sugar, under 12 grams per litre.

This is a wine that works hard on a hotel menu. It is a reliable by-the-glass pour for receptions, weddings, brunches and banquets, and an easy first recommendation for guests who want Champagne but have no preferred house.

Service and pairing: Serve well chilled at 6 to 8°C. Pair with canapés, smoked salmon, salty snacks, fried foods and light seafood. For banquet service, pre-chill bottles properly in an ice and water bucket rather than relying on ice alone.

Chablis

Burgundy, Chablis. Dry white.

  • Grapes: Chardonnay
  • Region: Chablis (Burgundy)
  • Tasting notes: Crisp, mineral, light-bodied, citrus, green apple, refreshing acidity

Chablis is the northernmost part of Burgundy and one of the cleanest illustrations of how place shapes a grape. Chardonnay here grows in a cool climate on soils rich in ancient marine limestone, which is why tasters talk about mineral, almost steely character. The wine is typically lean and bright, with citrus and green apple fruit and refreshing acidity, rather than the rich, buttery profile many people associate with Chardonnay from warmer regions.

The appellation has four levels: Petit Chablis, Chablis, Chablis Premier Cru and Chablis Grand Cru. Quality and price rise with the vineyard site. The poster's example, from the producer William Fèvre, shows the standard Chablis tier that most restaurants can stock by the glass. Many Chablis wines are made without heavy oak, which keeps them crisp and mineral, though some producers use oak for their top cuvées.

If you want to go further into the top tier, the Grand Cru vineyards are covered in detail in the guide to the 33 Grand Crus of Burgundy linked at the bottom of this page. For daily service, Chablis is one of the easiest white wines to sell, because its style is easy to describe in two words: crisp and mineral.

Service and pairing: Serve at 8 to 10°C in a medium white wine glass. It is classic with oysters, shellfish, grilled white fish, sushi and soft goat cheese.

Côtes du Rhône

Southern Rhône Valley. Red.

  • Grapes: Grenache, Syrah, Mourvèdre
  • Region: Rhône Valley
  • Tasting notes: Spicy, fruity, medium-bodied, smooth with earthy notes

Côtes du Rhône is the wide, accessible appellation of the Rhône Valley and one of the best places for guests to start with French red wine. The wines are most often blends led by Grenache, with Syrah and Mourvèdre adding colour, spice and structure. The warm Mediterranean-influenced climate gives ripe fruit, and the style is generally medium-bodied, fruity and smooth, with the earthy and peppery notes that the poster highlights.

The appellation covers a large area, so quality and style vary by producer. There are also higher-tier designations such as Côtes du Rhône Villages and named villages, which sit above the basic level. For a manager building a list, a well-chosen Côtes du Rhône is valuable because it gives guests a recognisably French wine at an approachable price, and it works across a wide range of dishes.

This is also an easy wine for staff training, because it teaches the idea of a blend. Instead of one dominant grape, the winemaker balances several, each contributing something different.

Service and pairing: Serve at 15 to 17°C, slightly cool for a red. It pairs with grilled meats, charcuterie, roasted vegetables, pizza, stews and lightly spiced dishes.

Châteauneuf-du-Pape

Southern Rhône Valley. Red.

  • Grapes: Grenache, Syrah, Mourvèdre, Cinsault
  • Region: Châteauneuf-du-Pape
  • Tasting notes: Rich, full-bodied, complex, notes of red fruit, spice and herbs

Châteauneuf-du-Pape takes its name from the fourteenth-century papal presence near Avignon, and it is one of the best-known appellations in the Rhône. It was among the first French appellations to be formally defined in the 1930s. The vineyards are famous for large, rounded stones on the surface, which store heat during the day and release it at night, helping the grapes ripen fully.

The appellation permits a long list of grape varieties, up to thirteen when counting by variety and eighteen when colour variants are included, although Grenache usually dominates, supported by Syrah and Mourvèdre, with Cinsault and others in smaller roles. The resulting wines are rich and full-bodied, often high in alcohol, with red fruit, spice and the dried herb notes often called garrigue after the scrubland of southern France. Many bottles carry an embossed papal coat of arms on the glass.

For service, this is a wine with power. It needs substantial food and some air, and staff should warn guests that it can be high in alcohol and generous in body. It is a sound recommendation for guests who enjoy big red wines but want something different from Bordeaux.

Service and pairing: Serve at 16 to 18°C and decant young bottles for an hour. It matches roast lamb, game, slow-braised beef, mushrooms and hard cheeses.

Sancerre

Loire Valley. Dry white.

  • Grapes: Sauvignon Blanc
  • Region: Sancerre (Loire Valley)
  • Tasting notes: Crisp, fresh, mineral, citrus, green apple, herbal notes

Sancerre sits in the upper Loire Valley and is the reference point for French Sauvignon Blanc. The vineyards are planted on a mix of limestone, flinty and clay soils, and the cool climate produces wines that are crisp, fresh and mineral, with citrus, green apple and the herbal edge that Sauvignon Blanc is known for. Compared with warmer-climate versions, Sancerre tends to be more restrained and more focused.

A useful teaching point: the appellation also produces red and rosé wines from Pinot Noir, though white Sauvignon Blanc is what most guests mean when they order Sancerre. The name alone has become a shorthand for a style, and that makes it a dependable by-the-glass choice. It is one of the wines most frequently requested by guests who say they like something dry, fresh and not oaky.

The village of Chavignol, within the Sancerre area, is also the home of the Crottin de Chavignol goat cheese, and the regional pairing of Sancerre and goat cheese is one of the best-known in French cuisine.

Service and pairing: Serve at 8 to 10°C. It is excellent with goat cheese, shellfish, salads, grilled fish and light vegetable dishes.

Riesling, Alsace

Alsace. Aromatic white.

  • Grapes: Riesling
  • Region: Alsace
  • Tasting notes: Aromatic, crisp, dry, floral, citrus, excellent acidity

Alsace lies in north-eastern France along the Rhine, protected from rain by the Vosges mountains, which makes it one of the driest regions in the country. Unlike most French regions, Alsace labels its wines by grape variety, and Riesling is its most celebrated white. The wines are usually dry, with floral and citrus aromatics and a backbone of high acidity, and they are bottled in the tall, slender flute-shaped bottle associated with the region.

Alsace Riesling also has a quality tier: the region has a number of Grand Cru vineyards, and late-harvest styles such as Vendange Tardive and Sélection de Grains Nobles are made as sweet wines. The producer Trimbach, featured on the poster, is one of the region's best-known names.

For anyone working in a hospitality market where guests love spicy food, Riesling deserves attention. The combination of aromatic fruit, acidity and sometimes a hint of residual sweetness makes dry or off-dry Riesling one of the most flexible wines with lightly spiced Indian, Thai and other Asian dishes, where heavier reds often struggle.

Service and pairing: Serve at 8 to 10°C in a narrow-rimmed white wine glass. Pair with choucroute, smoked fish, pork, spiced seafood and Asian-influenced cuisine.

Sauternes

Bordeaux, Sauternes. Sweet white.

  • Grapes: Sémillon, Sauvignon Blanc
  • Region: Sauternes (Bordeaux)
  • Tasting notes: Sweet, rich, honey, apricot, exotic fruit, long and luxurious finish

Sauternes is one of the world's great sweet wines and a rare example of a famous wine that depends on a fungus. In the autumn, mist from the cold Ciron river meets the warmer Garonne, creating conditions in which a mould called Botrytis cinerea, known as noble rot, can develop on the grapes. The mould concentrates sugar, acidity and flavour. The grapes are usually picked in several passes through the vineyard, selecting only the affected berries, which keeps yields low and costs high.

The main grapes are Sémillon, which brings richness and texture, and Sauvignon Blanc, which brings freshness, often with a small share of Muscadelle. The result is a wine with honey, apricot, orange peel and exotic fruit flavours, balanced by enough acidity to keep it from feeling heavy. In the 1855 classification, Château d'Yquem, the estate shown on the poster, was placed in its own top category above the other Sauternes properties.

Because of its price and richness, Sauternes is often sold in half bottles of 375 ml, which lets guests order a glass or a shared bottle with dessert without committing to a full bottle. That makes it an excellent candidate for a dessert wine upsell.

Service and pairing: Serve well chilled at 8 to 10°C. It is classic with foie gras, blue cheese such as Roquefort, tarte tatin, crème brûlée and apricot or almond desserts.

Serving French wine properly

A great wine served badly is a poor experience, and wine is one of the easiest products in a restaurant to damage through carelessness. Temperature is the most common failure. A red served warm tastes flat and alcoholic. A white or Champagne served too cold loses its aroma. Use the table below as a baseline, and remember that these are guides rather than rules: a very old red or a very light white may need slightly different handling.

Glassware matters because shape controls aroma. Large red wine glasses give full-bodied reds room to open up. Narrower white wine glasses keep aromas concentrated and temperature stable. Champagne is better in a tulip-shaped glass than a wide coupe. Keep glasses clean and free of detergent residue or cloth lint, because both interfere with aroma and with the bubbles in sparkling wine.

Decanting serves two purposes: separating older wine from its sediment and giving young, tight wines some air. Not every bottle needs it. As a rule, decant mature bottles gently just before service, and give young structured reds such as Bordeaux and Châteauneuf-du-Pape an hour or so. Sparkling wines are not decanted.

Pairing French wine with the menu

Matching wine with food is easier when you match weight with weight and acidity with richness. Full-bodied reds such as Margaux, Lafite and Châteauneuf-du-Pape need substantial dishes like roast lamb, beef and game. Medium reds such as Côtes du Rhône handle grilled meats, pizza and spiced dishes without overwhelming them. Crisp whites such as Chablis, Sancerre and dry Riesling are natural with shellfish, grilled fish, salads and goat cheese. Champagne is the most flexible of all, working with salty and fried foods as well as celebration dishes. Sweet wines such as Sauternes should be at least as sweet as the dessert they accompany, or the wine will taste thin.

Wine list tips for managers

A French section on a wine list works best when it is organised for guests rather than for the cellar. Group wines by style and price, give each one a short, plain-language description and avoid long strings of French terms that nobody at the table can pronounce. A good description does two things: it tells the guest what the wine tastes like and it suggests what to eat with it.

Offer a small number of French wines by the glass, and choose them for contrast: a Champagne or sparkling wine, a crisp white such as Chablis or Sancerre, an aromatic option such as Riesling, and a medium-bodied red such as Côtes du Rhône. Prestige wines like Margaux and Lafite generally sell by the bottle, and they need careful storage and an honest description of vintage and readiness.

Train the team on pronunciation as well as content, because hesitation at the table undermines confidence. Useful anchors are Chablis (sha-BLEE), Sancerre (sahn-SAIR), Sauternes (so-TERN), Châteauneuf-du-Pape (shah-toe-NUF doo PAHP) and Pérignon (pay-ree-NYOHN). A two-minute pronunciation drill in the pre-shift briefing builds more confidence than an hour of theory.

Finally, store wine properly: cool, steady temperatures around 12 to 15°C where possible, away from direct light and vibration, with cork-sealed bottles lying on their sides so the cork stays moist. Track stock and rotate it, and record the cost of each bottle so pricing decisions are based on real numbers. Many operators target a beverage cost percentage in the range of 25 to 35 percent for wine, but the right figure depends on the concept, location and positioning of the property.

Frequently asked questions

What does grand cru mean in French wine?

It depends on the region. In Burgundy and Chablis it is the top tier of named vineyards. In Alsace it also refers to specific classified vineyards. In Bordeaux the classification system ranks estates, so the term means something different. Always check the regional rules.

Why don't most French labels show the grape?

Because the appellation, such as Sancerre or Chablis, already defines which grapes are used. Alsace is the main exception, where the grape name is shown.

Can sparkling wine only be called Champagne if it comes from Champagne?

Yes. The name Champagne is protected and applies only to sparkling wine made in the Champagne region under its production rules. Other French sparkling wines have different names, such as Crémant.

Do I need to decant every red wine?

No. Decant older wines to remove sediment and young, structured reds to soften them with air. Light or delicate wines often do not need it.

How long does an opened bottle last?

As a general guide, sparkling wine with a proper stopper keeps for one to three days, still white wines for three to five days in the fridge, and red wines for three to four days. Sweet wines such as Sauternes often keep longer in the fridge because of their sugar and acidity.

Final thoughts

French wine looks complicated, but it becomes manageable when you treat it as a set of places and styles. Bordeaux gives structure and ageing potential. Champagne gives celebration and consistency. Chablis, Sancerre and Alsace give freshness and precision. The Rhône gives warmth and spice, and Sauternes gives luxury at the end of a meal. Once a team understands those ten reference points, the rest of the French list starts to make sense.
