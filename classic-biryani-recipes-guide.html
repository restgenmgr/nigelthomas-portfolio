Classic Biryani Recipes: 16 Traditional Biryanis, the Dum Method and a Kitchen Training Guide

By Nigel A. Thomas, Hospitality Executive • Food & Recipes Series • October 2026

Few dishes carry as much pride as biryani. It is a celebration dish, a restaurant bestseller, a hotel banquet centrepiece and a staple of home kitchens across India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and far beyond. Yet it is also one of the easiest dishes to get wrong. Rice that turns mushy, meat that stays tough, masala that burns at the bottom, and a pot that arrives at the table with no aroma at all: every cook has seen it.

This guide brings together 16 classic biryanis, from the everyday chicken and mutton biryani to Hyderabadi, Lucknowi, Kolkata, Sindhi, Malabar, Dhaka Kacchi, Afghani, Chicken Tikka and Paneer. Each has its ingredient list and a clear method, taken from a set of training sheets, with kitchen notes added for chefs, cooks and F&B trainers. A black and gold poster with all 16 recipes sits at the bottom of the page to print for your kitchen.

The aim is not to argue about whose biryani is the original. Every region has a loyal following and a strong opinion. The aim is to give your team a reliable framework so that whichever biryani is on the menu, it comes out of the pot the same way every time.

What Makes a Biryani a Biryani

At its simplest, biryani is rice and a spiced main ingredient, layered together and finished by dum: sealed, slow steam cooking in which the pot cooks the food in its own moisture. That sealing step is the soul of the dish. It lets the rice absorb the steam of the meat, the masala and the aromatics, and it is why a good biryani smells so strong the moment the lid lifts.

Three things separate biryani from a simple pilaf or a rice-and-curry plate. First, the rice is only partly cooked before layering, so it finishes inside the sealed pot. Second, the main ingredient is spiced and usually marinated in yogurt. Third, the layers are built deliberately, often with fried onion, herbs, saffron or whole spices between them. Get those three right and the recipe details are variations on a theme.

The Building Blocks

Basmati rice. All but one of the recipes here call for 400 g of basmati rice; the Malabar biryani uses jeerakasala, a short-grain aromatic rice from Kerala. Wash the rice until the water runs clear, soak it for a short time, and parboil it in well-salted water. The chicken sheet sets the standard: layer with rice that is about 70% cooked. It will finish in the pot, and if you cook it fully first, it will collapse into paste.

Yogurt. Yogurt tenderises meat, balances heat and gives the masala body. The recipes use 120 to 200 g. Use thick, plain, unsweetened yogurt at room temperature or lightly chilled, and whisk it smooth before adding spices so it does not split in the heat.

Onion. Onions appear two ways: cooked into the masala (the standard 120–150 g), and fried until deep golden for layering and garnish. The Hyderabadi and Dhaka Kacchi sheets call for 100 g of fried onion. Fry slowly and evenly; pale onions give no flavour and burnt onions make the whole pot bitter.

Ginger-garlic paste and biryani masala. Fresh paste (15 to 20 g per batch here) and a blend of 10 to 12 g of biryani masala carry the spice. Where a recipe lists whole spices instead, such as Lucknowi and Afghani at 10 g, they flavour the stock and the rice rather than the masala itself.

Saffron milk. Saffron steeped in warm milk is sprinkled over the top layer of Lucknowi, Kolkata and Hyderabadi biryanis. It gives the rice its yellow-orange streaks and a floral aroma, and a little goes a long way.

Fresh herbs. Mint and coriander are layered in or scattered on top. Add them fresh at the end of each layer so they perfume the steam instead of turning dark and bitter.

The seal. Dum only works if steam stays inside. Cover with a tight lid and seal the rim with dough, a damp cloth or foil. Start on medium heat to build steam, then drop to the lowest flame, or use a tawa or heat diffuser under the pot so the base does not scorch.

Two Styles: Cooked Meat vs Raw (Kacchi) Layering

The 16 recipes fall into two broad styles. In the pakki or cooked-meat style, the meat or vegetables are cooked into a masala first, then layered with parboiled rice and finished with dum. Mutton, beef, Lucknowi, Kolkata, Sindhi, Malabar, Afghani, Chicken Tikka and vegetable biryanis follow this path. It is more forgiving, easier to hold and easier to scale in a commercial kitchen.

In the kacchi or raw style, marinated raw meat goes into the pot underneath the rice and cooks entirely during dum. The Hyderabadi biryani, with its raw marinated chicken, and the Dhaka Kacchi biryani, with raw mutton and potatoes sealed for 60 to 75 minutes, are the examples in this guide. It gives remarkable flavour, but it demands exact timing, good marinade time and a heavy, tightly sealed pot. Teach the pakki method first, and the kacchi method once your cooks are confident with dum.

Equipment and Mise en Place

A heavy-bottomed handi, degchi or thick stockpot distributes heat evenly and protects the bottom layer. Add a tight lid, dough or foil for sealing, a tawa or flame tamer, a large wide pot for parboiling rice, a perforated spoon for draining, and a wide skimmer for the fried onions.

Before service, wash and soak rice, marinate proteins in advance, prepare ginger-garlic paste, fry the onions, steep the saffron, and wash and chop herbs. Biryani is a mise en place dish. If the components are ready, the assembly takes minutes and the dum does the rest.

Food Safety in the Biryani Kitchen

Biryani combines raw meat marinades, a large batch of rice and long holding times, which makes discipline essential. Keep raw meat, poultry, fish and eggs chilled, marinate in covered containers under refrigeration, and use separate boards and knives. Cook poultry to a safe core temperature of 74°C (165°F) or your local standard, and probe a few pieces in the pot rather than guessing.

Cooked rice is a known risk if it sits warm. Hold biryani hot, above 60°C or as your local code requires, cool leftovers quickly in shallow trays, refrigerate promptly, and reheat until piping hot throughout. Label allergens clearly: dairy (yogurt, ghee, paneer), tree nuts (cashews, almonds), eggs, fish and shellfish all appear in this list. Menu rules on beef and pork vary by region and guest, so check local regulations and guest dietary and religious needs before service.

The 16 Classic Biryani Recipes

The ingredient quantities below are as given on the training sheets. Where a recipe lists a flavouring such as mint, coriander or saffron without a weight, it is added to taste.

01. Chicken Biryani

500 g chicken • 400 g basmati rice • 150 g yogurt • 150 g onion • 20 g ginger-garlic paste • 10 g biryani masala

Method: Marinate chicken. Fry onions, cook chicken and layer with 70% cooked rice. Cover and dum-cook 25 minutes. The benchmark recipe for training. Chicken cooks quickly, so the 25-minute dum is enough, and anything longer risks dry meat.

02. Mutton Biryani

500 g mutton • 400 g basmati rice • 150 g yogurt • 150 g onion • 20 g ginger-garlic paste • 12 g biryani masala

Method: Marinate and slow-cook mutton until tender. Layer with parboiled rice and dum-cook 30 minutes. Mutton needs the tenderising step before it ever meets the rice, since dum will not soften tough meat. A touch more masala (12 g) stands up to the stronger flavour.

03. Hyderabadi Biryani

500 g chicken • 400 g basmati rice • 150 g yogurt • 100 g fried onion • 15 g green chilli • mint, coriander and saffron

Method: Marinate chicken with spices. Layer raw marinated chicken with parboiled rice and dum-cook 35–40 minutes. A kacchi-style biryani: the chicken cooks from raw under the rice. Marinate well ahead and seal tightly, because the dum time is longer than the cooked-meat versions.

04. Vegetable Biryani

400 g basmati rice • 400 g mixed vegetables • 120 g yogurt • 120 g onion • 15 g ginger-garlic paste • 10 g biryani masala

Method: Cook vegetables with yogurt and spices. Layer with parboiled rice and dum-cook 20 minutes. Cut the vegetables to a similar size and cook them only until just tender, since they continue to soften under dum. This is the main vegetarian offering for banquets.

05. Beef Biryani

500 g beef • 400 g basmati rice • 150 g yogurt • 150 g onion • 20 g ginger-garlic paste • 12 g biryani masala

Method: Marinate beef and cook until tender. Layer with parboiled rice, cover tightly and dum-cook 30 minutes. Like mutton, beef needs its slow cooking first. Serve only where local rules and guest preferences allow.

06. Fish Biryani

500 g firm fish fillet • 400 g basmati rice • 120 g yogurt • 120 g onion • 15 g ginger-garlic paste • 10 g biryani masala

Method: Marinate and lightly fry fish. Layer gently with spiced parboiled rice and dum-cook 15–20 minutes. Use a firm fillet that holds its shape, and layer gently so the pieces do not break. Fish overcooks easily, so keep the dum short.

07. Prawn Biryani

500 g large prawns • 400 g basmati rice • 120 g yogurt • 120 g onion • 15 g ginger-garlic paste • 10 g biryani masala

Method: Sauté marinated prawns briefly. Layer with parboiled rice and dum-cook 15 minutes. The shortest dum on the list. Prawns turn rubbery fast, so sear them just until they change colour and let the steam finish them.

08. Egg Biryani

6 boiled eggs • 400 g basmati rice • 120 g yogurt • 150 g onion • 15 g ginger-garlic paste • 10 g biryani masala

Method: Fry boiled eggs lightly. Prepare spiced masala, layer with rice and eggs, then dum-cook 20 minutes. Lightly frying the eggs gives them a golden skin that holds the masala. A budget-friendly, high-margin option for lunch menus.

09. Lucknowi Biryani

500 g mutton • 400 g basmati rice • 150 g yogurt • 120 g onion • 10 g whole spices • saffron milk

Method: Cook aromatic mutton yakhni until tender. Layer with parboiled rice and saffron milk, then dum-cook 25 minutes. Yakhni is a fragrant stock-based cooking style, and the whole spices rather than a ready masala give this biryani a gentler, more perfumed profile.

10. Kolkata Biryani

500 g chicken • 400 g basmati rice • 4 potatoes • 4 boiled eggs • 120 g yogurt • 10 g biryani masala

Method: Cook chicken and potatoes in mild masala. Layer with rice, eggs and saffron milk, then dum-cook 25 minutes. The potato and egg are signature elements. The masala stays mild so the saffron and the soft potato come through.

11. Sindhi Biryani

500 g mutton • 400 g basmati rice • 200 g yogurt • 200 g tomato • 150 g potato • mint, chilli and dried plum

Method: Cook spicy mutton masala with tomato and potato. Layer with parboiled rice and dum-cook 25–30 minutes. The tangy, spicy character comes from the tomato, yogurt and dried plum. This is the saucier, more assertive biryani on the list.

12. Malabar Biryani

500 g chicken • 400 g jeerakasala rice • 150 g yogurt • 150 g onion • 40 g cashews and raisins • mint and coriander

Method: Cook gently spiced chicken. Layer with aromatic rice, fried onion, nuts and raisins; dum-cook 20 minutes. Jeerakasala is a small, fragrant Kerala rice, so do not substitute basmati without noting it. The nuts and raisins add sweetness against a gently spiced chicken.

13. Dhaka Kacchi Biryani

500 g mutton • 400 g basmati rice • 300 g potatoes • 150 g yogurt • 100 g fried onion • 12 g biryani masala

Method: Marinate raw mutton with yogurt and spices. Layer with potatoes and parboiled rice, seal and dum-cook 60–75 minutes. The longest dum in the guide, and a true kacchi biryani. Potatoes sit under the rice and absorb the juices. Use a heavy pot and a perfect seal.

14. Afghani Biryani

500 g lamb • 400 g basmati rice • 150 g carrot • 80 g raisins • 50 g almonds • 10 g whole spices

Method: Cook tender spiced lamb. Layer with rice and garnish with caramelized carrot, raisins and almonds. A sweeter, milder style. Caramelise the carrots separately so they are glossy, and add the raisins and almonds at the end for colour and crunch.

15. Chicken Tikka Biryani

500 g chicken tikka • 400 g basmati rice • 150 g yogurt • 150 g onion • 150 g tomato • 10 g biryani masala

Method: Grill marinated chicken tikka. Prepare masala, layer with rice and tikka, then dum-cook 20 minutes. A good use of tandoor or grill production: the smoky tikka sits in the masala-rice layers. Do not overcook the tikka before layering.

16. Paneer Biryani

400 g paneer • 400 g basmati rice • 150 g yogurt • 150 g mixed vegetables • 120 g onion • 10 g biryani masala

Method: Marinate and lightly sear paneer. Cook vegetable masala, layer with rice and paneer, then dum-cook 20 minutes. Searing gives the paneer a crust so it does not crumble. A premium vegetarian option that pairs well with raita.

Common Biryani Problems and How to Fix Them

Mushy or broken rice. The rice was parboiled too far, stirred too hard or layered while still wet. Stop at about 70%, drain well, and spread the rice gently with a spoon instead of pressing it down. Basmati grains should stay long and separate.

Hard, raw rice on top. Steam escaped. Check the seal, use a heavier lid, and make sure the parboiling water was properly salted and the rice not undercooked beyond what dum can correct.

Scorched bottom layer. The heat was too high or the pot too thin. Use a heavy-bottomed pot, place a tawa or flame tamer underneath, and drop to the lowest flame once the steam begins.

Dry meat. Chicken, fish and prawns cook fast, so long dum times dry them out. Respect the short times on the recipe cards: 15 minutes for prawns, 15 to 20 for fish, 20 to 25 for chicken. Tough meats like mutton and beef need their slow cooking before layering, not extra dum afterwards.

Flat, weak aroma. Old whole spices, pale onions or herbs added too early. Replace spices regularly, fry onions to a deep golden colour, and add mint, coriander and saffron milk between the layers just before sealing.

Watery masala. Excess water from vegetables, tomatoes or yogurt. Cook the masala until the oil separates and it clings to the meat before it meets the rice. A runny masala turns the rice soggy from below.

Scaling for Banquets, Hotels and Cloud Kitchens

Every recipe above uses roughly 400 to 500 g of main ingredient and 400 g of rice, which makes the proportions easy to scale. Multiply the whole list evenly, but do not assume the cooking time scales with it. A bigger pot holds more heat and steam, so keep the dum time close to the recipe and check a sample before service. Use several medium pots rather than one very large one, so the layers stay even and the bottom does not burn.

In a hotel banquet, cook the masala and parboil the rice in advance, assemble in the final pots and dum on the day. In a cloud kitchen, pre-portion the marinated proteins and fried onions, and keep rice washed and ready, so a pot can be assembled in minutes from a standing mise en place. Write each recipe on a card with weights, dum time and portion size, and have every cook follow it.

Training Tips for the Team

Five rules for every biryani

1. Parboil the rice to about 70% and no further.
2. Marinate in advance, and tenderise tough meats before layering.
3. Fry onions slowly to an even golden colour.
4. Seal the pot tightly and use low heat for dum.
5. Rest the pot for a few minutes before opening, then fluff gently from the edge.

Train in stages. Start with the chicken biryani until every cook can parboil, layer and seal confidently. Then move to a long-cooked meat such as mutton, then to a kacchi version, and finally to the regional specials. Have trainees taste at each stage: rice before layering, masala before layering and the finished biryani, so they learn what “70% cooked” and “well seasoned” taste like.

For service, serve with raita, a salad of onion and cucumber, and a wedge of lemon. Present from the pot where possible; opening a sealed handi at the table is part of what guests are paying for. Portion consistently, and record the portion weight on your recipe card so cost stays predictable.
