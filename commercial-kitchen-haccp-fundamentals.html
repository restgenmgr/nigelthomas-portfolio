HACCP Fundamentals

A practical guide to Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points for commercial kitchens, restaurants, hotels, catering operations and food service professionals.

Prepared by Nigel A. Thomas • Commercial Kitchen Operations Academy

1. What Is HACCP?

HACCP stands for Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points. It is a systematic approach to identifying, evaluating and controlling food-safety hazards before they result in unsafe food reaching the guest.

HACCP is not simply a form, checklist or inspection exercise. It is a management system built around understanding the food process, identifying where hazards can occur, deciding which hazards require control, monitoring those controls and taking corrective action when the process moves outside the established limits.

Manager's principle: Do not wait for the finished dish to be unsafe. Identify where the risk can occur, establish a control and monitor that control consistently.

2. Why HACCP Matters in a Commercial Kitchen

Commercial kitchens handle large quantities of food, multiple ingredients, different cooking methods, refrigeration, storage, preparation and service. A small operational failure can therefore affect many guests.

A properly designed HACCP approach helps the kitchen team move from reacting to food-safety problems toward preventing them.

  • Identifies food-safety hazards systematically.
  • Focuses management attention on significant risks.
  • Creates measurable controls for important process steps.
  • Defines what staff must monitor.
  • Establishes corrective action when controls fail.
  • Creates evidence that food-safety controls are being managed.
  • Supports consistent kitchen operations and staff training.

3. HACCP Is Built on Prerequisite Controls

HACCP works best when the kitchen already has strong basic food-safety practices. These supporting programmes are commonly referred to as prerequisite programmes.

Examples include:

  • Personal hygiene.
  • Handwashing.
  • Cleaning and sanitation.
  • Pest control.
  • Safe water and ice.
  • Equipment maintenance.
  • Waste management.
  • Supplier and receiving controls.
  • Temperature-control procedures.
  • Food storage practices.
  • Allergen management.
  • Staff training.
Important: HACCP does not replace good kitchen hygiene. It builds on a controlled operating environment.

4. The Four Main Categories of Food-Safety Hazards

Biological Hazards

Biological hazards include harmful microorganisms and biological agents that may make food unsafe. Examples can include pathogenic bacteria, viruses, parasites and certain toxins produced by microorganisms.

Chemical Hazards

Chemical hazards may arise from cleaning chemicals, pest-control substances, agricultural residues, inappropriate chemical storage or other contaminants entering food.

Physical Hazards

Physical hazards are unwanted foreign materials that can enter food. Examples include glass, metal, plastic, stones, packaging fragments or damaged equipment parts.

Allergen Hazards

Allergen risks can arise when an allergenic ingredient is intentionally used or when cross-contact occurs through equipment, utensils, surfaces, storage or staff practices.

5. The Seven Principles of HACCP

The HACCP system is commonly structured around seven fundamental principles. These principles provide the framework for developing, operating and reviewing a HACCP plan.

Principle 1

Conduct a Hazard Analysis

Identify hazards that could reasonably occur at each stage of the food process. Evaluate their significance and determine which hazards require control.

Principle 2

Determine Critical Control Points

Identify process steps where control can be applied and is essential to prevent, eliminate or reduce a significant food-safety hazard to an acceptable level.

Principle 3

Establish Critical Limits

Establish measurable or observable criteria that distinguish acceptable operation from unacceptable operation at each critical control point.

Principle 4

Establish Monitoring Procedures

Define what will be monitored, how it will be monitored, when monitoring will occur and who is responsible for performing it.

Principle 5

Establish Corrective Actions

Determine what must happen when monitoring shows that a critical limit or other established control has not been met.

Principle 6

Establish Verification Procedures

Verification confirms that the HACCP system is operating as intended. This may include review of records, observations, audits, calibration checks, testing or other appropriate activities.

Principle 7

Establish Documentation and Record Keeping

Maintain appropriate records demonstrating that monitoring, corrective actions, verification and other HACCP activities have been completed.

6. Understanding the HACCP Flow

A HACCP plan begins with understanding the actual food journey through the operation. A typical commercial kitchen process may look like this:

Receiving → Storage → Preparation → Cooking → Holding → Service → Cooling → Storage/Reheating → Final Service

Not every operation uses every step, and some processes may occur in a different order. The important point is that the HACCP team understands what actually happens in the kitchen rather than creating a theoretical process that does not match real operations.

7. Hazard Analysis in Practice

For each process step, the team should ask:

  1. What food-safety hazard could occur here?
  2. Is the hazard reasonably likely to occur?
  3. How serious could the consequence be?
  4. What control is already in place?
  5. Is additional control required?
  6. Where in the process can the hazard be prevented or reduced?

8. Example Hazard Analysis

Process Step Possible Hazard Control Monitoring
Receiving chilled food Temperature abuse, contamination or damaged packaging Approved supplier and receiving inspection Receiving checks and records
Cold storage Growth of microorganisms or cross-contamination Controlled storage, separation and stock rotation Temperature and storage checks
Preparation Cross-contamination Segregation, hygiene and clean equipment Supervisor observation
Cooking Survival of harmful microorganisms Validated cooking process Process or temperature monitoring
Hot holding Microbial growth Controlled holding conditions Regular monitoring
Service Contamination or temperature abuse Protected food and controlled service Service checks

The exact controls and limits must be established for the specific food, process, equipment and applicable regulatory requirements. The table above is a training example, not a substitute for a site-specific HACCP study.

9. What Is a Critical Control Point?

A Critical Control Point, or CCP, is a step where control is essential for preventing, eliminating or reducing a significant food-safety hazard to an acceptable level.

Not every step in a kitchen is a CCP. A common mistake is to label every food-safety activity as a CCP. HACCP works better when significant control points are clearly identified and monitored.

Ask: If control is lost at this step, can the hazard still be adequately controlled later? The answer helps the HACCP team determine whether the step requires critical control.

10. Critical Limits

A critical limit separates acceptable operation from unacceptable operation at a CCP. Depending on the process, a critical limit may involve a measurable factor such as temperature, time, concentration or another validated criterion.

Critical limits must be appropriate to the food, process, equipment, validated method and applicable requirements. Managers should never invent limits simply because a number appears convenient.

11. Monitoring

A HACCP system is only useful when controls are actually monitored. Monitoring should be practical enough for staff to perform consistently.

A monitoring procedure should clearly identify:

  • What is being checked.
  • How it is checked.
  • When or how frequently it is checked.
  • Who is responsible.
  • Where the result is recorded.
  • What happens when the result is outside the acceptable limit.

12. Corrective Action

Corrective action is the planned response when monitoring identifies a loss of control.

A proper corrective action should address both the immediate food-safety issue and the reason the failure occurred.

Corrective Action Questions
  • What happened?
  • Which food or process was affected?
  • Can the food be safely controlled or must it be isolated?
  • What immediate action is required?
  • Who must be informed?
  • What caused the failure?
  • What prevents the failure from happening again?
  • Was the corrective action recorded?

13. Verification

Verification asks whether the HACCP system is actually working as designed. It is different from routine monitoring.

For example, a cook may monitor a process during production, while a manager may later review the monitoring record, observe the process, confirm equipment performance or conduct an internal audit.

Verification activities may include:

  • Reviewing HACCP records.
  • Observing employees performing procedures.
  • Checking equipment calibration where applicable.
  • Reviewing corrective actions.
  • Internal food-safety inspections.
  • Reviewing supplier performance.
  • Reviewing changes to recipes or processes.

14. Validation

Validation is concerned with whether the chosen control measures are capable of controlling the identified hazard when properly implemented.

For example, if a cooking process is relied upon as a control measure, the operation needs an appropriate basis for showing that the process is capable of achieving the intended food-safety outcome.

Validation should be based on suitable scientific, technical, regulatory or other competent evidence appropriate to the operation.

15. HACCP Records

Records should demonstrate that the food-safety system is being operated, monitored and reviewed.

  • Receiving records.
  • Storage monitoring records.
  • Cooking or process monitoring records where applicable.
  • Holding monitoring records where applicable.
  • Cooling records where applicable.
  • Cleaning and sanitation records.
  • Corrective action records.
  • Verification records.
  • Calibration or equipment checks where applicable.
  • Staff training records.
  • Internal inspection records.
Good record principle: A record should be completed at the time the activity occurs, be understandable, identify the responsible person and provide enough information to support management review.

16. HACCP and Personal Hygiene

Personal hygiene remains one of the foundations of food safety. HACCP cannot compensate for poor hand hygiene, illness reporting failures, incorrect protective clothing or unsafe employee practices.

Kitchen managers should ensure that hygiene procedures are understood, trained, supervised and reinforced every day.

17. HACCP and Cross-Contamination

Cross-contamination can occur when microorganisms, allergens, chemicals or physical contaminants move from one food, surface, utensil, container or person to another.

Controls include:

  • Effective handwashing.
  • Separation of raw and ready-to-eat foods.
  • Correct storage arrangements.
  • Clean and sanitised equipment.
  • Appropriate utensil control.
  • Controlled allergen handling.
  • Effective cleaning procedures.

18. HACCP and Temperature Control

Temperature control can be an important part of food-safety management, but the correct limits depend on the food, process, equipment, applicable requirements and validated procedures.

Kitchen managers should establish documented operational limits based on current applicable requirements and competent food-safety guidance rather than relying on memory or informal kitchen rules.

19. HACCP and Equipment

Equipment performance can directly affect food safety. Refrigeration, freezers, cooking equipment, hot-holding equipment, thermometers and other controls should be maintained and used according to the operation's procedures.

Where monitoring depends on measuring equipment, the operation should have suitable procedures for checking or calibrating that equipment as appropriate.

20. HACCP Team

HACCP should not be developed by one person who does not understand the actual kitchen operation.

A practical HACCP team may include representatives from:

  • Food and beverage operations.
  • Executive or production kitchen.
  • Food safety or quality functions.
  • Purchasing or receiving.
  • Engineering or maintenance where relevant.
  • Housekeeping or sanitation where relevant.
  • Senior management.

The team should understand the actual recipes, ingredients, equipment, processes and service conditions of the operation.

21. HACCP Flow Diagram

A useful flow diagram should represent the real movement of food through the kitchen.

Supplier → Receiving → Storage → Preparation → Cooking/Processing → Holding → Service

Where applicable, additional branches may include cooling, refrigerated storage, reheating and subsequent service.

The HACCP team should walk through the kitchen and verify that the flow diagram matches what actually happens during normal operations.

22. Manager's HACCP Walk-Through

Daily Manager Questions
  • Are receiving controls being followed?
  • Are foods stored correctly and separated?
  • Are date codes and stock rotation controlled?
  • Are preparation areas clean and organised?
  • Are raw and ready-to-eat foods adequately separated?
  • Are critical processes being monitored?
  • Are monitoring records complete?
  • Were any corrective actions required?
  • Are cleaning and sanitation controls working?
  • Are employees following the procedures?
  • Are food-safety problems being reported immediately?

23. HACCP Implementation Checklist

  1. Define the scope of the food operation.
  2. Assemble an appropriate HACCP team.
  3. Describe the products and processes.
  4. Identify intended use and consumers where relevant.
  5. Construct process flow diagrams.
  6. Verify the flow diagrams on site.
  7. Conduct the hazard analysis.
  8. Determine the significant hazards requiring control.
  9. Identify critical control points where applicable.
  10. Establish appropriate critical limits.
  11. Establish monitoring procedures.
  12. Establish corrective actions.
  13. Establish verification procedures.
  14. Establish documentation and records.
  15. Train employees.
  16. Implement the system.
  17. Review performance and update the system when appropriate.

24. Common HACCP Failures

Failure 1: HACCP Exists Only on Paper

A beautiful HACCP document has little value if the kitchen team does not follow the procedures.

Failure 2: Staff Do Not Understand the Reason

Employees are more likely to follow controls when managers explain the food-safety risk and the reason behind the procedure.

Failure 3: Records Are Completed Later

Delayed recording can make records unreliable. Monitoring should be documented according to the established procedure when the activity takes place.

Failure 4: Corrective Action Is Ignored

A failed monitoring result should trigger the established response. It should not simply be crossed out or forgotten.

Failure 5: The HACCP Plan Is Never Reviewed

Changes to menus, recipes, ingredients, equipment, suppliers, kitchen layout or processes may require the HACCP system to be reviewed.

25. HACCP Culture

HACCP becomes effective when food safety becomes part of everyday kitchen management rather than a once-a-year audit exercise.

The chef, supervisor and manager should demonstrate that food safety has priority even during a busy service.

A strong food-safety culture means employees know what is expected, have the equipment and time needed to perform the procedure, understand how to report problems and know that management will respond when a food-safety control fails.

26. HACCP in a Busy Restaurant Service

The real test of a HACCP system is not a quiet training session. It is a busy service when orders are coming rapidly, staff are under pressure and multiple dishes are being produced simultaneously.

Management should therefore design controls that can realistically be followed during actual operations.

Procedures that are too complicated, too slow or impossible to perform during service are unlikely to remain effective.

Operational rule: Food-safety controls must be designed for the real kitchen, not an imaginary kitchen.

27. The Kitchen Manager's HACCP Responsibility

The kitchen manager is responsible for ensuring that food-safety procedures are translated into daily operational behaviour.

  • Train the team.
  • Set clear expectations.
  • Verify procedures.
  • Review monitoring records.
  • Investigate failures.
  • Take corrective action.
  • Maintain communication with management and relevant food-safety personnel.
  • Review changes to products and processes.
  • Lead by example.

28. Practical HACCP Routine

Before Production
  • Review planned production.
  • Confirm equipment is operating correctly.
  • Check receiving and storage conditions.
  • Confirm required monitoring equipment is available.
  • Brief employees on important food-safety controls.
During Production
  • Monitor established control points.
  • Observe employee practices.
  • Prevent cross-contamination.
  • Record required monitoring results.
  • Act immediately on deviations.
After Production
  • Review records.
  • Confirm corrective actions are complete.
  • Check cleaning and sanitation.
  • Review any food-safety incidents.
  • Identify improvements for the next shift.

29. HACCP Training for Kitchen Employees

Training should be practical and related to the employee's actual job. A commis chef does not need the same depth of HACCP knowledge as a food safety manager, but every employee should understand the controls that apply to their work.

Useful training methods include:

  • Short shift briefings.
  • Demonstrations.
  • Visual procedures.
  • Supervisor observation.
  • Practical assessments.
  • Refresher training.
  • Incident-based coaching.

30. HACCP Review Triggers

A HACCP system should be reviewed when significant changes occur. Examples may include:

  • New menu items.
  • New ingredients.
  • New suppliers.
  • New equipment.
  • Changes in cooking or processing methods.
  • Changes in kitchen layout.
  • Repeated food-safety failures.
  • Changes in applicable requirements.
  • Significant customer or allergen risks.
  • Changes in production volume.

31. HACCP Manager's Quick Reference

Question Manager's Focus
What can go wrong? Identify food-safety hazards.
Where can it go wrong? Map the food process.
Where must control be applied? Determine appropriate control points.
What is acceptable? Establish suitable critical limits or criteria.
How do we know control is working? Monitor the process.
What happens when control fails? Apply corrective action.
How do we know the system works? Verify and review it.
Where is the evidence? Maintain appropriate records.

32. Final HACCP Principle for Hospitality Managers

HACCP should be treated as a living management system. Its purpose is not to create paperwork for an auditor. Its purpose is to help the operation understand where food-safety hazards can occur and maintain effective controls throughout the food process.

The strongest HACCP systems are integrated into purchasing, receiving, storage, preparation, production, service, cleaning, training and management review.

Remember: Identify the hazard. Control the risk. Monitor the control. Correct failures. Verify the system. Keep appropriate records. Train the people who operate it.
