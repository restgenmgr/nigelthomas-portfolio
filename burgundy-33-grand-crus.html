The 33 Grand Crus of Burgundy: A Journey Through the World's Most Prestigious Vineyards

In Burgundy, the true identity of a wine comes from its terroir. At the very top of the classification stand 33 Grand Cru appellations: 24 in the Côte de Nuits, 8 in the Côte de Beaune and 1 in Chablis.

Why Burgundy Is Different

Walk into any serious restaurant, hotel cellar or wine bar and sooner or later a guest will ask the same question: what actually makes one Burgundy worth ten times another? The answer is not a famous château, a clever blend or a marketing budget. In Burgundy, the answer is the ground itself. The region's identity rests on terroir, the combined effect of geology, soil, slope, exposure, climate and centuries of human decisions, and nowhere is that idea taken more literally than at the very top of the pyramid, among the 33 Grand Cru appellations.

These are the vineyards that history, and later the law, recognised as consistently capable of producing the region's most complex, age-worthy and expressive wines. They represent only a small fraction of Burgundy's total output, roughly one to two percent, which is exactly why they are scarce, discussed so passionately and priced the way they are. This guide walks through all 33 in the order shown on the poster, explains how the classification works, and finishes with practical notes on service, storage and food pairing for hospitality professionals and serious enthusiasts.

The Four Levels of Burgundy

Burgundy's appellation system is a pyramid, and it is built on place names rather than producer names. At the base sit the regional appellations, such as Bourgogne Rouge or Bourgogne Blanc, which can be made from grapes grown almost anywhere in the region. One step up are the village appellations, for example Gevrey-Chambertin or Puligny-Montrachet, where the grapes must come from the named commune. Above that are the Premier Cru vineyards, individually named plots within a village that are judged superior, and the label shows the village name followed by the vineyard name.

At the summit are the Grand Crus. A Grand Cru label carries the vineyard name on its own, and the village name is normally left off entirely. That is a quiet but powerful signal: the site has become more famous than the village around it. When a guest reads "Musigny" or "Montrachet" on a bottle, the vineyard is the whole message. The two grapes that define the category are Pinot Noir for the great reds and Chardonnay for the great whites, with only a few exceptions where a small amount of the other colour is permitted.

Vineyard, Not Estate: The Burgundian Philosophy

Compare this with Bordeaux, where the most famous classifications, including the celebrated 1855 ranking of the Médoc, are tied largely to wine estates. A Bordeaux château owns a block of vines and makes its wine, and the reputation belongs to the estate. Burgundy turns that logic around. The classification belongs to the vineyard, and the vineyard is usually divided among many owners. A famous Grand Cru may be farmed by a dozen or even dozens of growers, each with a few rows, each with a different philosophy in the vineyard and the cellar.

The practical consequence is something every sommelier learns early: the name of the vineyard tells you where the grapes grew, but the name of the producer tells you how good the wine is likely to be. Two bottles from the same Grand Cru can differ in style, concentration, oak and price. That is why Burgundy rewards knowledge of growers as much as knowledge of maps.

The same thinking explains the Burgundian word climat, a precisely delimited vineyard parcel with its own name, history and character. The patchwork of climats along the Côte d'Or and in Chablis was recognised by UNESCO in 2015 as a World Heritage cultural landscape, a remarkable acknowledgement that the "map" of Burgundy is itself a human masterpiece, shaped over many centuries by monks, merchants and farming families. Two neighbouring vineyards, separated by a path or a low wall, can taste remarkably different even when planted with the same grape. That is the fascinating beauty of Burgundy.

Côte de Nuits: The 24 Grand Crus of Pinot Noir

The Côte de Nuits, the northern half of the Côte d'Or, is the legendary home of red Burgundy. Twenty-four of the 33 Grand Crus sit here, strung along a narrow limestone slope from Gevrey-Chambertin in the north to Vosne-Romanée in the south. The wines are famed for perfume, silky tannin, depth and an almost unreasonable ability to age. We take the communes in turn.

Gevrey-Chambertin: Nine Grand Crus (1–9)

Gevrey-Chambertin is the most generous commune in the Grand Cru count, with nine appellations that all carry the Chambertin name. The two heavyweights are Chambertin and Chambertin-Clos de Bèze. The latter takes its name from a walled vineyard that is said to have been given to monks in the seventh century, and its wines may be sold under either name. Chambertin has long been associated with powerful, structured, long-lived red wine and, as the story is traditionally told, was a favourite of Napoleon.

Around them sit seven more: Chapelle-Chambertin, Charmes-Chambertin, Griotte-Chambertin, Latricières-Chambertin, Mazis-Chambertin, Mazoyères-Chambertin and Ruchottes-Chambertin. In broad terms, Mazis and Ruchottes lean towards firmness and mineral tension, Charmes and Chapelle towards perfume and softer fruit, and Griotte, the smallest of the group, towards delicate red-cherry charm. Wines from Mazoyères are permitted to be labelled as Charmes-Chambertin, a useful footnote for anyone studying wine lists.

Morey-Saint-Denis: Four Grand Crus (10–13) and Bonnes-Mares (14)

Next come the Grand Crus of Morey-Saint-Denis: Clos de la Roche, Clos Saint-Denis, Clos de Tart and Clos des Lambrays. Clos de la Roche is typically described as the most muscular of them, with rocky, earthy depth, while Clos Saint-Denis is often more graceful and floral. Clos de Tart is a monopole, meaning a single owner holds the entire vineyard, a rarity at this level and a clear illustration of how unusual unified ownership is in Burgundy. Clos des Lambrays was promoted to Grand Cru status relatively recently, in 1981, a reminder that the list is not frozen in time.

Bonnes-Mares straddles two communes, Chambolle-Musigny and Morey-Saint-Denis, with the larger part lying in Chambolle. Its wines tend to be darker, firmer and more structured than the silky, perfumed wines usually associated with Chambolle.

Chambolle-Musigny and Vougeot (15–16)

Musigny is often held up as the most ethereal red wine in the world: fine-boned, aromatic, floral, with a texture that is frequently compared to silk or lace. It is a small vineyard, and a tiny amount of white wine is also made within it. Immediately to the south, Clos de Vougeot is the great walled vineyard built by Cistercian monks, and at around fifty hectares it is the largest Grand Cru in the Côte de Nuits. It is split among dozens of owners, so quality depends enormously on the grower and on where in the slope the vines grow. Today its château is also the headquarters of the Confrérie des Chevaliers du Tastevin, which holds ceremonial dinners that are a fixture of Burgundian hospitality.

Flagey-Échezeaux and Vosne-Romanée (17–24)

Échezeaux and Grands Échezeaux lie in the commune of Flagey-Échezeaux and are often seen as the more approachable way into the Vosne neighbourhood: aromatic, spicy and graceful, with Grands Échezeaux usually the more serious of the pair.

Then comes the most prestigious cluster of vineyards on earth. Vosne-Romanée holds six Grand Crus: Romanée-Conti, La Romanée, La Tâche, Richebourg, Romanée-Saint-Vivant and La Grande Rue. Romanée-Conti is a monopole of Domaine de la Romanée-Conti and covers under two hectares. La Tâche, also a DRC monopole, is larger and famously powerful and spicy. La Romanée, under one hectare, is the smallest appellation in France, and La Grande Rue, a monopole, was only elevated to Grand Cru in 1992. Richebourg is celebrated for richness and opulence, while Romanée-Saint-Vivant is often more delicate and perfumed. Together these wines are the reference point for the entire category, and a single bottle can cost more than a month's rent in many cities.

Côte de Beaune: The 8 Grand Crus (25–32)

South of the hill of Corton, the Côte de Beaune is the great home of white Burgundy, although the first of its eight Grand Crus is a notable exception.

Corton is a large hillside around Aloxe-Corton, Ladoix-Serrigny and Pernand-Vergelesses. It is the only red Grand Cru of any size in the Côte de Beaune, producing structured, long-lived Pinot Noir, and it can also produce white wine. Its neighbour Corton-Charlemagne is the white counterpart: a broad, mineral Chardonnay with great power and ageing potential. Legend ties the name to the Emperor Charlemagne, who is said to have ordered white vines planted to spare his wife's embarrassment from red wine stains on his beard. Whether or not the tale is true, it is a favourite of tour guides. Charlemagne itself is a closely related appellation that overlaps the same hill.

The remaining five Grand Crus all sit in or around Puligny-Montrachet and Chassagne-Montrachet and are, without exception, white. Montrachet is the king of the group, a vineyard of roughly eight hectares shared between the two villages and long considered the benchmark for dry white wine: rich, layered, complex, with remarkable length. Chevalier-Montrachet, sitting higher on the slope in Puligny, is leaner, more mineral and more tightly wound. Bâtard-Montrachet straddles both villages and tends towards a fuller, rounder, more generous style. Bienvenues-Bâtard-Montrachet in Puligny is typically a touch more delicate and perfumed, and Criots-Bâtard-Montrachet in Chassagne is the smallest of the family, often marked by freshness and a distinctive almond note.

When a guest asks why these wines cost so much, the honest answer is a mix of tiny surface area, painstaking farming, low yields, strong global demand and the simple fact that the best examples can evolve gracefully for many years.

Chablis: One Grand Cru, Seven Climats (33)

Far to the north, around an hour and a half from the Côte d'Or, lies the Chablis region, and with it the single Grand Cru appellation that completes the list: Chablis Grand Cru. Unlike the Côte de Nuits and Côte de Beaune, which list each vineyard as its own appellation, Chablis has one Grand Cru appellation that groups seven named climats on a single south-west-facing slope above the Serein river. The total area is only about a hundred hectares.

The seven climats are Les Clos, Vaudésir, Valmur, Grenouilles, Blanchot, Bougros and Les Preuses. Les Clos is the largest and is often considered the most powerful and long-lived, with a firm mineral backbone. Vaudésir and Les Preuses tend to be rounder and more aromatic, Valmur is typically a balance of richness and tension, Blanchot is known for floral delicacy, Bougros for a more robust, earthy character, and Grenouilles, the smallest, for a distinctive, almost saline freshness. The soils are Kimmeridgian limestone, packed with fossilised oyster shells, which gives the wines their famous mineral snap. A good Chablis Grand Cru is the very definition of precision, a Chardonnay that is sharp, saline and capable of improving for a decade or more.

Two Grapes, One Philosophy

It is easy to forget how narrow the grape palette is. Almost every Grand Cru is either Pinot Noir or Chardonnay. Pinot Noir is a thin-skinned, temperamental variety that shows soil differences with almost embarrassing transparency, which is precisely why Burgundians love it. Chardonnay is a more neutral canvas that shows the site through texture, acidity and mineral tension rather than through loud fruit flavours. Because neither grape shouts, the vineyard speaks. That is the entire logic of the system, and the reason a few metres of slope can change the price of a bottle several times over.

Practical Notes for Hospitality Professionals

Reading the label

On a Grand Cru label look for the vineyard name, the words Appellation Contrôlée and the producer or négociant. Producers vary enormously in size and style. A small domaine that farms its own vines and bottles its own wine is not the same proposition as a négociant that buys grapes or wine and blends across many growers, although excellent examples exist in both categories. The vintage matters a great deal in Burgundy because of the cool, variable climate, so a staff briefing on recent vintages is time well spent.

Serving temperature and glassware

Overchilling a white Grand Cru hides its aromas and overwarming a red exaggerates alcohol. As a practical guide, serve white Burgundy at around 10 to 12 degrees Celsius and red Burgundy at around 15 to 17 degrees. In a warm dining room the red benefits from ten minutes in a bucket before service. Use a generously sized, tulip-shaped glass with a wide bowl, because Burgundy is about aroma, and fill it only about a third full so the guest can swirl.

Decanting and timing

Young, structured Grand Crus often benefit from an hour or two in a decanter. Very old Pinot Noir is more fragile and may need only a gentle pour off its sediment shortly before service. Allow the guest to taste first and let the wine develop in the glass, because a great Burgundy often changes noticeably over the course of a meal.

Storage and handling

Store bottles on their side at a steady 12 to 14 degrees Celsius, away from vibration, light and strong smells. A reliable cellar log is as important as the wine list itself when a single bottle carries a four-figure price. Rotate stock by vintage, record any bottle with seepage or a low fill level, and never serve a bottle you have not first inspected for cork taint.

Selling and explaining Grand Cru

Guests rarely need a lecture. A short, confident explanation works better: this vineyard is one of the 33 best sites in Burgundy, this grower farms it by hand, and here is how it will taste. Offer comparisons rather than facts, for example the more floral Musigny against the more muscular Chambertin, or the richer Montrachet against the more mineral Chevalier-Montrachet. Where budgets are limited, point guests towards good Premier Cru bottles or village wines from top producers, which often capture the same style at a fraction of the cost. Selling honestly builds trust, and trust is what brings guests back.

Food Pairing

Red Grand Crus from the Côte de Nuits love roasted game birds, duck, mushroom dishes, aged cheeses such as Époisses and slow-cooked beef in red wine reductions. Powerful reds from Chambertin or Corton can handle richer dishes such as venison, while the lighter, more perfumed wines from Chambolle suit roast squab or lightly spiced lamb. White Grand Crus from the Côte de Beaune pair beautifully with lobster in butter, scallops, roast chicken, veal in cream sauce and fine soft cheeses, and they can stand up to truffle. Chablis Grand Cru is the classic partner for oysters, shellfish, grilled white fish and anything finished with lemon or a clean butter sauce. As a rule, match weight with weight: the more concentrated the wine, the more care the plate needs to keep from overwhelming it.

Quick Recap

  • 33 Grand Cru appellations sit at the very top of Burgundy's classification.
  • 24 in the Côte de Nuits, mainly Pinot Noir, from Gevrey-Chambertin to Vosne-Romanée.
  • 8 in the Côte de Beaune: Corton for red, and the Charlemagne and Montrachet family for white Chardonnay.
  • 1 in Chablis, covering seven climats of mineral, Kimmeridgian Chardonnay.
  • The classification belongs to the vineyard, not the estate, so the grower matters as much as the name.

Thirty-three Grand Cru appellations. Two iconic grape varieties. One extraordinary philosophy: terroir.
