Hospitality Safety & Compliance

OSHWC & Workplace Safety

What every hotel, restaurant and hospitality manager should know, check and document to keep people safe and the operation inspection-ready.

Operational guide for India • Nigel A. Thomas
Manager's principle: Workplace safety is not a file kept for an inspector. It is a daily operating system. A safe hotel or restaurant identifies hazards before someone is injured, provides appropriate controls and training, maintains equipment, records incidents and closes corrective actions.

1. What is the OSHWC Code?

The Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 is one of India's four Labour Codes. It consolidates and amends laws relating to occupational safety, health and working conditions. The Code came into force on 21 November 2025.

For hospitality managers, the important point is not simply knowing the name of the legislation. The practical question is: can your operation demonstrate that people are being protected while work is being performed?

The OSHWC framework sits within a much wider hospitality compliance environment. A hotel or restaurant can also have obligations relating to fire safety, electrical safety, LPG and gas, food safety, environmental protection, building safety, lifts, municipal permissions, employment requirements and other State or local requirements.

That means an effective hotel safety system should connect the requirements together rather than keeping them in isolated files.

2. Why workplace safety is different in hospitality

Commercial kitchens

Knives, hot oil, steam, ovens, slicers, mixers, wet floors, chemicals, LPG and repetitive work create multiple hazards in a concentrated working area.

Housekeeping

Wet floors, cleaning chemicals, lifting, mattresses, carts, bathroom surfaces, electrical appliances and repetitive tasks require active controls.

Engineering

Electrical panels, generators, refrigeration, pumps, boilers, plant rooms, lifts and maintenance work require competent people and controlled procedures.

Guest areas

Restaurants, bars, stairs, entrances, public toilets and service areas introduce slip, trip, crowd movement and emergency-response risks.

Stores & receiving

Deliveries, crates, trolleys, loading areas, waste movement, storage racks and manual handling create additional risks.

Human factors

Fatigue, rushing, poor supervision, inadequate training, language barriers and production pressure can turn known hazards into incidents.

3. The manager's workplace safety system

A useful safety system can be built around five questions:

  1. What can hurt someone? Identify hazards by department, equipment and task.
  2. Who could be affected? Consider employees, trainees, contractors, visitors and other people exposed to the activity.
  3. What controls are in place? Prefer physical and engineering controls before depending only on warnings or PPE.
  4. How do we know the controls work? Inspect, test, maintain, supervise and record.
  5. What happens when something goes wrong? Protect people, report, investigate, correct and learn.

4. Building a practical workplace hazard register

Every hotel should maintain a practical hazard register, risk assessment or equivalent risk-management record appropriate to its operation.

The purpose is not to create paperwork for its own sake. The purpose is to identify hazards before they become accidents and to show who is responsible for correcting them.

  • Department or location
  • Task, activity or equipment
  • Hazard identified
  • People exposed
  • Existing control
  • Risk level or priority
  • Additional action required
  • Responsible person
  • Target date
  • Completion evidence
  • Close-out date

A hazard register becomes useful when supervisors actually walk the operation and update it. A document that says "kitchen safe" while a damaged plug, leaking hose or broken floor tile is visible is not a functioning safety system.

5. Kitchen and restaurant workplace safety

Heat, burns, steam and hot oil

Hot equipment is one of the most obvious hospitality hazards, yet many burns happen because employees become accustomed to the environment and begin taking shortcuts.

  • Keep pan handles away from traffic routes.
  • Use appropriate gloves and tools for hot trays and equipment.
  • Control access to hot oil and fryer filtration.
  • Train employees in the correct response to hot-oil fires.
  • Keep emergency equipment accessible.
  • Do not use water on burning cooking oil.
  • Control steam-release hazards when opening pressure or steam-producing equipment.
Knives, slicers and food machinery

Machines must be operated with their designed guards and safety features in place. Employees should never bypass a safety feature simply because production is busy.

  • Train employees on the specific equipment they use.
  • Keep guards fitted and functional.
  • Use appropriate push tools where designed.
  • Never reach into moving machinery.
  • Isolate equipment before maintenance or clearing a dangerous jam where required.
  • Remove damaged equipment from service until repaired.
Wet floors, slips and trips

A slip should not automatically be recorded as "employee carelessness". Managers should investigate why the floor became unsafe.

Look for leaking equipment, poor drainage, condensation, grease, incorrect cleaning methods, damaged flooring and uncontrolled water movement.

  • Clean spills immediately.
  • Control the source of water or grease.
  • Use suitable floor-cleaning procedures.
  • Keep walkways clear.
  • Repair damaged flooring.
  • Use appropriate footwear policies where required.

6. Housekeeping and chemical safety

Cleaning products can cause burns, respiratory irritation, poisoning or dangerous reactions when handled incorrectly.

Every housekeeping, stewarding and engineering team should know the chemicals used in the property and the safe handling information provided by the manufacturer.

  • Keep chemicals in correctly labelled containers.
  • Never store chemicals in food or beverage containers.
  • Do not mix chemicals unless the manufacturer's instructions specifically require it.
  • Keep incompatible chemicals separated.
  • Provide appropriate PPE for the task.
  • Train employees in dilution and safe handling.
  • Maintain appropriate spill-response arrangements.
  • Keep chemical storage secure and controlled.

Safety Data Sheets or equivalent manufacturer information should be accessible for relevant hazardous chemical products. Supervisors should know where this information is stored.

7. Manual handling and ergonomics

Hotels move heavy and awkward objects every day: beverage crates, flour sacks, rice bags, linen bags, mattresses, furniture, housekeeping supplies and equipment.

"Be careful when lifting" is not an adequate safety system.

Reduce the load

Use smaller containers or split loads where practical.

Reduce the distance

Store frequently used supplies closer to their point of use without creating additional hazards.

Use equipment

Use trolleys, carts, dollies, lifts or other suitable mechanical assistance where appropriate.

Team lifting

Establish when a second person or additional equipment is required for awkward loads.

8. Working conditions managers should inspect

Workplace safety includes the conditions in which employees actually perform their jobs.

Area Manager's practical check
Cleanliness Are work areas, staff areas, service spaces and equipment kept clean and maintained?
Ventilation Are kitchens, stores, staff areas and enclosed workplaces adequately ventilated for their use?
Lighting Can employees safely see their work, equipment, labels, steps and hazards?
Drinking water Is suitable drinking water available and properly maintained where required?
Sanitation Are toilets, washing facilities and related areas clean, usable and maintained?
Waste Is waste removed without creating pests, contamination, slips or other hazards?
Temperature Are working conditions appropriate to the work, particularly in hot kitchens and enclosed areas?
Access Are routes, stairs and working areas clear, stable and usable?
Emergency exits Are emergency routes and exits accessible and unobstructed?

9. PPE: important, but not the whole safety system

Personal protective equipment is an important control, but PPE should not become an excuse for leaving a hazard uncontrolled.

Managers should first ask whether the task, equipment, layout or engineering control can reduce the exposure.

  • Identify the hazard.
  • Determine the appropriate PPE.
  • Provide suitable equipment.
  • Train employees in correct use.
  • Inspect PPE.
  • Replace damaged or ineffective PPE.
  • Supervise compliance.

10. Electrical, gas and maintenance work

Hospitality properties combine high electrical demand with wet areas, cooking heat, refrigeration, laundry equipment, generators, gas systems and maintenance activities.

Manager's visual check:
  • No exposed conductors, broken plugs or improvised electrical repairs.
  • Electrical panels are identified, accessible and not blocked by storage.
  • Extension leads are not being used as permanent substitutes for appropriate installations.
  • Gas cylinders, hoses, regulators and associated equipment are maintained according to applicable requirements.
  • Maintenance work is controlled so employees are not unexpectedly exposed to live, pressurised, hot or moving equipment.
  • Inspection and maintenance records are current and traceable.

Do not ask unqualified employees to perform electrical or gas work simply because the hotel is busy. Production pressure is never a safety control.

11. Contractors and temporary workers

Contractor safety is frequently overlooked because the contractor may not be on the hotel's payroll.

Operationally, however, a contractor can still injure hotel employees, guests or other contractors.

  • Identify the contractor and scope of work before starting.
  • Verify applicable competency, licences or authorisations.
  • Explain site rules and emergency procedures.
  • Control hot work, work at height, electrical work and other higher-risk activities through appropriate procedures or permits where required.
  • Keep work areas segregated.
  • Prevent unsafe shortcuts.
  • Record significant contractor incidents.

12. Emergency preparedness

A written emergency plan is useful only if employees know what to do when an emergency actually occurs.

Hospitality emergency arrangements should connect workplace safety with the property's fire, medical, evacuation, security, gas, electrical and disaster-response procedures.

Emergency readiness checklist
  • Emergency contacts are current.
  • Employees know how to raise an alarm.
  • Evacuation routes are understood.
  • Assembly arrangements are known.
  • First-aid arrangements are appropriate to the operation.
  • Emergency equipment is accessible.
  • Staff know who takes charge during an incident.
  • Relevant contractors and visitors can be accounted for.
  • Emergency drills or exercises are conducted where required or appropriate.
  • Lessons from drills are recorded.
  • Corrective actions are tracked to closure.

13. Accident, incident and near-miss management

A strong hotel safety system learns from events before the same event happens again.

A near miss is valuable information. If a worker almost slips beside the fryer, the correct response is not simply: "Nobody was hurt."

The correct question is: "Why did this happen, and what are we going to change?"

When an incident occurs

  1. Protect people. Stop the immediate danger and arrange first aid or emergency assistance.
  2. Secure the area. Prevent another person from being exposed.
  3. Notify the appropriate people. Follow the property's reporting procedure and applicable notification requirements.
  4. Record the facts. Document what happened, when, where and who was involved.
  5. Investigate the cause. Look beyond "employee error".
  6. Correct the system. Repair, redesign, retrain or change the procedure as appropriate.
  7. Close the action. Record who completed the corrective action and when.

14. Training and competency

Safety training should be connected to the actual job. A new steward, commis chef, housekeeping attendant, bartender, engineering technician and supervisor face different hazards.

Induction

Site rules, emergency arrangements, hazard reporting, prohibited practices and department-specific risks.

Job training

Safe operation of equipment, chemicals, tools, manual handling and job-specific procedures.

Refresher training

Update training when risks, equipment, procedures or incidents show a need.

Supervisor training

Teach supervisors to identify hazards, intervene, report and close corrective actions.

Keep appropriate attendance and competency evidence. A signature on a training sheet alone does not demonstrate that an employee can safely perform a high-risk task.

15. Employee welfare and working conditions

Workplace safety is not only about accidents. Managers should also pay attention to the conditions in which people work for long periods.

In hospitality this includes practical attention to sanitation, drinking water, ventilation, lighting, rest arrangements where applicable, cleanliness, heat exposure, workload and other working-condition controls required by the applicable framework.

Managers should also ensure that applicable working-hour, overtime, leave, appointment and employment requirements are handled through the property's HR and compliance systems. These matters can involve the Labour Codes and State rules, so they should not be treated as one universal hotel checklist.

16. Daily, weekly and monthly manager routine

Frequency Manager's safety routine
Every shift Walk kitchens, restaurants, housekeeping, stores, engineering and public areas. Look for immediate hazards, spills, blocked routes, damaged equipment and unsafe behaviour.
Daily Review incidents and near misses, confirm critical equipment issues are being addressed and check emergency access.
Weekly Review open corrective actions, inspect higher-risk areas, review contractor activity and discuss safety observations with supervisors.
Monthly Review trends, training status, maintenance records, incidents, near misses, recurring hazards and overdue actions with department heads.
Periodic Complete applicable statutory inspections, tests, servicing, drills, audits, licences and renewals according to the relevant authority and equipment requirements.

17. Building the workplace safety compliance file

A well-organised compliance file makes management easier and inspection preparation much less stressful.

The exact documents required depend on the establishment, workforce, activities and applicable Central, State and local requirements.

  • Current registrations, licences and approvals relevant to workplace operations.
  • Organisation chart and named safety responsibilities.
  • Hazard register or risk assessments.
  • Safety policies, SOPs and emergency procedures.
  • Employee induction and safety training records.
  • Equipment inspection, testing and maintenance records.
  • Fire and emergency drill records.
  • First-aid arrangements and relevant inspection records.
  • Contractor records and permits where applicable.
  • Incident, accident and near-miss reports.
  • Corrective-action tracker with closure evidence.
  • Applicable occupational-health or medical records where required.
  • Records relating to welfare, sanitation, drinking water and other working-condition controls where applicable.

18. What should an inspector see when walking your property?

Managers should not design safety around hiding problems. The stronger approach is to make the normal workplace visibly controlled.

  • Clear access and escape routes.
  • Clean and orderly work areas.
  • Employees using appropriate equipment.
  • Machines operated with guards and safety features in place.
  • Chemicals labelled and stored correctly.
  • Electrical and service areas maintained.
  • Emergency equipment visible and accessible.
  • Staff who know how to report hazards.
  • Records that match what is actually happening on the floor.
  • Open deficiencies being actively corrected.

19. The practical 30-minute GM safety walk

For a busy General Manager or F&B Manager, a structured 30-minute safety walk can be more valuable than a long meeting.

  1. Start at the back-of-house entry and check access.
  2. Walk through receiving and stores. Look for stacking, manual handling and blocked routes.
  3. Enter the kitchen. Check floors, heat, machinery, knives, chemicals, gas and electrical concerns and emergency access.
  4. Walk through stewarding and waste areas.
  5. Check housekeeping and service corridors.
  6. Check public areas, stairs, toilets and restaurant routes.
  7. Check engineering access and active contractor work.
  8. Ask two employees: "What is the biggest safety problem you see in your job?"
  9. Record the top three actions and assign owners.
  10. Return later and verify closure.

20. Questions every manager should ask

Ask the supervisor

What is the most dangerous task in your department? What changed this month? What has not yet been fixed?

Ask the employee

What could injure you here? Do you know how to report it? Do you have the equipment and training you need?

Ask Engineering

Which safety-critical equipment is overdue for inspection, testing or maintenance?

Ask HR

Which safety inductions, refresher training and required worker records are outstanding?

21. Contractor and outsourced-operation checks

Many hotels outsource laundry, pest control, security, housekeeping, engineering, waste collection, kitchen support or other services.

Outsourcing a task does not mean management can ignore the risks created by the activity on the property.

  • Know which contractors are working on the property.
  • Know what work they are performing.
  • Identify higher-risk activities before they begin.
  • Verify applicable competence or authorisation.
  • Communicate emergency arrangements.
  • Control access to restricted areas.
  • Use permits or formal controls where appropriate.
  • Review unsafe contractor behaviour immediately.
  • Record significant incidents and corrective actions.

22. Linking OSHWC with the wider hospitality compliance system

OSHWC is one part of hospitality compliance. A hotel manager should connect workplace safety with the property's wider safety and regulatory map.

  • FSSAI & Food Safety — food hygiene, food safety management and kitchen inspection readiness.
  • Fire Safety — fire prevention, emergency systems, evacuation and local fire-authority requirements.
  • LPG, Gas & Commercial Kitchen Safety — safe storage, handling, installation and maintenance under applicable requirements.
  • Electrical Safety — safe installations, inspection and competent maintenance.
  • Environment & Waste — waste, wastewater, pollution-control and environmental requirements.
  • Building, Lift & Property Safety — structural, lift, access and property-related requirements.
  • Labour & Employment Compliance — working conditions and employment obligations applicable to the establishment.

23. Central, State and local requirements

One of the most important points for a hospitality manager is that Indian compliance is not always a single national checklist.

The Central OSHWC Code provides the national legislative framework, while applicable rules, notifications and competent authorities can affect how requirements are implemented.

State Labour Departments also publish their own material. For example, Maharashtra's Labour Department currently publishes the OSHWC Code together with State draft rules, Central Rules and OSHWC FAQs.

Therefore, a hotel operating in Maharashtra should not automatically assume that a checklist prepared for another State is sufficient. The same principle applies when operating in Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Telangana, Delhi, Rajasthan or another jurisdiction.

25. The hospitality manager's final checklist

Before you say your property is safety-ready, ask:
  • Have we identified the major hazards in every department?
  • Do supervisors know their safety responsibilities?
  • Are employees trained for the equipment and tasks they perform?
  • Are emergency routes and equipment accessible?
  • Are chemicals labelled and controlled?
  • Are machinery guards and safety devices functioning?
  • Are electrical and gas systems being maintained by competent persons?
  • Are workplace sanitation, ventilation, lighting and drinking-water arrangements being maintained?
  • Are contractor activities controlled?
  • Are incidents and near misses investigated?
  • Are corrective actions actually closed?
  • Can we produce the relevant records when requested?
  • Does our documentation reflect what is really happening on the operating floor?

26. Final operational message

The strongest hospitality safety culture does not begin with an inspector arriving at the front door.

It begins when the General Manager walks through the kitchen before breakfast service, when the F&B Manager notices a wet floor before someone slips, when the Executive Chef removes damaged equipment from service, when the Housekeeping Manager corrects an unsafe chemical practice, when Engineering refuses an unsafe shortcut and when a supervisor listens to an employee who reports a hazard.

That is what workplace safety looks like in a real hospitality operation.

The OSHWC framework provides an important legal foundation, but compliance should become part of the property's operating discipline: identify, control, train, inspect, record, correct and learn.

A hotel that operates this way is not merely preparing for an inspection. It is protecting employees, contractors, guests, management and the business itself.
