Profit is Not Lost at the End of the Month — It’s Lost Every Day

By Nigel A. Thomas • Hospitality Executive & Corporate Trainer

Hotel P&L Explained: Revenue minus operating expenses equals profit or loss.

The Problem with Waiting for Month-End

In the hospitality industry, waiting for a monthly P&L report means discovering problems after they have already affected profitability. A small food-cost increase, unnecessary overtime, purchasing variance or unfavorable menu mix can continue for weeks before management sees its full financial effect.

A 10-day P&L control cycle helps management identify operational gaps early and take corrective action before losses compound. The purpose is not to create more reports — the purpose is to create faster operational action.

The monthly P&L should remain the formal financial statement, but operational managers should not wait 30 days to discover that the business is moving away from its targets.

Why a 10-Day P&L Control Cycle Works

Hospitality profitability is created through hundreds of daily decisions. Every room sold, every meal produced, every purchase made, every hour scheduled and every item wasted affects the final P&L.

By reviewing the key operating numbers every 10 days, management gets an early warning system. If food cost is moving in the wrong direction, the purchasing and kitchen teams can investigate immediately. If labor productivity falls, scheduling can be corrected before excessive payroll becomes a month-end problem.

How to Act on the Key KPIs

  • Food Cost %: If actual food cost is materially above target, investigate purchasing prices, portion control, recipe adherence, waste, spoilage and theoretical-versus-actual usage.
  • Labor Cost %: Compare scheduled hours with actual hours and revenue. Identify overtime, overstaffing and low-productivity operating periods before they become a recurring monthly cost.
  • Menu Mix: Monitor whether high-contribution items are maintaining their expected share of sales. A change in menu mix can reduce profitability even when total revenue remains stable.
  • MPLH: Monitor meals or covers produced per labor hour. A decline can indicate overstaffing, weak scheduling, slow service periods or operational inefficiency.
  • Waste & Variance: Investigate unusual stock variance, spoilage, overproduction, portioning errors and unexplained consumption.
  • Purchasing Price Variance: Compare current supplier prices with approved or historical prices. Unusual increases should be investigated immediately rather than discovered during month-end review.
  • Sales by Menu Item: Identify significant changes in the performance of key dishes and beverages. Use menu engineering, staff training and suggestive selling to improve the sales mix.
“Profit is controlled in cycles, not at month end. If you correct within 10 days, you recover margin before it compounds.”

Make the 10-Day Cycle Your New Standard

Start your first 10-day cycle tomorrow rather than waiting for the beginning of the next month.

The objective is simple: measure, identify, act and recover.

When management waits until month-end, the P&L tells you what happened. When management reviews the business continuously, the numbers can help you change what happens next.

That is the difference between reporting profitability and managing profitability.

The Bottom Line

In hotels, resorts and restaurants, profit is not created when the accountant prepares the P&L. Profit is created every day through operational discipline.

Food cost, labor productivity, purchasing, menu mix, waste, stock control and revenue management all contribute to the final result.

Good hospitality creates memories. Good management creates profitability.
