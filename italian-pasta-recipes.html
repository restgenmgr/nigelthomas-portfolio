5 Classic Italian Pasta Recipes

Restaurant-style pasta, built the way a professional kitchen builds it — by Nigel A. Thomas

Italian pasta cooking rewards patience more than complexity. None of the five recipes below calls for an unusual ingredient or a piece of equipment beyond a large pot and a wide sauté pan, yet each one, made correctly, tastes like something plated in a proper Italian dining room rather than a weeknight kitchen. The difference between an average bowl of pasta and a genuinely good one almost never comes down to the recipe itself. It comes down to four habits that professional cooks treat as non-negotiable and home cooks often skip: salting the pasta water properly, timing the pasta to finish a minute under fully cooked, reserving a cup of that starchy water before draining, and finishing the dish in the sauce rather than simply spooning sauce over plain pasta. Every recipe in this guide leans on those four habits, and I have called them out again at the end so you can apply them to any pasta dish you cook, not only the five here.

These five were chosen because together they cover the main directions Italian pasta cooking can go in: an earthy, meat-free dish built on mushrooms and cream; a quick seafood dish that leans on citrus and garlic; a slow-simmered meat ragù that rewards a lazy afternoon; a spicy, tomato-forward dish finished with fresh burrata; and a comforting, protein-forward chicken dish with a bright lemon edge. If you cook through all five over a few weeks, you will have a genuinely useful repertoire — vegetarian, seafood, red meat, spicy, and poultry — that covers almost any dinner request a household or a small table of guests is likely to make.

1. Truffle Mushroom Tagliatelle

01Truffle Mushroom Tagliatelle

Vegetarian · Serves 2 · 25 minutes

Mushrooms and truffle oil are a natural pairing because both bring umami without meat, and tagliatelle's wide, ribboned shape is built to carry a cream sauce without the pasta disappearing underneath it. This is the dish to make when you want something that feels indulgent but takes no more effort than a weeknight stir-fry.

Ingredients

  • 200 g tagliatelle
  • 200 g mixed mushrooms (a mix of cremini, oyster and shiitake gives the best texture contrast)
  • 2 garlic cloves, finely chopped
  • 20 g butter
  • 120 ml cream
  • 35 g Parmesan, finely grated
  • 1 tsp truffle oil
  • A small handful of parsley, chopped
  • Salt and black pepper, to taste

Method

  • Bring a large pot of well-salted water to a rolling boil and cook the tagliatelle until just short of al dente. Scoop out and reserve at least a cup of the starchy pasta water before draining.
  • While the pasta cooks, melt the butter in a wide pan over medium-high heat. Add the mushrooms in a single layer and leave them alone for two to three minutes so they brown rather than steam, then stir and cook until deeply golden.
  • Add the garlic and cook for thirty seconds until fragrant, then pour in the cream along with a splash of the reserved pasta water. Simmer for a minute to let the sauce loosen and come together.
  • Add the drained tagliatelle directly into the pan and toss to coat, adding more pasta water a tablespoon at a time if the sauce needs loosening further.
  • Stir in the Parmesan off the heat so it melts smoothly rather than clumping, season with salt and pepper, and finish with the truffle oil and chopped parsley.
Chef's tip: stir the truffle oil in only after the pan has come off the heat. Truffle aroma is built on volatile compounds that cook off quickly, so adding it while the sauce is still simmering wastes most of what you paid for.

Serve this in a warmed, wide bowl so the tagliatelle doesn't cool and clump, and finish tableside with an extra pass of the Parmesan grater. A dry, medium-bodied white — a Soave or an unoaked Chardonnay — cuts through the cream without competing with the truffle note.

2. Creamy Garlic Shrimp Linguine

02Creamy Garlic Shrimp Linguine

Seafood · Serves 2 · 25 minutes

This is the recipe I reach for when a dinner needs to look impressive but has to be on the table in well under half an hour. Shrimp cook in minutes, and the garlic-cream sauce comes together in the same pan, which keeps the washing-up light and the flavour concentrated.

Ingredients

  • 200 g linguine
  • 250 g peeled shrimp, deveined
  • 3 garlic cloves, finely chopped
  • 20 g butter
  • 120 ml cream
  • 30 g Parmesan, finely grated
  • 1 tbsp lemon juice
  • A small handful of parsley, chopped
  • Salt and black pepper, to taste

Method

  • Cook the linguine in well-salted, rapidly boiling water until just short of al dente, then reserve a cup of the pasta water and drain.
  • Pat the shrimp dry with paper towels — this matters more than it sounds like it should, because wet shrimp steam instead of searing. Season them with salt and pepper.
  • Heat the butter in a wide pan over fairly high heat and sear the shrimp for roughly a minute per side, until just pink and opaque. Remove them to a plate immediately; they will finish cooking in residual heat.
  • In the same pan, lower the heat and cook the garlic gently for thirty seconds so it doesn't catch and turn bitter, then add the cream and Parmesan and stir until the cheese melts into a glossy sauce.
  • Loosen the sauce with a splash of the reserved pasta water, add the drained linguine, and toss until every strand is coated.
  • Return the shrimp to the pan just long enough to warm through, then finish with lemon juice and parsley.
Chef's tip: always pull the shrimp out of the pan before you build the sauce and add them back at the very end. Leaving them in while the cream reduces is the single most common way this dish turns rubbery.

A wedge of lemon on the side lets guests add extra acidity to taste, since cream sauces vary in richness depending on the exact fat content of your cream. A crisp Vermentino or Pinot Grigio is the classic pairing here.

3. Classic Beef Ragù Pappardelle

03Classic Beef Ragù Pappardelle

Meat · Serves 2 · 45 minutes (mostly unattended)

A proper ragù is a lesson in why Italian cooking prizes restraint: a handful of vegetables, good tomatoes, and beef, given enough time on low heat, produce a sauce with more depth than a long ingredient list would suggest. Pappardelle's broad, flat ribbons are the traditional match because they hold onto the chunky sauce far better than a thin strand pasta would.

Ingredients

  • 200 g pappardelle
  • 250 g ground beef
  • ½ onion, finely diced
  • 1 small carrot, finely diced
  • 1 garlic clove, chopped
  • 300 g crushed tomatoes
  • 1 tbsp tomato paste
  • 40 g Parmesan, plus shavings to finish
  • Olive oil, salt and black pepper

Method

  • Warm a little olive oil in a heavy pot over medium heat and soften the onion and carrot for five to six minutes until they turn translucent and start to sweeten.
  • Turn the heat up, add the ground beef, and break it apart with a wooden spoon. Cook until it is well browned all over — this browning is where most of the sauce's flavour comes from, so don't rush it or crowd the pot.
  • Stir in the garlic and tomato paste and cook for a minute until the paste darkens slightly and smells toasted rather than raw.
  • Add the crushed tomatoes, season with salt and pepper, and bring to a gentle simmer. Partially cover and let it cook for 30 to 40 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the sauce has thickened and the flavours have melded.
  • Near the end of the simmer, cook the pappardelle in well-salted water until al dente, reserving a little pasta water.
  • Toss the drained pappardelle through the ragù with a splash of pasta water to loosen it, then finish with Parmesan stirred through and extra shavings on top.
Chef's tip: resist the urge to simmer the sauce hard. A slow, gentle simmer with the lid slightly ajar breaks down the beef and vegetables without evaporating all the liquid too quickly, which is what gives a ragù its silky, rather than dry, texture.

This is a dish that improves on the second day, so it's worth making a double batch of the sauce and freezing half. A medium-bodied red — Chianti or Montepulciano — is the traditional match, since both have enough acidity to stand up to the tomato.

4. Spicy Tomato & Burrata Rigatoni

04Spicy Tomato & Burrata Rigatoni

Vegetarian · Serves 2 · 25 minutes

This is the loudest dish on the list, in the best sense — a sharp, chili-forward tomato sauce tamed at the last second by a whole ball of burrata melting into the centre of the bowl. Rigatoni's ridges and hollow centre are ideal here because they trap both the sauce and the pockets of cream that spill out of the burrata as it's torn open.

Ingredients

  • 200 g rigatoni
  • 250 g crushed tomatoes
  • 2 garlic cloves, thinly sliced
  • 1 tbsp olive oil
  • ½ tsp chili flakes (more, if you like real heat)
  • 1 ball burrata
  • Fresh basil leaves
  • Salt, to taste

Method

  • Cook the rigatoni in well-salted boiling water until al dente, reserving a cup of pasta water before draining.
  • While the pasta cooks, warm the olive oil in a pan over medium-low heat and gently cook the garlic and chili flakes until the garlic is fragrant but not browned — burnt garlic turns bitter and will dominate the whole dish.
  • Add the crushed tomatoes, season with salt, and simmer for about 15 minutes until the sauce thickens slightly and loses its raw, acidic edge.
  • Toss the drained rigatoni through the sauce with a splash of the reserved pasta water, adjusting the seasoning as needed.
  • Plate the pasta, tear the burrata open over the centre of each bowl so the cream spills naturally, and scatter with torn basil.
Chef's tip: add the burrata at the very last second, right before serving. It should still be cold and just-torn when it hits the hot pasta, so the contrast between the warm, spicy sauce and the cool, milky cheese is still there when it reaches the table.

If burrata isn't available, fresh mozzarella torn into pieces is a reasonable substitute, though it won't ooze in quite the same way. A light, chilled red like a Barbera works surprisingly well against the chili heat.

5. Lemon Parmesan Chicken Fettuccine

05Lemon Parmesan Chicken Fettuccine

Poultry · Serves 2 · 30 minutes

This is the most complete meal of the five, in that it delivers a full protein portion alongside the pasta rather than folding a smaller amount of protein through the sauce. The lemon keeps what would otherwise be a heavy cream-and-Parmesan sauce feeling bright, which is why it's a good choice for a dinner where you want something substantial without it feeling like too much.

Ingredients

  • 200 g fettuccine
  • 250 g chicken breast
  • 2 garlic cloves, finely chopped
  • 20 g butter
  • 100 ml cream
  • 40 g Parmesan, finely grated
  • Zest of ½ lemon
  • 1 tbsp lemon juice
  • Salt and black pepper, to taste

Method

  • Cook the fettuccine in well-salted boiling water until just short of al dente, then reserve a cup of pasta water and drain.
  • Season the chicken breast well on both sides. Sear it in a hot pan for four to five minutes per side, depending on thickness, until cooked through and golden. Rest it for a few minutes before slicing — this keeps it juicy rather than letting the juices run out the moment you cut into it.
  • In the same pan, lower the heat and cook the garlic gently in the butter for thirty seconds.
  • Add the cream and Parmesan, stirring until the cheese melts into a smooth sauce, then loosen it with a splash of the reserved pasta water.
  • Toss the drained fettuccine through the sauce, then stir in the lemon zest and juice off the heat.
  • Plate the pasta and top with the sliced chicken, finishing with a little extra Parmesan and lemon zest.
Chef's tip: add the lemon juice only after the pan is off the heat. Cooking cream and lemon juice together over direct heat risks the acid splitting the sauce; stirring it in at the end keeps everything smooth.

Resting the chicken before slicing is not optional here — it's the difference between clean slices on top of the pasta and a plate flooded with juices that thin out the sauce. A buttery, lightly oaked white like a Chardonnay complements the cream without overwhelming the lemon.

Getting Restaurant Results at Home

All five recipes share the same underlying technique, and once it's second nature you can apply it to almost any pasta dish, not just the ones above.

  • Salt the water properly. Pasta water should taste distinctly salty — close to seawater. This is the only chance the pasta itself gets to be seasoned from the inside, since sauce only coats the surface.
  • Undercook slightly, then finish in the sauce. Pulling the pasta a minute before it's fully al dente and finishing it in the pan with the sauce lets the pasta absorb flavour and lets its surface starch thicken the sauce naturally.
  • Always reserve pasta water. That cloudy, starchy water is what lets a sauce cling to pasta instead of pooling at the bottom of the bowl. Add it a tablespoon at a time until the sauce coats the back of a spoon.
  • Toss, don't just top. Combining pasta and sauce in the same pan over gentle heat, tossing to coat, produces a noticeably better result than plating pasta and spooning sauce over the top.
  • Finish delicate ingredients off the heat. Truffle oil, fresh herbs, lemon juice and burrata all lose something if they're cooked rather than folded in at the last moment. Add them after the pan comes off the stove.

A note on substitutions: any of these five will still work with a different pasta shape if that's what you have in the cupboard — long ribbon shapes (tagliatelle, fettuccine, pappardelle) are broadly interchangeable with each other, and short tubular shapes (rigatoni, penne, casarecce) are broadly interchangeable with each other. What matters more than the exact shape is matching a lighter, smoother sauce to a smoother pasta and a heartier, chunkier sauce to a more textured or ridged one, which is why the ragù works so well with pappardelle and the burrata sauce works so well with rigatoni's ridges.

Portioning is worth a word too. All five recipes here are written to serve two generously, using 200 g of dry pasta and roughly 250 g of protein where relevant. If you're cooking for a larger table, resist simply doubling everything in one pan — sauces reduce and proteins sear differently at larger volumes, and it's usually better to cook in two batches, or to scale the sauce components up by 1.5x per additional two servings rather than a flat multiple, since salt, acid and dairy don't always scale linearly with taste.
