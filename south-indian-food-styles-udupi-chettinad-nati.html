🍛 South Indian Food Styles:
Udupi, Chettinad, Nati & Beyond

A journey through the spice trails, temple kitchens, and village hearths of South India's most iconic culinary traditions.

✍️ By Nigel A Thomas 📅 June 2026 ⏱️ 12 min read 🌶️ South Indian Cuisine

South India is not one cuisine. It is a vast, living tapestry of food traditions that stretch across five states — Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Andhra Pradesh, and Telangana — each carrying centuries of history in every pot, every spice blend, and every technique passed down through generations. From the austere, sattvic purity of Udupi temple cooking to the fierce, complex heat of Chettinad kitchens, and the raw, rustic honesty of Nati-style village food, South Indian cuisine is one of the most diverse and profound food cultures on earth.

In this article, we travel through these remarkable food traditions — their origins, their defining ingredients, the fascinating stories behind them, and what makes each one utterly unique. Whether you are a food professional, a hospitality student, or simply someone who loves great food, this is a journey worth taking.

"South Indian food is not just about taste. It is about philosophy, climate, community, and the land itself. Every dish tells you where it came from and who made it."

🏛️ Udupi Cuisine — The Sacred Kitchen of Karnataka

History & Origins

Udupi cuisine takes its name from the temple town of Udupi in coastal Karnataka, home to the famous Sri Krishna Matha — a 13th-century Vaishnavite monastery established by the philosopher-saint Madhvacharya. The kitchen of this monastery, known as the Madhva Brahmin kitchen, became the birthplace of one of India's most disciplined and refined vegetarian food traditions.

For over 700 years, the cooks of Udupi — called Shivalli Brahmins — developed a cuisine governed by strict religious principles. No onion, no garlic, no meat, no fish. Instead, they mastered the art of coaxing extraordinary flavour from vegetables, lentils, rice, coconut, and a careful palette of spices. The result was a cuisine of astonishing depth and variety, achieved entirely without the shortcuts that most cuisines rely on.

Udupi cuisine entered the mainstream when enterprising Udupi Brahmins migrated to Mumbai and other Indian cities in the early 20th century and opened small, affordable vegetarian restaurants. The iconic Udupi restaurant — with its stainless steel plates, filter coffee, dosas, and sambar — became a beloved institution across India and later around the world.

🔍 Interesting Fact: The masala dosa — now one of India's most recognised dishes globally — is believed to have originated in the Udupi restaurant tradition. The crisp rice-and-lentil crepe filled with spiced potato was a humble temple food that became a global street food icon.

Defining Flavours & Techniques

Udupi food is characterised by a balance of sweet, sour, and spicy — rarely extreme in any direction. Coconut is the backbone of nearly every dish, appearing as fresh grated coconut, coconut milk, or coconut oil. Tamarind and raw mango provide sourness. Jaggery adds a gentle sweetness that lifts and rounds the flavours. The tempering — called tadka or oggarane — is done in pure coconut oil with mustard seeds, curry leaves, dried red chillies, and asafoetida.

Dishes like Kosambari (lentil salad), Gojju (tangy vegetable curry), Saaru (thin, pepper-laced rasam), Upkari (dry vegetable stir-fry), and Payasa (coconut milk dessert) form the core of a traditional Udupi meal. The food is served on a banana leaf in a specific order, reflecting the Ayurvedic principle that different tastes should be experienced in sequence for optimal digestion.

Fresh Coconut Coconut Oil Curry Leaves Mustard Seeds Tamarind Jaggery Asafoetida (Hing) Dried Red Chilli Urad Dal Turmeric Byadagi Chilli

🌶️ Chettinad Cuisine — The Spice Kingdom of Tamil Nadu

History & Origins

If Udupi cuisine is a whisper, Chettinad is a thunderclap. Originating from the Chettinad region of Tamil Nadu — a cluster of 75 villages in the Sivaganga district — this cuisine belongs to the Nattukotai Chettiars, a wealthy merchant and banking community whose trade networks stretched across Southeast Asia, Burma, Sri Lanka, and beyond.

From the 18th to the early 20th centuries, the Chettiars travelled extensively across Asia, and their cuisine reflects this global exposure. They brought back exotic spices — kalpasi (stone flower), marathi mokku (dried flower pods), star anise from China — that became signatures of Chettinad cooking. Their grand mansions, built with Burma teak and Italian marble, housed kitchens where elaborate multi-course non-vegetarian feasts were prepared for festivals, weddings, and community gatherings.

Chettinad cuisine is famous for being one of the spiciest in India — but it is not spicy in a crude, one-dimensional way. The heat in Chettinad cooking is layered, aromatic, and deeply complex, built from a masala that can contain up to 25 different spices ground fresh for each dish.

🔍 Interesting Fact: Chettinad cuisine uses kalpasi — a dried lichen found on rocks and tree bark — as a flavouring agent. This unusual ingredient, rarely found in any other Indian cuisine, gives Chettinad gravies a deep, earthy, almost mushroom-like undertone. It is sometimes called "stone flower" and is irreplaceable in an authentic Chettinad masala.

Defining Flavours & Techniques

The Chettinad kitchen begins with freshly ground spice pastes — nothing comes from a packet. Whole spices are dry-roasted and ground each morning. Meats — typically country chicken (nattu kozhi), lamb, quail, or crab — are marinated in these pastes and slow-cooked in clay pots, which impart a distinctive earthiness to the finished dish.

Signature dishes include Chettinad Chicken Curry, Kavuni Arisi (black rice pudding), Meen Kuzhambu (fish curry), Idiyappam (string hoppers served with coconut milk), and Kavuni Arisi Payasam. The base for most gravies is a combination of shallots, tomatoes, and freshly grated coconut, with the spice paste forming the aromatic core. Gingelly (sesame) oil is the preferred cooking medium, adding its own nutty character.

What sets Chettinad apart is also its use of vethal kuzhambu — sun-dried vegetables and meats that are rehydrated and cooked during lean seasons. This tradition of preservation speaks to the merchant community's need to sustain themselves during long travels.

🌿 Key Spices & Bases — Chettinad

Kalpasi (Stone Flower) Marathi Mokku Star Anise Kunjipodi (Black Pepper) Gundu Chilli Gingelly Oil Shallots Fresh Coconut Fennel Seeds Cinnamon Cloves Dried Mango

🌾 Nati Style — The Soul Food of South India

History & Origins

Nati is a Kannada word meaning "native" or "local" — and Nati-style cooking is exactly that: the unpolished, honest, deeply flavourful food of the South Indian village. It is not the food of temples or merchant mansions. It is the food of farmers, shepherds, and fishing communities — people who cooked with whatever the land gave them that day, without waste, without ceremony, but with instinctive wisdom.

Nati cuisine is finding a powerful revival today as urban South Indians reconnect with their roots. Restaurants across Bengaluru, Chennai, and Hyderabad now proudly serve Nati menus — featuring country chicken (nattu kozhi), free-range eggs, river fish, red sorghum (jolada roti), and foraged greens — and charge premium prices for the authenticity that was once considered "poor man's food."

The philosophy of Nati cooking is zero waste. Chicken feet, liver, gizzard, and head — all go into the pot. Tender drumstick leaves, wild sorrel, banana flowers, and jackfruit seeds are staple ingredients. The cooking is done over wood fire in iron vessels, and this technique — the smoke, the slow heat, the seasoned iron — is considered inseparable from the final flavour.

🔍 Interesting Fact: Country chicken (nattu kozhi) takes significantly longer to cook than broiler chicken — sometimes up to two hours — but its flavour is incomparably richer. The free-range birds develop firm, flavourful meat from their active lifestyle, and Nati cooks say no amount of spice can replicate what the bird itself brings to the dish.

Defining Flavours & Techniques

Nati cooking is bold and unapologetic. The spice levels are high, the portions are generous, and the flavours are unfiltered. A Nati chicken curry is typically dry or semi-dry, coated in a thick paste of roasted spices, garlic, and shallots, cooked until the oil separates and the masala clings to every piece of meat. The result is intensely aromatic and deeply satisfying.

Jolada roti (sorghum flatbread), ragi mudde (finger millet balls), and akki roti (rice flatbread) are the traditional accompaniments — heavy, nutritious, and perfectly designed to hold up against robust, spicy curries. Saaru (thin spiced broth), palya (dry vegetable stir-fry), and mosaru (fresh curd) complete the Nati meal.

Nati cooking also has a strong tradition of using medicinal plants and ingredients — betel leaves, moringa, turmeric root, wild turmeric (kasturi manjal) — that double as flavour agents and health tonics. This reflects an ancient understanding of food as medicine, long before the concept became fashionable in modern wellness circles.

🌿 Key Spices & Bases — Nati Style

Coarse Black Pepper Dried Red Chilli Shallots Garlic Ginger Coconut Oil / Groundnut Oil Curry Leaves Coriander Seeds Wild Turmeric Tamarind Drumstick Leaves Ragi (Finger Millet)

🥥 Kerala Cuisine — Where the Coconut Reigns Supreme

History & Origins

Kerala's food story is inseparable from its geography — a narrow strip of land between the Western Ghats and the Arabian Sea, blessed with backwaters, paddy fields, spice gardens, and one of the most biodiverse coastlines in Asia. Kerala was the original spice capital of the ancient world. Black pepper, cardamom, cloves, and cinnamon from Kerala's hills drew Arab, Chinese, Portuguese, Dutch, and British traders for over two millennia — and each left their mark on the local cuisine.

The Syrian Christian community of Kerala — descendants of converts from the 1st century AD — developed one of the most celebrated non-vegetarian traditions in India, with dishes like Duck Mappas, Pork Vindaloo (adapted from Portuguese influence), and Beef Fry. The Muslim Moplah community contributed Malabar Biryani and rich, aromatic meat preparations. And the Hindu Nair and Brahmin communities developed an elaborate vegetarian tradition of Sadhya — the grand feast served on banana leaf for festivals.

🔍 Interesting Fact: Kerala's Sadhya — the traditional vegetarian feast served at Onam — can feature up to 28 dishes served simultaneously on a single banana leaf, eaten in a specific sequence. It is considered one of the most elaborate single-sitting vegetarian meals in the world.

Defining Flavours & Techniques

Coconut is the soul of Kerala cooking — fresh coconut, coconut milk (first and second press), toasted coconut, and coconut oil all appear in different preparations. Kerala curries tend to be milder than Tamil or Andhra preparations, relying more on aromatic warmth than fiery heat. Kodampuli (Malabar tamarind / Gamboge) — a dark, intensely sour dried fruit unique to Kerala — is used in fish curries and gives them an unmistakable tartness.

Appam with stew, Puttu with kadala curry, Kerala Prawn Curry, Karimeen Pollichathu (pearl spot fish baked in banana leaf), and Payasam in its many forms define this cuisine. Cooking is done in manchatti — traditional clay pots — which regulate heat beautifully and give the curries their characteristic depth.

🌿 Key Spices & Bases — Kerala

Coconut Milk Kodampuli Cardamom Cloves Black Pepper Coconut Oil Curry Leaves Shallots Ginger Green Chilli Fenugreek

🔥 Andhra & Telangana Cuisine — Fire on the Plate

History & Origins

If you are looking for the hottest food in India, you will find it in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. These two states — once united, separated in 2014 — share a culinary tradition that is legendary for its ferocious use of chilli. The Guntur Sannam chilli, grown in Guntur district of Andhra Pradesh, is one of the most traded chilli varieties in the world and forms the heat backbone of this cuisine.

Andhra cuisine was shaped by several ruling dynasties — the Satavahanas, the Kakatiyas, the Vijayanagara Empire, and later the Nizams (in the Hyderabad region). The Nizam's influence brought rich Mughlai elements into Hyderabadi cooking, giving rise to the world-famous Hyderabadi Biryani — a dish that stands apart from all other biryanis for its dum cooking technique and the use of kacche gosht (raw marinated meat layered with partially cooked rice).

🔍 Interesting Fact: Andhra Pradesh is the largest producer and exporter of chillies in India, accounting for nearly 50% of the country's total chilli production. The Guntur chilli market is one of the largest spice markets in Asia.

Defining Flavours & Techniques

Andhra food is built on three pillars: chilli, tamarind, and rice. Gongura — a sour leafy green (sorrel) — is the defining ingredient of Andhra cuisine, used in chutneys, pickles, and meat preparations. Gongura Mutton is considered the signature dish of the region. Pesarattu (green moong dal crepes), Pulusu (tamarind-based stew), Chegodilu (fried snack), and Pootharekulu (paper-thin sweet) round out the repertoire.

Telangana cuisine, while related, has its own distinct character — heavier use of jowar (sorghum), more robust meat preparations, and a stronger tradition of roti rather than rice as the staple. The Sarvapindi (rice flour pancake) and Sakinalu (sesame rice snack) are Telangana specialities rarely found elsewhere.

🌿 Key Spices & Bases — Andhra & Telangana

Guntur Chilli Tamarind Gongura (Sorrel) Mustard Seeds Fenugreek Curry Leaves Sesame Seeds Peanuts Groundnut Oil Dried Red Chilli Asafoetida

🍽️ What Unites South Indian Cuisine

Despite their remarkable diversity, South Indian cuisines share certain foundational threads. Rice is the universal staple — whether as steamed rice, fermented batter for idlis and dosas, or ground into flatbreads. Lentils appear in some form in almost every meal. The tadka — that sizzling tempering of mustard seeds, curry leaves, and dried chilli in hot oil — is the opening note of nearly every South Indian dish, announcing what is to come. And coconut, in one form or another, weaves through the entire tradition from Karnataka to Kerala and Tamil Nadu.

South Indian cooking is also deeply Ayurvedic in its instincts — even when cooks don't consciously think about it. The use of tamarind to aid digestion, turmeric as an anti-inflammatory, pepper to stimulate metabolism, and curry leaves for their antioxidant properties reflects a food culture where health and flavour have always been considered the same thing.

What is particularly exciting today is that South Indian cuisine is experiencing a global renaissance. From fine dining restaurants in London and New York serving elevated Chettinad tasting menus, to YouTube channels celebrating Nati village cooking with millions of followers, the world is finally beginning to discover the extraordinary depth that South India's kitchens have always held.

"The greatest cuisines in the world are not the ones with the most expensive ingredients. They are the ones with the deepest roots — and South India's roots go down to the very beginning of civilisation itself."

— Nigel A Thomas, Hospitality Trainer

Whether you are a chef seeking inspiration, a food professional building a menu, or a traveller planning your next culinary adventure — South India's food traditions offer a lifetime of discovery. Start with a masala dosa and a cup of filter coffee. Let the journey begin.
