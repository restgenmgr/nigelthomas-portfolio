10 Stuffed & Filled Foods From Around the World

A hospitality executive's field guide to the dough-wrapped, filling-packed dishes that show up on menus everywhere — what to call them, where they come from, and how they're cooked.

One Dough, Endless Possibilities

Nigel Anthony Thomas
Hospitality Executive & Corporate Trainer • CHS, ServSafe Food Protection Manager • 30+ Years Across Hotels, Resorts & Cruise Lines

Almost every food culture in the world has its own answer to the same basic idea: wrap something delicious inside a dough, pastry or wrapper, then steam, boil, fry or bake it. On a global menu, in a training kitchen, or simply on a cruise buffet, being able to place a dish — where it's from, how it's cooked, what it's meant to taste like — is a small piece of product knowledge that pays off constantly in guest-facing hospitality work.

Below is a quick-reference poster covering ten of the most recognisable stuffed and filled foods on menus today, followed by a short note on each.

Ten stuffed & filled foods from around the world — a quick-reference guide for hospitality menus.

Why This Matters on a Hospitality Menu

Guests increasingly expect global menus to use the right names, and to get them right. Calling a pierogi a "dumpling" isn't wrong exactly, but it flattens a dish with its own dough, filling logic and cooking method into a generic catch-all. The same goes for training new F&B staff — knowing that a samosa is a stuffed pastry rather than a dumpling, or that chimaki is a wrapped rice food rather than a dough-wrapped dish, changes how it should be plated, held and served.

The Ten, at a Glance

  • Momo (Nepal/Tibet) — steamed or fried, usually chicken, vegetable or minced meat, served with spicy tomato chutney.
  • Gyoza (Japan) — Japan's take on Chinese jiaozi, pork/cabbage/garlic/ginger, pan-fried on one side and steamed to finish.
  • Manti (Turkey/Central Asia) — small dumplings of minced meat and onion, served with yogurt and paprika butter.
  • Dumpling (worldwide) — the broad category name for any dough wrapped around a filling; every region has its own version.
  • Ravioli (Italy) — Italian stuffed pasta, not a traditional Asian-style dumpling, served with sauce rather than steamed or fried.
  • Wonton (China) — thin dough wrapper filled with pork, shrimp or vegetables; boiled, steamed or deep-fried.
  • Samosa (South Asia) — crispy stuffed pastry with spiced potato and peas, traditionally deep-fried.
  • Pierogi (Poland) — unleavened dough dumplings, potato-and-cheese or sauerkraut-and-mushroom, boiled and sometimes finished pan-fried.
  • Chimaki (Japan) — rice wrapped in bamboo leaves and steamed, associated with Japanese festivals.
  • Empanada (Spain/Latin America) — filled pastry, baked or fried, with beef, chicken, cheese or seafood.
From the training room

When I run product-knowledge sessions for F&B teams, I use exactly this kind of quick-reference format — country, cooking method, type — because it's how staff actually remember it under service pressure. A one-line "steamed, Nepal, dumpling" sticks far better than a paragraph of history.

The Bottom Line

None of these dishes are complicated to explain individually. What makes them worth knowing as a set is the pattern: dough or pastry, a filling, a cooking method, and a serving tradition — repeated with local variation across nearly every food culture on earth. One dough, endless possibilities.
