Frozen Desserts The Complete Gelato, Ice Cream, Sherbet & Sorbet Guide

Four styles, one freezer case. What every pastry chef, server and F&B leader should know about the frozen desserts that anchor almost every dessert menu in the world.

By Nigel A. Thomas October 2026 ~18 min read Pastry & Dessert Reference

Ask a server to explain the difference between gelato and ice cream and most will say "gelato is Italian" and stop there. Ask a pastry commis why sherbet contains dairy while sorbet does not, or why a properly made sorbet should taste slightly softer straight from the freezer than a gelato, and the kitchen usually goes quiet. Frozen desserts are treated as interchangeable in a lot of training programs, when in reality they are four distinct categories with different bases, different textures, different service temperatures, and different guest expectations. Getting them confused behind a dessert station is the fastest way to look undertrained in front of a guest who orders sorbet for dietary reasons.

This guide covers the four frozen dessert styles that make up the overwhelming majority of any dessert menu: gelato, ice cream, sherbet and sorbet. For each style you get the base composition, the dairy status, the air content and its effect on texture, and the service context that actually matters on a working floor - not just a definition, but the reasoning a trained pastry team carries in their head. It closes with a comparison table, a service temperature guide, a training framework, and answers to the questions that come up most often behind a real dessert station.

"A guest doesn't remember that you served a good sorbet. They remember that you didn't have to check whether it contained dairy."

01 Why Four Styles Is the Right Mental Model

Every frozen dessert menu, no matter how creative or how large, is built on the same four styles: gelato, ice cream, sherbet and sorbet. Organising frozen dessert knowledge around these four categories rather than around individual flavours - pistachio, vanilla, mango, raspberry - is what separates a pastry team that has memorised a menu from one that actually understands the category. Once a team understands what each style brings to the bowl - dense silkiness, rich creaminess, fruity tang, or bright refreshment - they can improvise substitutions, explain texture and dietary differences to guests, and recommend confidently instead of defaulting to whatever is easiest to describe.

This is also why the four sections below are structured the same way every time: a short character description of the style, followed by the key technical variables that define it. The same underlying framework - base, dairy, air, texture - appears again and again once you see it, and that repetition is exactly what makes this material trainable in a single shift rather than over months. 02 Gelato

Gelato is dense, smooth and intensely flavoured, built on a milk-led base rather than a cream-led one. It is the Italian frozen dessert tradition at its most refined, and its defining characteristic is low overrun - the amount of air incorporated during freezing. Where ice cream might be half air by volume, a well-made gelato is closer to twenty to thirty percent air, which is why a scoop of gelato feels heavier, melts more slowly, and delivers a more concentrated hit of flavour than the same volume of ice cream.

The milk-led base is the other critical distinction. Gelato uses more milk and less cream than ice cream, which lowers the fat content and allows the flavour of the base ingredient - pistachio, stracciatella, coffee - to come through more clearly. The texture is best described as silky and dense rather than fluffy, and it is typically served at a slightly warmer temperature than ice cream, around minus twelve to minus fourteen degrees Celsius, because the lower fat content means it would be rock-hard and unappealing at a colder service temperature.

In a professional service context, gelato is the style most likely to be served in a display case rather than scooped from a deep freezer, and it is the style where visual presentation - the swirl of a spatula, the peak of a scoop - matters most. Service staff should know that gelato contains dairy, which makes it unsuitable for vegan guests, and that its lower fat content makes it a reasonable recommendation for guests who find ice cream too rich.

03 Ice Cream

Ice cream is rich, airy and creamy, built on a cream-led base that gives it its characteristic mouthfeel. It is the frozen dessert style most guests are familiar with, and that familiarity is precisely why it is so often taken for granted in training. The cream-led base means higher fat content than gelato, and the higher fat content supports more air incorporation - what the industry calls overrun - which produces the light, scoopable texture that defines the style.

Overrun in ice cream is often much higher than in gelato, sometimes reaching fifty percent or more, which is why a litre of ice cream weighs less than a litre of gelato and why ice cream feels less dense on the palate. This is not a defect; it is the defining characteristic of the style. The air is what makes ice cream scoopable straight from a domestic freezer and what gives it the familiar fluffy texture that guests expect. A properly made ice cream should be rich but not heavy, sweet but balanced, and smooth without the gumminess that comes from over-stabilisation.

Ice cream is the style most likely to be served as a base for sundaes, floats and other composite desserts, precisely because its airy texture holds toppings well and its neutral creaminess pairs with almost any sauce or garnish. Service staff should know that ice cream contains dairy and, depending on the recipe, may contain eggs - the "custard base" or "French style" ice cream that is richer and smoother than the "Philadelphia style" made without eggs.

04 Sherbet

Sherbet occupies the middle ground between sorbet and ice cream, and it is the style most often confused with sorbet by guests and undertrained staff alike. The defining characteristic of sherbet is that it contains both fruit and dairy - typically milk or cream, sometimes egg white - in a proportion that produces a silky, fruity texture that is neither as rich as ice cream nor as lean as sorbet. The dairy content is usually lower than in ice cream, often in the range of one to two percent milkfat, which keeps the fruit flavour dominant while still providing the creaminess that distinguishes sherbet from sorbet.

The texture of sherbet is best described as silky and fruity, with a lighter body than ice cream and a smoother, more integrated mouthfeel than sorbet. The fruit content can be quite high, and in a well-made sherbet the fruit should taste fresh and present rather than cooked or artificial. The dairy is there to soften the edges of the fruit's acidity and to provide a creamy counterpoint, not to dominate the flavour profile.

In a service context, sherbet is the style most likely to be recommended to guests who want something fruity but find sorbet too sharp or too icy. It is also the style that causes the most dietary confusion - guests who avoid dairy will sometimes order sherbet assuming it is dairy-free, which is why staff training on the dairy content of each style is not optional. A guest who orders sherbet for a dairy allergy and receives a milk-based product is a serious service failure, not a minor mix-up.

05 Sorbet

Sorbet is bright, refreshing and entirely dairy-free, built on a base of fruit and water - sometimes with wine, liqueur or other alcohol - and stabilised with sugar syrup. It is the oldest of the four styles in historical terms, and it remains the most versatile from a dietary perspective because it contains no dairy and can be made vegan without modification. The absence of dairy is not a limitation; it is the defining characteristic of the style, and it produces a texture that is icier and more crystalline than the other three categories.

The texture of sorbet is best described as bright and refreshing, with a clean, sharp finish that clears the palate between courses. A well-made sorbet should be smooth enough to scoop but should retain a slightly icy bite that distinguishes it from sherbet. The fruit flavour should be pure and intense, and the sugar level should be calibrated to the acidity of the fruit - a lemon sorbet needs more sugar than a mango sorbet, and a sorbet that is under-sweetened will taste harsh rather than refreshing.

Sorbet is the style most likely to be served as a palate cleanser between courses in fine dining, precisely because its clean, dairy-free profile does not coat the mouth or interfere with the flavours of the following course. It is also the style that requires the most careful service temperature management - sorbet served too cold will be hard and flavourless, while sorbet served too warm will collapse into a puddle. The ideal service temperature for sorbet is around minus ten to minus twelve degrees Celsius, slightly warmer than gelato, because the absence of fat means it needs to be warmer to achieve the right texture.

07 Overrun - Why Air Content Matters

Overrun is the industry term for the volume gained from air incorporated during freezing, and it is the single technical variable that most clearly separates these four styles. A frozen dessert with low overrun - gelato, typically - is dense and intensely flavoured. A frozen dessert with high overrun - ice cream, typically - is light and airy, with a texture that feels more generous in the bowl even though it contains less actual solids per litre.

Understanding overrun matters in service because it affects portion perception. A guest who orders gelato and receives a smaller-looking scoop than they would get with ice cream may feel short-changed, even though the gelato scoop contains more actual dessert by weight. Training staff to explain this - "gelato is denser, so the portion looks smaller but eats richer" - turns a potential complaint into a moment of expertise. It also matters in cost control: a kitchen that over-aerates its gelato to stretch volume is producing a product that will melt too quickly, taste watery, and fail on the plate.

The poster notes that styles vary by recipe and equipment, and this is worth repeating in training. A domestic ice cream maker cannot achieve the same overrun as a commercial continuous freezer, and a sorbet made without a machine will have a coarser, more crystalline texture than one made with proper agitation. Kitchen teams should calibrate their expectations to their equipment rather than chasing a texture their setup cannot produce.

08 Service Temperature Guide

Temperature is the most common source of frozen dessert complaints, and it is entirely within a kitchen's control. Serving any of these styles too cold produces a hard, flavourless product that guests cannot eat comfortably. Serving them too warm produces a melting puddle that arrives at the table already lost.

  • Gelato: serve at −12°C to −14°C. The lower fat content means it needs to be warmer than ice cream to achieve the right texture
  • Ice cream: serve at −16°C to −18°C. The higher fat content supports a colder service temperature without becoming unpleasantly hard
  • Sherbet: serve at −12°C to −14°C. Similar to gelato, the dairy content is low enough that it needs warmth to scoop properly
  • Sorbet: serve at −10°C to −12°C. The absence of fat means sorbet must be served warmer than the other styles or it will be icy and flavourless
  • All styles: temper in the refrigerator for 10-15 minutes before scooping rather than leaving at room temperature, which causes uneven melting
  • Palate cleanser sorbet: serve slightly warmer still, around −8°C to −10°C, so it melts quickly on the tongue and clears the palate without chilling the mouth

09 Training Framework for Pastry & Service Teams

This is a genuinely high-return training topic precisely because guests interact with frozen desserts constantly, and because dietary questions about dairy content come up almost every service. A few practical steps that work well when standardising this material across a team:

  • Group training by the four styles, not by individual flavours - it builds transferable understanding rather than rote memorisation of a menu
  • Have new staff taste all four styles side by side during onboarding, focusing on texture differences rather than flavour, so the distinctions become physical rather than theoretical
  • Brief service staff specifically on the dairy status of each style, since sherbet is the most common source of dietary confusion
  • Pair temperature training with the service guide so staff understand that the same freezer cannot serve all four styles correctly without adjustment
  • Reinforce portion consistency using scales rather than scoops for gelato and sorbet, where density varies more than in ice cream
  • Keep a copy of the frozen desserts poster at the dessert station for fast reference during service

The Real Test

If a server can name a frozen dessert's dairy status and texture profile in one breath, they can answer dietary questions confidently, describe the dessert accurately to a guest, and recommend the right style for the right palate - that is the entire point of organising frozen dessert knowledge this way.

10 Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between gelato and ice cream?

Gelato is milk-led with lower fat content and lower overrun, producing a denser, more intensely flavoured dessert. Ice cream is cream-led with higher fat content and often higher overrun, producing a lighter, airier texture. Both contain dairy, but they are served at different temperatures and have distinctly different mouthfeels.

Does sherbet contain dairy?

Yes. Sherbet contains both fruit and dairy - typically milk or cream - in a proportion that produces a silky, fruity texture. This is the key distinction between sherbet and sorbet, which is entirely dairy-free. Service staff must know this distinction because guests who avoid dairy will sometimes order sherbet assuming it is dairy-free.

Why does sorbet need to be served warmer than ice cream?

Sorbet contains no fat, and fat is what allows ice cream to remain scoopable at colder temperatures. Without fat, sorbet becomes hard and icy at cold service temperatures, so it must be served warmer - around −10°C to −12°C - to achieve the right texture and release its flavour.

Is gelato healthier than ice cream?

Gelato typically has lower fat content than ice cream because it uses more milk and less cream, but it often contains more sugar to compensate for the lower fat. "Healthier" is not the right framing; they are different styles with different nutritional profiles. The relevant question for service is dietary restriction - both contain dairy - and portion size, which is affected by density differences.

Can sorbet be made without an ice cream machine?

Yes, but the texture will be coarser and more crystalline than sorbet made with proper agitation. Without a machine, the mixture must be frozen in a shallow container and stirred periodically to break up ice crystals. The result is still delicious but will not achieve the smooth texture of machine-churned sorbet.

Four styles, four textures, one underlying framework - none of it is trivia once a guest is seated at your table asking whether the sorbet is dairy-free or why the gelato portion looks smaller than the ice cream. Building this into onboarding costs a single structured tasting session and pays back every time a server answers a dietary question without hesitation or gets a dessert recommendation exactly right on the first attempt.
