🍲 The 5 Mother Sauces: Complete Professional Guide with Recipes, Procedures & Variants

📅 June 12, 2026 | 👤 Nigel Thomas | ⏱ 15 min read | 🍽️ Classical Cuisine & F&B Operations

In the world of professional cooking, mastering the five Mother Sauces is the single most important foundation a chef can build. Codified by the legendary French chef Auguste Escoffier in the early 20th century — building on the earlier work of Marie-Antoine Carême — the Mother Sauces are not merely recipes. They are a system. A grammar of cooking from which hundreds of classical and modern sauces are derived.

Understanding Mother Sauces transforms a cook into a chef. Once you understand the underlying structure of each sauce — its thickening agent, its liquid base, its flavor profile — you gain the ability to improvise, adapt, and create. Every fine dining kitchen in the world operates on this foundation. Every culinary school begins here.

This comprehensive guide covers all five Mother Sauces in professional detail: the full recipe, step-by-step procedure, common mistakes to avoid, and the most important derivative (small) sauces born from each one.

📌 What Are the Mother Sauces?

The five Mother Sauces — also called grandes sauces or leading sauces — are the five fundamental sauce preparations from which all classical European sauces are derived. Each has a distinct thickening agent and liquid base:

Mother Sauce Liquid Base Thickening Agent Color
Bechamel Whole Milk White Roux White/Cream
Veloute White Stock (chicken/veal/fish) Blonde Roux Pale Ivory
Espagnole Brown Veal/Beef Stock Brown Roux Deep Brown
Hollandaise Clarified Butter Egg Yolk Emulsion Pale Yellow
Tomate Tomatoes + Stock Natural reduction Deep Red
💡 Historical Note: Some culinary schools list Mayonnaise as a sixth Mother Sauce (a cold emulsion sauce). Others include Sauce Allemande as a variant of Veloute. In the Escoffier system, the five above are universally accepted as the classical Mother Sauces.

1 Sauce Bechamel — The White Sauce

🥛 What is Bechamel?

Bechamel is the simplest and most widely used of all the Mother Sauces. Named after Louis de Bechamel, a French courtier, it is a smooth, creamy white sauce made from a white roux enriched with hot milk. It is the foundation of countless comfort foods — from lasagne and moussaka to macaroni and cheese, creamed vegetables, and souffles.

In Indian hotel kitchens, Bechamel is used extensively in continental dishes, pasta bakes, gratins, and as the base for cream soups.

📝 Classic Bechamel — Recipe (Yields 1 Litre)

Ingredients:

  • 60g unsalted butter
  • 60g plain flour (all-purpose)
  • 1 litre full-fat milk (hot)
  • 1 small onion, peeled and halved
  • 2 cloves (studded into the onion)
  • 1 bay leaf
  • Pinch of freshly grated nutmeg
  • Salt and white pepper to taste

Procedure:

  1. Infuse the milk: Place the milk in a saucepan with the clove-studded onion and bay leaf. Heat gently until just below boiling. Remove from heat, cover, and allow to infuse for 15 minutes. Strain and keep hot.
  2. Make the white roux: In a heavy-bottomed saucepan, melt butter over medium heat. Add flour all at once and stir vigorously with a wooden spoon or whisk for 1-2 minutes until the mixture smells slightly nutty but has not taken on any color. This is your white roux.
  3. Add the milk gradually: Remove the pan from heat. Add the hot infused milk in a slow, steady stream, whisking constantly to prevent lumps. Start with a small amount, whisk until smooth, then add more.
  4. Return to heat and simmer: Return the pan to medium heat. Continue stirring until the sauce comes to a gentle boil and thickens — approximately 8-10 minutes. Reduce heat and simmer for a further 5 minutes to cook out the flour taste completely.
  5. Season: Add nutmeg, salt, and white pepper. Pass through a fine sieve for a perfectly smooth finish.

Consistency Guide: Thin (coating consistency) for soups and creamed dishes; Medium for pasta sauces and gratins; Thick (panada) for souffles and croquettes.

⚠️ Chef's Tip: Always add HOT milk to the roux — cold milk causes lumps. If lumps do form, pass through a fine sieve or use a hand blender. Never stop stirring during the cooking process.

🔀 Bechamel Derivative Sauces

  • Cream Sauce (Sauce Creme): Bechamel + heavy cream reduced together. Richer, silkier texture. Used for poultry, veal, and vegetables.
  • Mornay Sauce: Bechamel + grated Gruyere and Parmesan cheese. The classic sauce for Croque Monsieur, cauliflower gratin, and fish gratins.
  • Nantua Sauce: Bechamel + crayfish butter + cream. A luxurious sauce for seafood dishes and quenelles.
  • Soubise Sauce: Bechamel + finely sweated onion puree. Mild, sweet, and creamy — perfect for lamb, veal, and egg dishes.
  • Mustard Sauce: Bechamel + Dijon mustard + cream. Excellent with grilled fish, pork, and ham.
  • Aurora Sauce: Bechamel + tomato puree. Pale pink, slightly tangy — used for eggs, chicken, and pasta.

2 Sauce Veloute — The Velvet Sauce

🍗 What is Veloute?

Veloute (from the French word for "velvety") is a light, elegant sauce made by thickening a pale white stock — chicken, veal, or fish — with a blonde roux. It is a more refined and complex sauce than Bechamel, carrying the deep savory flavors of the stock. The quality of your stock directly determines the quality of your Veloute.

📝 Chicken Veloute — Recipe (Yields 1 Litre)

Ingredients:

  • 60g unsalted butter
  • 60g plain flour
  • 1.2 litres hot chicken stock (white, well-skimmed)
  • Salt and white pepper to taste
  • Bouquet garni (thyme, bay leaf, parsley stalks)

Procedure:

  1. Make blonde roux: Melt butter over medium heat. Add flour and cook, stirring constantly, for 3-4 minutes until the roux turns a pale straw/blonde color with a light nutty aroma. This is slightly more cooked than a white roux.
  2. Add hot stock: Remove from heat and gradually whisk in the hot chicken stock, starting slowly to prevent lumps. Add the bouquet garni.
  3. Simmer and skim: Return to medium heat, bring to a gentle simmer, and cook for 20-25 minutes, stirring regularly. Skim any impurities or fat that rise to the surface — this is critical for a clear, clean Veloute.
  4. Strain and season: Pass through a fine chinois (conical sieve). Season with salt and white pepper. The sauce should coat the back of a spoon.

Note: Fish Veloute uses fish stock; Veal Veloute uses veal stock. The procedure is identical.

⚠️ Chef's Tip: Skimming is not optional — it is essential. An unskimmed Veloute will be cloudy, greasy, and bitter. Skim every 5 minutes during the simmering process for a clean, glossy sauce.

🔀 Veloute Derivative Sauces

  • Sauce Supreme: Chicken Veloute + heavy cream, reduced until silky and glossy. The most elegant and widely used derivative. Pairs with poached chicken, vol-au-vents, and pasta.
  • Sauce Allemande (German Sauce): Veal Veloute + egg yolk liaison + lemon juice + cream. Rich, tangy, and luxurious. Used for veal, poultry, and mushroom dishes.
  • Sauce Ivoire: Supreme sauce + meat glaze (glace de viande). Ivory colored, deeply savory — for roasted poultry.
  • Sauce Mushroom: Chicken Veloute + sauteed mushrooms + cream. Classic accompaniment for chicken and veal escalopes.
  • Bercy Sauce: Fish Veloute + shallots + white wine + parsley. The definitive sauce for grilled and poached fish.
  • Normande Sauce: Fish Veloute + mushroom cooking liquor + oyster liquor + egg yolk + cream. Rich and complex — a classic of Normandy cuisine.
  • Hungarian Sauce: Chicken Veloute + onions + paprika + white wine. Vibrant and aromatic — for chicken and veal.

3 Sauce Espagnole — The Brown Sauce

🥩 What is Espagnole?

Espagnole is the most complex and labor-intensive of all the Mother Sauces. A rich, deeply flavored brown sauce made from a brown roux, mirepoix (aromatic vegetables), tomato puree, and brown veal stock, it is the foundation of the classic French brown sauce tradition. Its most important derivative — Demi-Glace — is considered one of the greatest achievements of classical French cuisine.

📝 Sauce Espagnole — Recipe (Yields 1 Litre)

Ingredients:

  • 60g clarified butter or lard
  • 60g plain flour
  • 1.5 litres brown veal stock (hot)
  • 150g mirepoix (75g onion, 40g carrot, 35g celery — roughly diced)
  • 100g tomato puree
  • 2 ripe tomatoes, roughly chopped
  • Bouquet garni (thyme, bay leaf, parsley, peppercorns)
  • Salt to taste

Procedure:

  1. Brown the mirepoix: In a heavy-bottomed pot, heat the butter over medium-high heat. Add the mirepoix and cook, stirring occasionally, until deeply caramelized and golden brown — approximately 15 minutes. Do not rush this step; the color develops the flavor.
  2. Make brown roux: Add the flour to the browned mirepoix and stir constantly over medium heat for 5-8 minutes until the roux turns a rich chestnut brown color. This is your brown roux — it must be properly colored for the sauce to have depth.
  3. Add tomato: Stir in the tomato puree and chopped tomatoes. Cook for 3-4 minutes, stirring, until the tomato darkens slightly (this is called "pincer" — cooking out the raw tomato taste).
  4. Add stock and bouquet garni: Gradually whisk in the hot brown stock. Add the bouquet garni. Bring to a gentle simmer.
  5. Long slow simmer: Simmer over very low heat for 45-60 minutes, skimming frequently. The sauce will reduce and deepen in color and flavor.
  6. Strain: Pass through a fine chinois. The finished Espagnole should be a glossy, deep mahogany brown sauce with a rich, savory aroma.
⚠️ Chef's Tip: A true Espagnole is a two-day process in classical kitchens — the sauce is made, cooled overnight, skimmed of fat, then reduced further. Don't skip the long simmer; this is what develops the complex depth of flavor.

🔀 Espagnole Derivative Sauces

  • Demi-Glace: Espagnole + equal quantity of brown stock, reduced by half until syrupy and intensely flavored. The queen of all brown sauces — the base for almost all classical brown sauce derivatives. Rich, glossy, and deeply savory.
  • Sauce Robert: Demi-Glace + white wine + onions + Dijon mustard. A classic sauce for pork and grilled meats.
  • Sauce Charcutiere: Sauce Robert + julienned gherkins. Traditionally served with pork chops.
  • Sauce Chasseur (Hunter's Sauce): Demi-Glace + mushrooms + shallots + white wine + tomato + tarragon. One of the most popular French bistro sauces — for chicken, veal, and rabbit.
  • Sauce Bourguignon: Demi-Glace + red Burgundy wine + mushrooms + pearl onions + bacon lardons. The sauce of Beef Bourguignon.
  • Sauce Madeira: Demi-Glace + Madeira wine, reduced. For beef tenderloin, ham, and foie gras.
  • Sauce Perigueux: Madeira sauce + truffle essence + diced truffles. The ultimate luxury sauce for beef and foie gras.
  • Sauce Poivrade: Demi-Glace + crushed peppercorns + vinegar + mirepoix. The classic game sauce.
  • Sauce Diable (Devil's Sauce): Demi-Glace + white wine + shallots + cayenne. Spicy and pungent — for grilled poultry and devilled kidneys.

4 Sauce Hollandaise — The Emulsion Sauce

🥚 What is Hollandaise?

Hollandaise is the most technically demanding of the five Mother Sauces — and arguably the most celebrated. A warm emulsion sauce made by slowly incorporating clarified butter into egg yolks whisked over gentle heat, it produces a rich, buttery, slightly tangy sauce with a smooth, creamy texture that coats everything it touches in luxury.

Hollandaise is the sauce of Eggs Benedict, Salmon en Croute, and steamed asparagus. It requires skill, attention, and respect for temperature — but once mastered, it is one of the most rewarding preparations in classical cooking.

📝 Sauce Hollandaise — Recipe (Yields 500ml, serves 8-10)

Ingredients:

  • 500g unsalted butter (for clarifying — yields approx. 350g clarified butter)
  • 6 large egg yolks
  • 3 tablespoons white wine vinegar
  • 3 tablespoons cold water
  • 10 black peppercorns, cracked
  • 1 bay leaf
  • Juice of half a lemon
  • Salt and white pepper
  • Pinch of cayenne pepper

Procedure:

  1. Clarify the butter: Melt butter slowly over low heat. Skim the white foam from the surface. Carefully pour off the clear golden liquid (clarified butter), leaving the milky solids at the bottom behind. Keep warm but not hot.
  2. Make the reduction: In a small saucepan, combine the white wine vinegar, cracked peppercorns, and bay leaf. Reduce over medium heat until only 1 tablespoon of liquid remains. Strain and allow to cool slightly. Add the cold water.
  3. Whisk the yolks: Place egg yolks in a heatproof bowl with the cooled reduction. Set the bowl over a bain-marie (saucepan of barely simmering water — the bowl must NOT touch the water). Whisk the yolks vigorously and continuously until they become pale, thick, and creamy — the ribbon stage. The whisk should leave trails in the mixture. This takes 4-6 minutes.
  4. Add the clarified butter: Remove the bowl from the heat. Begin adding the warm clarified butter in an extremely slow, thin, steady stream — almost drop by drop at first — while whisking continuously. As the emulsion forms and stabilizes, you can add the butter slightly faster, but never rush. If the sauce becomes too thick, add a few drops of warm water to loosen.
  5. Season and finish: Season with lemon juice, salt, white pepper, and cayenne. Strain through a fine sieve for a silky finish. Keep warm in the bain-marie until service — do not allow it to get too hot or it will split.

Holding Temperature: 60-65°C. Above 70°C the eggs scramble and the sauce splits. Below 55°C it becomes a food safety risk.

⚠️ Chef's Tip — Rescuing a Split Hollandaise: If your Hollandaise splits (the butter separates from the emulsion), start with a fresh egg yolk in a clean bowl over the bain-marie. Slowly whisk the split sauce into the fresh yolk as if it were clarified butter. The fresh yolk will re-emulsify the sauce. Never discard a split Hollandaise without attempting this rescue.

🔀 Hollandaise Derivative Sauces

  • Sauce Bearnaise: The most famous derivative. Hollandaise base made with a tarragon and shallot reduction instead of plain vinegar reduction, finished with fresh tarragon and chervil. The definitive sauce for grilled beef — particularly steak and beef tenderloin. Arguably more popular in modern kitchens than Hollandaise itself.
  • Sauce Choron: Bearnaise + tomato puree. Vibrant pink-orange color — for grilled fish and steaks.
  • Sauce Foyot (Valois): Bearnaise + meat glaze (glace de viande). Deeply savory — for grilled beef and veal.
  • Sauce Grimrod: Hollandaise + saffron. Luxurious golden sauce for poached eggs and fish.
  • Sauce Maltaise: Hollandaise + blood orange juice and zest. Fruity, fragrant — the classic sauce for steamed asparagus in spring.
  • Sauce Mousseline (Chantilly): Hollandaise + lightly whipped cream folded in just before service. Light, airy, and delicate — for steamed vegetables and delicate fish.
  • Sauce Paloise: Bearnaise made with fresh mint instead of tarragon. The classic sauce for roast lamb.

5 Sauce Tomate — The Tomato Sauce

🍅 What is Sauce Tomate?

The French Mother Sauce Tomate is a far more complex and layered preparation than a simple Italian tomato sauce. The classical French version is enriched with a roux, salt pork, aromatic vegetables, and long-simmered stock — producing a deeply savory, full-bodied tomato sauce with remarkable depth of flavor.

In modern professional kitchens, the simpler Italian-influenced tomato sauce (without roux) is often used, but understanding the classical French preparation is essential for any serious chef.

📝 Sauce Tomate — Classical French Recipe (Yields 1 Litre)

Ingredients:

  • 1.5kg ripe tomatoes, roughly chopped (or 800g quality canned whole tomatoes)
  • 150g salt pork or streaky bacon (blanched), diced
  • 1 medium onion, diced
  • 2 carrots, diced
  • 4 cloves garlic, crushed
  • 50g butter
  • 30g plain flour
  • 500ml white veal or chicken stock
  • 100g tomato puree
  • Bouquet garni (thyme, bay leaf, parsley)
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • Salt and black pepper
  • 2 tbsp olive oil

Procedure:

  1. Render the salt pork: In a large heavy-bottomed pot, heat olive oil over medium heat. Add the diced salt pork and cook until the fat renders and the pork begins to color — approximately 5 minutes.
  2. Sweat the aromatics: Add the onion, carrot, and garlic to the pot. Cook over medium heat, stirring occasionally, until softened and lightly golden — 8-10 minutes.
  3. Add roux: Add butter to the pot. Once melted, sprinkle in the flour and stir for 2-3 minutes to make a light roux with the vegetables.
  4. Add tomatoes and stock: Add the chopped tomatoes, tomato puree, and stock. Stir well to combine. Add bouquet garni and sugar.
  5. Long simmer: Bring to a boil, reduce to a very low simmer, and cook uncovered for 45-60 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the sauce has reduced and deepened in color and flavor.
  6. Pass and season: Remove bouquet garni. Pass through a food mill or fine sieve for a smooth sauce, or blend and strain for a very smooth finish. Adjust seasoning with salt, pepper, and a pinch more sugar if needed.
⚠️ Chef's Tip: The sugar is not optional — it balances the natural acidity of tomatoes. Always taste and adjust. A pinch of sugar transforms a sharp, acidic tomato sauce into a rounded, balanced one. Use ripe, in-season tomatoes for the best flavor; if tomatoes are out of season, quality canned San Marzano tomatoes are superior.

🔀 Sauce Tomate Derivative Sauces

  • Sauce Creole: Tomato sauce + green peppers + celery + onions + cayenne + thyme. The soul of Louisiana cuisine — for shrimp, fish, and rice dishes.
  • Sauce Portugaise: Tomato sauce + sauteed onions + garlic + white wine + parsley. Light and fragrant — for fish, eggs, and chicken.
  • Sauce Provencale: Tomato sauce + garlic + herbs de Provence (thyme, rosemary, oregano, basil) + olive oil. The essence of Southern French cooking.
  • Sauce Milanaise: Tomato sauce + julienned ham + mushrooms + tongue + truffle. Rich and complex — for pasta and veal dishes in the Milanese style.
  • Sauce Fondue de Tomate: Tomatoes + shallots + garlic + butter, cooked down to a thick, jammy consistency. Used as a base, condiment, and finishing sauce across modern cuisine.
  • Puttanesca: Tomato sauce + anchovies + capers + black olives + chilli + garlic. Bold, briny, and intense — a modern classic for pasta.
  • Arrabbiata: Tomato sauce + garlic + dried red chilli. Simple, fiery, and perfect with penne.

🧈 Understanding Roux: The Foundation of Three Mother Sauces

A roux is a cooked mixture of equal parts (by weight) of fat and flour. It is the thickening agent for Bechamel, Veloute, and Espagnole. The degree to which the roux is cooked determines both its color and its thickening power — the more it is cooked, the less thickening power it retains, but the more flavor it develops.

Roux Type Color Cooking Time Flavor Used In
White Roux White/Pale 1-2 minutes Neutral, floury Bechamel
Blonde Roux Pale straw 3-5 minutes Slightly nutty Veloute
Brown Roux Chestnut brown 8-15 minutes Rich, nutty, toasted Espagnole
Dark Roux Dark chocolate 20-30 minutes Deep, smoky Gumbo (Cajun cuisine)
🎯 The Golden Rule of Roux: Hot roux + cold liquid = lumps. Cold roux + hot liquid = lumps. To prevent lumps always use: hot roux + hot liquid OR cold roux + cold liquid — then heat together while whisking constantly.
