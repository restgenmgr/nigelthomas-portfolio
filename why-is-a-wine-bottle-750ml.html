Why Is a Wine Bottle 750 ml?

By Nigel A Thomas • August 2026 • 8 min read

A story of measurement, trade and mathematics — how litres, gallons, Bordeaux barrels and a bit of 18th-century arithmetic gave the wine world its single most universal number.

Pick up almost any bottle of wine, anywhere in the world, and the label will quietly confirm the same number: 750 ml. It doesn't matter whether you're holding a Bordeaux grand cru, a bulk-produced table wine, or a bottle of sparkling wine from a completely different hemisphere — the volume is, with only a handful of exceptions, identical. For an industry as fiercely regional and tradition-bound as wine, that kind of uniformity is unusual. It didn't happen by accident, and it didn't happen because 750 ml is some naturally "correct" amount of wine to drink. It happened because of trade, taxation, barrel arithmetic, and more than two centuries of commercial compromise between countries that couldn't agree on how to measure a litre.

As someone who has spent three decades running bars, training F&B teams, and controlling beverage costs across hotels, resorts and cruise lines, I get asked this question more often than almost any other piece of wine trivia. It's a good one to know cold, because the answer touches nearly everything a hospitality professional deals with day to day: costing, portion control, case logistics, and even the layout of a wine list. Let's walk through it properly.

The Core Problem: Two Empires, Two Units of Measurement

To understand the 750 ml bottle, you have to go back to the 18th and 19th centuries, when France was one of the largest wine-producing and wine-exporting nations on earth, and Britain was among its most important customers. Bordeaux, in particular, built an enormous export trade with English merchants, some of whom — the Bordeaux "négociants" of Irish and Scottish descent — became so embedded in the region's wine trade that their names are still on the labels of famous châteaux today.

The trouble was that France and Britain didn't measure liquid the same way. France worked in litres, a unit that would later become the backbone of the metric system. Britain worked in gallons — specifically, after 1824, the Imperial gallon, defined as the volume of ten pounds of water. Every shipment of wine that crossed the English Channel had to be converted from one system to the other, and that conversion mattered enormously, because it directly affected import duty, taxation, and the amount merchants could legally claim was in a barrel or bottle.

This wasn't a minor bookkeeping inconvenience. Customs officials, wine merchants and shippers needed numbers that converted cleanly, or at least predictably, between litres and gallons, and between barrels and bottles. Over time, the trade essentially "solved" this problem by settling on bottle and case sizes that happened to work reasonably well in both systems — and that convergence is the real origin of 750 ml.

The Bordeaux Barrel: Where the Arithmetic Actually Lines Up

The cleanest and most commonly cited piece of the puzzle is the Bordeaux barrique — the traditional oak barrel used to age and transport Bordeaux wine, still in use today. A barrique holds 225 litres.

Divide 225 litres by 0.75 litres (750 ml) and you get exactly 300. One barrel of wine, bottled at 750 ml, yields exactly 300 bottles — a clean, whole number with no awkward remainder to argue over. For merchants who were literally being taxed and paid by volume, a bottle size that divided a standard barrel into a round number was worth a great deal. It meant a barrel could be accounted for, invoiced, and taxed without fractional bottles or wasted product, and it made stocktaking and pricing dramatically simpler across an entire supply chain that ran from Bordeaux cellars to English dining rooms.

225 litres (1 barrique) ÷ 0.75 litres (1 bottle) = 300 bottles — a whole number, and a very convenient one for 18th-century trade, tax and accounting.

Why Wine Comes in Cases of 6 or 12

The 750 ml figure gets even more interesting when you look at how bottles were packed for shipping. Wine has been sold and transported in cases of 6 or 12 bottles for so long that most people never stop to ask why those specific numbers.

Here's the part that ties back to the Imperial gallon: six bottles of 750 ml wine add up to 4.5 litres — remarkably close to one Imperial gallon, which is 4.546 litres. Twelve bottles come to 9 litres, almost exactly two Imperial gallons (9.092 litres). In other words, a standard case of wine was, for practical trade purposes, roughly equivalent to a whole number of gallons. That mattered enormously to British merchants and customs officers who were still thinking, taxing and pricing in gallons long after France had gone metric.

This overlap between the metric and imperial systems — a barrel that divides evenly into 750 ml bottles, and a case that lines up almost exactly with a whole number of gallons — is why 750 ml and 6-or-12-bottle cases became the default across the entire European wine trade, and eventually the world's. It wasn't a single decree from a single government. It was a size that kept winning in the marketplace because it made the paperwork easier on both sides of the Channel.

The American Connection: "One Fifth"

The United States adds its own chapter to the story, particularly for spirits. Before metrication, American liquor was commonly sold in a size called "one fifth" — literally one-fifth of a US gallon, which works out to roughly 757 ml, very close to 750 ml but not quite identical.

When the United States began moving toward metric standardisation for alcoholic beverages in the late 1970s (the shift largely took effect from 1979 to 1980 under regulations from the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms), 750 ml was adopted as the new standard bottle size for wine and distilled spirits. It was close enough to the old "one fifth" that the change was barely noticeable to drinkers and easy for producers to absorb, while finally bringing the massive US beverage market into line with the metric sizes already dominant in Europe.

That single regulatory decision matters more than it might seem. It meant that instead of the US running on gallons and fifths while the rest of the wine-producing world ran on litres and barriques, everyone converged on the same number. From that point on, 750 ml wasn't just a European trade convenience — it was a genuinely global default.

The Global Standard Today

With international trade agreements, metric standardisation, and the global scale of the modern wine and spirits industry, 750 ml has become the near-universal bottle size for wine, and it dominates spirits as well. It's written into labelling law in most major markets, it's what glassware, storage racks, wine fridges and shipping cartons are designed around, and it's the number every restaurant wine list, every distributor invoice, and every hotel beverage cost sheet is built on.

Magnums, Half Bottles and the Ageing Curve

Once 750 ml became the anchor size, everything else in the wine world was defined as a multiple or fraction of it, which is itself a small piece of evidence for how central the number is. A half bottle (375 ml) is exactly half; a magnum (1.5 litres) is exactly two bottles; a double magnum or Jeroboam (3 litres) is exactly four. This isn't a coincidence of nice round numbers — it's a direct consequence of the industry standardising on 750 ml first and building every other format around it afterward.

There's also a genuine technical reason serious collectors and sommeliers prefer larger formats for long-term ageing. A magnum has roughly twice the wine volume of a standard bottle but only a marginally larger neck and cork surface area, which means the ratio of oxygen exposure to wine volume is lower. In practice, that slows the ageing process slightly and is widely believed to help wines develop more gracefully over decades. None of that changes the base unit, though — it's still built on 750 ml.

A Short Timeline

  • 18th century: Bordeaux's export trade with Britain grows rapidly; French litres and British (later Imperial) gallons need constant conversion.
  • Early-to-mid 19th century: The 225-litre barrique becomes the standard Bordeaux barrel; bottling at roughly 750 ml yields a clean 300 bottles per barrel.
  • 1824: Britain formally defines the Imperial gallon; 6- and 12-bottle cases at 750 ml align closely with 1 and 2 Imperial gallons respectively, reinforcing the size across the trade.
  • Late 19th – 20th century: 750 ml spreads well beyond Bordeaux, becoming the de facto standard across most of Europe's wine-producing regions.
  • 1979–1980: The United States adopts metric standard sizes for wine and spirits, replacing "one fifth" with 750 ml and bringing the world's largest beverage market into alignment.
  • Today: 750 ml is enshrined in labelling regulations across most major markets and is the default around which glassware, racking, and cellar design are built.

Why 750 ml Beat a Simple, Round 1 Litre

It's worth pausing on a question people often ask once they've heard the barrel-and-gallon explanation: if the metric system was going to win out eventually anyway, why didn't the industry just settle on a clean 1-litre bottle? The honest answer is that a 1-litre bottle would have broken almost every piece of arithmetic that made 750 ml so convenient in the first place. A 225-litre barrique divided by 1 litre gives 225 bottles — not an unreasonable number on its own, but it no longer lines up neatly with 6- or 12-bottle cases against Imperial gallons, and it doesn't preserve any continuity with the "one fifth" sizing already entrenched in the American market. In short, 750 ml wasn't chosen because someone sat down and calculated the "ideal" glass of wine; it survived because it was the one number that kept working across multiple, mutually incompatible systems of trade, tax and measurement at once. That's a very hospitality-industry kind of answer: the winning standard is rarely the most elegant one on paper, it's the one that keeps the supply chain, the paperwork and the customs officers happy.

Why This Matters in Day-to-Day Hospitality Operations

For anyone working in F&B, this isn't just historical trivia — it's operational fact that underpins several parts of the job:

  • Costing and pour cost: A 750 ml bottle typically yields five 150 ml glasses or six 125 ml glasses. Your standard pour size, glass cost, and menu pricing all trace back to this one number.
  • Inventory and par levels: Case counts of 6 or 12 bottles per case still shape how beverage inventory is ordered, stored and reconciled, exactly as they did two centuries ago.
  • Storage and cellar design: Wine racking, cellar shelving and refrigeration units are almost universally engineered around the 750 ml bottle footprint.
  • Compliance and labelling: Understanding standard sizes helps F&B managers verify deliveries, spot short-fills, and train staff on correct stock rotation and service standards.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why isn't a wine bottle exactly 1 litre?
A 1-litre bottle wouldn't divide the 225-litre Bordeaux barrique into a clean whole number of bottles, and it wouldn't align with Imperial gallon-based case sizes the way 750 ml does. The 750 ml figure won out because it worked cleanly in both the metric and imperial trade systems of the time.
Do all countries use 750 ml bottles?
The vast majority of the world's wine is sold in 750 ml bottles today, though some markets and producers use half bottles (375 ml), magnums (1.5 L) and other formats for specific styles, occasions or ageing purposes.
Is 750 ml also standard for spirits?
Yes. Since the US adopted metric standard sizes for distilled spirits in the late 1970s, 750 ml has become the dominant standard bottle size for spirits as well as wine in most major markets.
How many glasses of wine come from a 750 ml bottle?
At a standard 150 ml pour, a 750 ml bottle yields five glasses. At a smaller 125 ml pour, it yields six glasses — the same logic hospitality outlets use to calculate pour cost and menu pricing.

So the next time you pick up a 750 ml bottle, remember that behind that simple number is a genuinely interesting piece of trade history — French litres, British gallons, a 225-litre Bordeaux barrel, and an American "fifth" all converging, over roughly two centuries, on one universally convenient size.
