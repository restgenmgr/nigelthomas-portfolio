What Is Management Consulting? A Hospitality Leader's Take on an Industry Built to Solve Problems

By Nigel Thomas  |  Hospitality Executive, Trainer & Consultant  |  8 min read

I sat in on an online training session recently, the kind with a small video tile in the corner and a slide deck that kept scrolling past faster than my notes could keep up with. The speaker, a consultant named Shubham Agarwal, was walking a room full of professionals through the fundamentals of business consulting. I've spent thirty years running hotel operations, food and beverage departments, and resort properties across India, the Middle East, and the United States. I've hired consultants, sat across the table from them, and occasionally been skeptical of them. But that afternoon, listening to the numbers and the framework being laid out slide by slide, something clicked for me in a way it hadn't before. I want to share what I learned, because I think every hospitality professional eyeing a senior leadership role, or considering a second career as an advisor, needs to understand this world.

Business Consulting, in Plain Numbers

The session opened with a simple definition: business consulting is a group of professional problem solvers. Companies bring in consultants when they need outside eyes on strategic decisions, when internal teams are too close to the problem to see the way forward, or when a business simply needs to grow faster than its current playbook allows. That's a description I recognized immediately. It's exactly what a strong Director of Operations or General Manager does inside a property, except consultants do it across dozens of companies instead of one.

What surprised me were the numbers behind the industry.

$300Bn+ Annual revenue, having quadrupled in the last decade
700K+ Consultants working worldwide
200+ Industries and functional areas served

Consulting has quadrupled its revenue base in ten years to cross $300 billion annually. There are over 700,000 consultants operating globally, and they serve more than 200 industries and functional areas, everything from manufacturing to healthcare to, yes, hospitality. That scale tells you something important: this isn't a niche corner of the corporate world. It's a massive, fast-growing engine that touches nearly every sector, including the hotels and resorts many of us have spent our careers running.

The Three Pillars of Business Consulting

The deck broke the industry into three broad pillars, and I found this structure genuinely useful for making sense of what "consulting" actually covers, since the word gets thrown around loosely.

Financial Advisory deals with the money side of a business at a structural level: how it's funded, how it survives a downturn, how risk is managed, and how deals get done. Technology consulting has exploded in the last decade as companies digitize everything from booking engines to supply chains. But the pillar that got the most airtime in the session, and the one I found myself nodding along to the most, was Management Consulting.

So What Exactly Is Management Consulting?

Here's the number that stopped me mid-note: Management Consulting alone makes up 50 to 60 percent of the entire consulting industry. More than half of every consulting dollar spent globally goes toward helping organizations make better decisions about strategy, operations, and people. That's not a side business. That's the core of the profession.

The session broke Management Consulting into three areas, and each one maps almost perfectly onto the responsibilities I've carried in hotel and resort leadership roles over the years.

Strategy

Everything from corporate strategy to organizational and functional strategy falls here. This is the "where are we going and how do we get there" work. In hospitality terms, it's the difference between a resort that reacts to occupancy dips with discounting and one that has a deliberate positioning strategy, a clear sense of which market segment it serves, and a five-year plan for how it wants to grow revenue per available room. Every General Manager and Director of Operations does strategy work whether or not they'd ever call themselves a strategist.

Operations

Process management, supply chain, and project management sit under this heading. If Strategy is the "what," Operations is the "how," and this is where I've spent the bulk of my career. Anyone who has redesigned an F&B service hierarchy, tightened a kitchen's HACCP compliance process, or rebuilt a housekeeping department's workflow has been doing operations consulting, just without the title or the day rate. The discipline is identical: find the inefficiency, redesign the process, measure the outcome.

Human Resource

Organizational change, learning and development, talent management, and rewards make up the HR pillar. This is close to my heart because hospitality is, at the end of the day, a people business wearing an operations costume. A property can have the best strategy and the tightest processes in the market and still fail if the team isn't trained, motivated, and retained. Consultants who work in this space help companies build the systems that keep talent engaged, and it's precisely the kind of work I've done as a corporate trainer.

Why This Matters If You're in Hospitality

Every one of these three areas, Strategy, Operations, and Human Resource, is something a seasoned hotel or resort executive already practices daily, just without calling it "consulting." If you've run a property, you've done strategic planning, process redesign, and talent development, often all in the same week. Recognizing that is the first step toward positioning yourself as a consultant or corporate trainer once you've built enough scar tissue in operating roles.

Where This Fits Into a Hospitality Career

I've watched the hospitality industry change shape over three decades, moving from independently run properties to large branded portfolios that increasingly lean on outside advisors for everything from pre-opening strategy to F&B repositioning to quality audits. Hotel groups now regularly bring in management consultants for exactly the kind of work described above: strategic repositioning after a change in ownership, operational audits when margins tighten, and organizational restructuring when a property scales from a single resort to a multi-property group.

That creates an opening. Hospitality professionals who reach the senior levels, General Manager, Resort Manager, Director of Operations, Director of Food and Beverage, are sitting on exactly the kind of operational and people-management experience that management consulting firms value, but rarely recruit for directly because they don't always know where to look. The traffic tends to run the other way: consultants get hired to advise operators, not operators hired to become consultants. That's slowly shifting, and I think it's worth hospitality leaders paying attention to.

The Skills Overlap Is Bigger Than People Realize

Sitting through that training session, I kept a mental tally of how many consulting competencies map directly onto skills hospitality leaders already have.

Problem diagnosis under pressure. A consultant walks into an unfamiliar business and has weeks, sometimes days, to identify the real problem. A hotel GM does this constantly: a sudden drop in guest satisfaction scores, a labor cost spike, a supply chain disruption during peak season. The muscle is the same.

Cross-functional fluency. Management consultants need to speak finance, operations, and people all in the same meeting. So does anyone who has run a property, where the F&B numbers, the housekeeping labor model, and the front office guest experience all have to be reconciled into one coherent operation.

Change management. The Human Resource pillar of consulting is essentially about helping organizations absorb change without losing their people. Hospitality leaders who have navigated brand transitions, ownership changes, or post-pandemic service model overhauls have already done this work in the field, under real stakes, not in a slide deck.

The biggest difference between a hotel operator and a management consultant isn't the skill set. It's the framing. Consultants are trained to package what they know into a repeatable, sellable methodology. Operators usually aren't, and that's the gap worth closing.

What I'm Taking Away From This

I didn't walk out of that session planning to rebrand myself as a management consultant overnight. But it sharpened something I've believed for a while: the line between "operator" and "consultant" is thinner than the industry makes it seem. A $300 billion industry, growing at the pace this one is, with more than half of its revenue sitting in Strategy, Operations, and Human Resource work, is an industry built almost entirely on the kind of judgment that comes from running real operations under real pressure.

For anyone in hospitality eyeing a Corporate Training and Quality Manager role, or thinking about consulting as the next chapter after a General Manager or Director of Operations career, my advice is simple: you already have more of the raw material than you think. What's missing for most operators isn't competence, it's the vocabulary and structure to present that competence the way a consulting firm would. Learning to frame your operational war stories as strategy work, your process fixes as operations consulting, and your team-building as HR and talent management isn't dishonest repackaging. It's translation. And it's a translation worth learning.
