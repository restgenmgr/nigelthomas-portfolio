Why storekeepers get this wrong

Staff use "par stock," "stock on hand," and "reorder level" interchangeably, when each one answers a completely different question. The three divisions, explained

1. Par Stock

The fixed, ideal quantity of an item you've decided to always keep on hand, based on usage rate, storage space, and delivery frequency. Par stock is a target, not a count Ã¢â‚¬â€ it rarely changes week to week.

2. Inventory on Hand

The actual, physically counted quantity sitting on your shelf right now. This is a fact, verified by a physical stock take Ã¢â‚¬â€ not a system number, not a guess, not what the last shift said.

3. Reorder Level / Stock to Order

The trigger point that tells you to place an order, and the exact quantity you need to bring stock back to par. This is the action Ã¢â‚¬â€ the whole reason you counted in the first place.

Here's the sequence that ties them together every single day: you set a Par Stock once (and review it periodically). You count Inventory on Hand daily or per shift. You compare the count to the Reorder Level. If the count is at or below that level, you calculate Stock to Order and place the purchase requisition Ã¢â‚¬â€ the same day, not "tomorrow if I remember."

Worked example: Sugar in the pastry store

Par Stock of sugar is set at 20 kg Ã¢â‚¬â€ that's the maximum you want on the shelf at any time, based on weekly usage and storage space. Today's physical count (Inventory on Hand) comes to 8 kg. Your Reorder Level for sugar Ã¢â‚¬â€ calculated from average daily usage, supplier lead time, and a safety buffer Ã¢â‚¬â€ is also 8 kg. Because Inventory on Hand has reached the Reorder Level, the action is immediate: place the order today for 12 kg (Par Stock minus Inventory on Hand) to bring the shelf back to 20 kg before the next delivery cycle.

The rule that removes all the guesswork: if Inventory on Hand ≤ Reorder Level, order now. Everything else Ã¢â‚¬â€ the spreadsheets, the apps, the whiteboards Ã¢â‚¬â€ is just a way of getting a human being to see that comparison clearly enough to act on it.

The three formulas every store should use

Reorder Level  =  (Average Daily Usage × Lead Time in Days)  +  Safety Stock
Safety Stock  =  (Maximum Daily Usage × Maximum Lead Time)  −  (Average Daily Usage × Average Lead Time)
Stock to Order  =  Par Stock  −  Inventory on Hand

Safety stock is the buffer that protects you when a delivery runs late or usage spikes unexpectedly Ã¢â‚¬â€ a busy weekend, a last-minute banquet, a delayed supplier truck. Skip it, and your reorder level is really just a wish, not a plan.

Common mistakes vs best practice

✘ Common mistakes

  • Setting par stock too high Ã¢â‚¬â€ capital tied up, spoilage risk
  • Setting par stock too low Ã¢â‚¬â€ chronic stockouts
  • Ignoring real usage rate when setting par
  • Never calculating a reorder level Ã¢â‚¬â€ ordering "by feel"
  • Skipping safety stock, then blaming the supplier for delays
  • Treating paper & plastic as an afterthought

✔ Best practice

  • Set par stock from actual usage data and storage capacity
  • Count inventory on hand at the same time, every day
  • Calculate reorder level with usage, lead time, and safety stock
  • Review par stock monthly against real consumption
  • Run Food, Bar, and Paper & Plastic on the same discipline
  • Put the golden rule on the wall where the count happens

How to set an accurate par stock (step by step)

Most par stock numbers I inherit when auditing a new property were set once, years ago, by someone who has since left, and never reviewed again. That's how you end up with 20 kg of sugar sitting as "par" for a pastry section that now bakes half the volume it did in 2019. Setting par stock properly takes thirty minutes per category and pays for itself within a week.

  1. Pull 4Ã¢â‚¬â€œ8 weeks of consumption data for the item Ã¢â‚¬â€ from requisitions, POS sales, or covers served, not from memory.
  2. Calculate average daily usage, and separately note the busiest single day in that window Ã¢â‚¬â€ that's your peak usage.
  3. Confirm supplier lead time Ã¢â‚¬â€ how many days between placing the order and the goods hitting your shelf, including weekends and holidays when suppliers may be closed.
  4. Check physical storage capacity Ã¢â‚¬â€ there's no point setting a par stock your shelf, chiller, or store cannot physically hold.
  5. Set par stock to cover usage through the next delivery cycle, plus a margin for the busiest realistic day, not the average day.
  6. Write the number down on the worksheet and the shelf label, and revisit it every month Ã¢â‚¬â€ seasonality, menu changes, and occupancy swings all move the target.

Reading your reorder level like a pro

A reorder level that's set too tight Ã¢â‚¬â€ right at the edge of your average usage, with no safety stock Ã¢â‚¬â€ looks efficient on paper and fails the first time a supplier truck is delayed by a public holiday or a weather disruption. A reorder level set too generously just drags your par stock logic along with it and defeats the purpose of having a trigger point at all. The safety stock formula exists precisely to protect the middle ground: it sizes your buffer using the gap between your worst-case scenario (maximum usage during maximum lead time) and your average-case scenario, so the cushion reflects real risk instead of a guess.

Frequently asked questions

Is par stock the same as reorder level?

No. Par stock is the ceiling you want to maintain; reorder level is the floor that tells you when to act. They can occasionally land on the same number for a fast-moving item, as in the sugar example above, but they answer different questions and should always be calculated separately.

How often should inventory on hand be counted?

High-value and fast-moving items Ã¢â‚¬â€ proteins, spirits, key disposables Ã¢â‚¬â€ should be counted daily or every shift. Slower-moving dry goods can be counted two to three times a week. Whatever the frequency, consistency matters more than perfection: a count done at the same time, the same way, every day, beats an occasional "perfect" audit.

What happens if I don't calculate stock to order and just "top up"?

Topping up without doing the subtraction almost always over-orders, because it's easy to round up "to be safe." Over time that inflates your average inventory, ties up working capital, and increases spoilage risk Ã¢â‚¬â€ especially in food and bar categories with shelf-life limits. The formula exists so the order quantity is a calculation, not a feeling.

Does this system work for a small independent restaurant, not just a hotel?

Yes Ã¢â‚¬â€ the scale changes, the discipline doesn't. A single-outlet restaurant may only need to track twenty to thirty key items closely rather than several hundred, but the same three numbers and the same golden rule apply whether you're provisioning one kitchen or a resort with six outlets.

Putting it into the bigger inventory picture

Par stock, inventory on hand, and reorder level are the operational core of inventory control, but they sit inside a wider system: FIFO/FEFO rotation to protect what you've already bought, accurate receiving to make sure what's invoiced is what's delivered, and periodic full stock takes to reconcile the storeroom against the books. None of that works, though, if the three numbers on this worksheet aren't tracked honestly, every single day, by every single storekeeper on every single shift. Systems don't fail from bad software Ã¢â‚¬â€ they fail from a shelf nobody counted.

Whether you're running a single restaurant pass, a resort's central store, or a cruise ship's provisions hold, the principle scales without changing: know your target, know your truth, know your trigger. Everything else in inventory management is built on top of those three facts.

Training a team on this system is far more effective than simply handing them a spreadsheet. Walk each storekeeper through one real item Ã¢â‚¬â€ sugar, house vodka, or a case of takeaway boxes Ã¢â‚¬â€ and have them calculate par stock, count inventory on hand, and work out stock to order themselves before they touch the worksheet unsupervised. Once the logic clicks on one item, it transfers instantly to every other item in the store, because the three questions never change: what should I have, what do I actually have, and what do I need to order right now to close the gap.
