Power of Kitchen Herbs: Tulsi, Coriander, Parsley, Rosemary, Thyme & Mint

By Nigel A. Thomas, Hospitality Executive • Updated 15 October 2026 • 15 min read

Across thirty years in hotels, resorts and ship galleys, I have noticed that the humblest ingredients in a kitchen often do the most work. Herbs cost very little and take up almost no space, yet they decide whether a dish tastes flat or alive. They also carry a second story that cooks and guests increasingly ask about: the natural compounds inside each leaf, and the health benefits that traditional cooking and modern research have linked to them.

This guide accompanies the infographic Power of Kitchen Herbs, which pairs six everyday herbs with one key compound and one benefit commonly associated with it. Holy basil is paired with eugenol and healthy lungs. Coriander is paired with linalool and the kidneys. Parsley is paired with vitamin K and strong bones. Rosemary is paired with carnosic acid and a glowing brain. Thyme is paired with thymol and an immune shield, and mint is paired with menthol and a soothed stomach.

Infographics have to compress complicated ideas into a few words, so this article does the other half of the job. For each herb, it explains what the evidence actually shows, how to use it well, and where caution is needed. Some links on the poster are well supported, and some are traditional associations that science has only begun to examine.

What Are Herb Compounds, and How Should We Read Them?

Plants cannot run away from insects, microbes or the sun, so they defend themselves with chemistry. Herbs are especially rich in volatile oils and phenolic compounds, which protect the plant and also create the aromas and flavours that cooks value. Eugenol, linalool, thymol, menthol and carnosic acid are all examples. When we eat the leaf, we taste these molecules first, and our bodies then encounter them as part of the food.

In laboratory studies, many of these compounds show antioxidant, anti-inflammatory or antimicrobial behaviour. That is genuinely interesting, but it needs careful reading. A result in a dish of cells, or in an animal, does not automatically translate into a benefit for a person eating a sprinkle of herbs on dinner. The amounts in a normal portion of fresh herb are small, and concentrated extracts, essential oils and capsules behave very differently from leaves on a plate.

A sensible way to read the poster is this: each herb is associated with a benefit, in tradition and in some research. Culinary herbs are also valuable because they make healthy food taste better, which helps people use less salt and fat.

1. Holy Basil (Tulsi) — Eugenol — Healthy Lungs

Holy basil, known in India as tulsi (Ocimum tenuiflorum), is not the same plant as the sweet basil used in pesto and Margherita pizza. It has a warmer, clove-like and slightly peppery aroma, and it has been grown beside homes and temples for centuries. In Ayurvedic tradition it is called the “queen of herbs”, and a cup of tulsi tea is one of the most familiar household remedies for a winter cough or a heavy chest.

The compound named on the poster is eugenol, a phenolic compound that is also the dominant flavour molecule in clove. It gives holy basil its spicy warmth. Tulsi also contains rosmarinic acid, ursolic acid and several flavonoids. In laboratory work, eugenol shows antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity, which is the scientific reason tulsi is so often linked to respiratory comfort.

What does the evidence say? A systematic review of human trials found encouraging results for tulsi across several areas, but most of the studies were small and of modest quality. That is a reasonable basis for enjoying tulsi as part of a healthy diet, and not a basis for treating asthma, bronchitis or any chest infection with tea alone. Persistent cough, breathlessness or fever always deserves medical attention.

In the kitchen: Use tulsi fresh in teas and infusions, in Thai-style stir-fries (where the related holy basil is used for pad kra pao), in chutneys, and as an aromatic addition to hot lemon-and-honey drinks. Add the leaves late, because long boiling dulls the aroma. In a hotel setting, a tulsi-ginger infusion makes a simple and authentic welcome drink or in-room wellness offering.

Caution: Tulsi is generally safe in food amounts. People who take blood-thinning or diabetes medication, and women who are pregnant or breastfeeding, should check with their doctor before using it in concentrated forms such as extracts, capsules or strong daily infusions.

2. Fresh Coriander — Linalool — Kidneys

Fresh coriander (called cilantro in North America) is one of the most widely used herbs in the world, from Indian chutneys and Mexican salsas to Thai curries and Middle Eastern salads. Every part of the plant is used: the leaves, the tender stems, the roots (pounded into Thai curry pastes) and the dried seeds, which taste warm, citrusy and nutty and are a separate spice in their own right.

The compound on the poster is linalool, a fragrant terpene alcohol that is the main component of coriander seed oil, often making up well over half of it. Linalool is also found in lavender, basil and citrus peel, and is responsible for the floral, slightly sweet note in coriander. The fresh leaf has its own distinctive aldehydes, which some people experience as soapy because of a genetic difference in smell receptors. If a guest dislikes coriander, it is usually biology and not bad cooking.

The link to the kidneys is the most traditional and least proven connection on the poster. Coriander has a long history in folk medicine as a mild diuretic, and some animal studies have explored its effects on kidney function. Good human evidence is limited. It is fair to say that coriander is a flavourful, low-sodium way to season food, which supports a kidney-friendly eating pattern of less salt and more fresh ingredients. It is not fair to say that coriander treats or protects against kidney disease. Anyone with kidney problems should follow the dietary advice of their doctor or dietitian.

In the kitchen: Add coriander at the end of cooking or as a fresh garnish, because heat quickly mutes the leaf. Use the stems and roots, which carry more flavour than the leaves, in curry bases, marinades and stocks. Chop just before service to keep the colour bright.

Caution: Coriander belongs to the same plant family as celery, carrot and parsley. People with celery or spice allergies can react to it, so it should be declared when relevant.

3. Parsley — Vitamin K — Strong Bones

Parsley is the most underestimated herb in the professional kitchen. Too many menus treat it as a decoration beside the plate, when it is a genuine ingredient: the backbone of tabbouleh, gremolata, salsa verde, chimichurri and the classic persillade. Flat-leaf (Italian) parsley has the stronger, cleaner flavour and is the better choice for cooking. Curly parsley holds its shape well and suits garnish work.

Parsley is the one herb on the poster where the named nutrient is a measurable fact. It is among the richest food sources of vitamin K, with roughly 1,600 micrograms per 100 grams of raw leaf. Even a tablespoon of chopped fresh parsley provides an amount in the region of 60 micrograms, which is a meaningful share of the adequate daily intake commonly cited as 120 micrograms for men and 90 micrograms for women.

Vitamin K supports two jobs in the body: normal blood clotting, and the proteins that help bind calcium into bone. Observational studies have linked higher vitamin K intake with lower fracture risk, although trials of vitamin K supplements have produced mixed results. So the honest summary is that parsley contributes to a bone-supportive diet alongside calcium, vitamin D, protein and weight-bearing exercise, and does not build strong bones on its own.

In the kitchen: Treat parsley as a vegetable and not a garnish. Blend it into green sauces, fold handfuls into grain salads, stir it into soups at the end, and use the stems in stocks. A fine chop of parsley, lemon zest and garlic (gremolata) lifts braised dishes such as ossobuco instantly.

Caution: People taking warfarin or similar anticoagulants do not need to avoid parsley, but they should keep their vitamin K intake steady from week to week and discuss changes in diet with their doctor. Parsley also contains oxalates, which matters to people advised to limit them. Very large medicinal doses, especially of parsley seed or oil, are not recommended during pregnancy, while normal culinary amounts are fine.

4. Rosemary — Carnosic Acid — Glowing Brain

Rosemary is a woody Mediterranean shrub with needle-like leaves and a resinous, pine-and-lemon fragrance. It is a hardy herb: it survives long cooking, strong heat and the grill, which is why it pairs so naturally with roast lamb, potatoes, focaccia and barbecued meats. It also grows easily in pots, so it is a practical candidate for a hotel or restaurant herb garden.

The compound named on the poster is carnosic acid, a powerful antioxidant diterpene found in rosemary leaves together with carnosol and rosmarinic acid. These compounds are the reason rosemary extract is used in the food industry as a natural preservative: they slow the oxidation of fats and so extend shelf life and protect flavour.

The brain connection comes from two lines of research. Laboratory studies suggest carnosic acid can protect nerve cells from oxidative stress. Separately, a small number of human studies have looked at rosemary aroma and memory or alertness, with some reporting better test performance. This work is early, the studies are small and the results are mixed. The responsible reading is that rosemary is an interesting herb for brain-health research, and not a proven way to sharpen memory.

One practical, kitchen-relevant finding deserves a mention: several studies have found that marinating meat with rosemary can reduce the formation of heterocyclic amines, compounds that form when meat is grilled at high temperature. For anyone running a grill station, a rosemary marinade is a sensible habit.

In the kitchen: Strip the leaves from the woody stem, chop finely, and use sparingly, as rosemary can dominate. Add it early in cooking so the resinous flavour mellows. Whole sprigs work well in roasting trays and as skewers for kebabs.

Caution: Culinary amounts are safe. Concentrated rosemary oil and supplements are a different matter, and should be avoided in pregnancy and by people with epilepsy unless a doctor advises otherwise.

5. Thyme — Thymol — Immune Shield

Thyme is the quiet professional of the herb shelf. It is a member of the bouquet garni, a foundation of French stocks and sauces, a natural partner for chicken, mushrooms and root vegetables, and a key part of za’atar in the Middle East. Common thyme has a subtle earthy flavour, while lemon thyme adds a bright citrus note that suits fish and desserts.

Thymol is the star compound. It is a phenol that makes up a large share of the essential oil of common thyme, and it gives the herb its sharp, medicinal edge. In the laboratory, thymol has clear antimicrobial and antioxidant properties, and it is an ingredient in some mouthwashes and household cleaners for that reason. Thyme preparations, often combined with ivy leaf or primrose, are also used in several European cough remedies.

Where the poster says “immune shield”, a careful reader should pause. Antimicrobial activity in a test tube is not the same as protecting a person from infection, and no single herb can switch on immunity. Immune health depends on sleep, a varied diet with enough protein, vitamins and minerals, vaccination, regular movement and managing stress. Thyme belongs in that diet as a flavourful contributor, supplying small amounts of vitamin C, vitamin A and minerals along with its aromatic compounds.

In the kitchen: Thyme is a hardy herb, so add it at the start of cooking in stocks, braises, soups and roasts, where its flavour has time to release. Strip the tiny leaves by running your fingers down the stem against the direction of growth. Whole sprigs can go into the pot and be removed before service.

Caution: Thyme is safe in cooking. Thyme essential oil is very concentrated and should never be swallowed or applied undiluted to skin.

6. Fresh Mint — Menthol — Soothed Stomach

Mint is the herb that works on both sides of a hotel: it sits in the kitchen for raitas, chutneys, tabbouleh and lamb sauces, and it sits at the bar for mojitos, juleps and mocktails. There are many varieties, but two matter most. Peppermint is the cooling one, and spearmint is the sweeter, gentler one used in most cooking and in chewing gum.

Menthol, the compound on the poster, is the molecule that creates the famous cooling sensation. It activates cold-sensing receptors in the mouth and skin, which is why mint feels fresh even when nothing is cold. One precision for professionals: menthol is the main compound of peppermint. Spearmint is dominated by a different compound called carvone, and contains very little menthol.

The digestive connection has more support than several others on the poster. Menthol relaxes smooth muscle in the digestive tract, and enteric-coated peppermint oil capsules have shown benefit for symptoms of irritable bowel syndrome in several clinical trials and reviews. Mint tea is a traditional after-dinner drink in many cultures for exactly this reason, although research on the tea itself is thinner than research on the capsules.

There is an important exception. Mint can relax the valve at the top of the stomach, which may worsen heartburn or acid reflux in people prone to it. For those guests, a soothing after-meal drink might be better served as ginger or fennel tea than peppermint.

In the kitchen: Mint is a tender herb, so add it at the end. Tear or roll the leaves, and avoid chopping too far in advance, since cut mint darkens quickly. Use it in yoghurt sauces, fruit salads, peas, sorbets and, of course, drinks. For cocktails and mocktails, gently clap or press the leaves to release the oils, and never muddle them to a pulp, which releases bitterness.

Caution: Peppermint oil should not be given to infants or small children, especially near the face. Culinary amounts of fresh leaf are safe.

Handling Herbs Like a Professional: Cooking, Storage and Safety

Tender herbs versus hardy herbs. This is the single most useful rule for any cook. Tender herbs, which are basil, coriander, parsley and mint, have soft leaves and delicate oils, so they go in at the end of cooking or raw. Hardy herbs, which are rosemary and thyme, have woody stems and tougher leaves, so they go in at the start and can stand up to long heat. Mixing the two up is the most common reason herbs taste either dull or harsh.

Storage. Stand tender herbs such as coriander, parsley and mint in a jar with an inch of water, cover loosely and refrigerate. Wrap hardy herbs in a barely damp towel inside a container. Keep basil and tulsi at room temperature in water, because refrigeration blackens the leaves. Label everything with the date, rotate stock on a first-in, first-out basis, and store herbs on upper shelves away from raw meat and fish, in a refrigerator at or below 5°C.

Food safety. Fresh herbs are eaten raw, which means they deserve the same respect as salad leaves. Fresh coriander and basil have been linked to outbreaks of Cyclospora infection in some countries, so buy from trusted suppliers, wash herbs in clean running water, and avoid cross-contamination from raw proteins on boards, knives and hands. A documented HACCP routine for herbs, covering receiving, washing, storage and labelling, protects both guests and the business.

Allergens. Parsley and coriander belong to the same botanical family as celery, carrot and fennel. Guests who are allergic to celery, which is a declared allergen in many countries, may react to these herbs, so servers need to know exactly what is in a garnish or a sauce.

Putting Herbs to Work on the Menu

Herbs offer excellent value on a menu, because a small cost produces a large increase in perceived freshness and quality. A few practical ways to use them well in a hotel or restaurant:

Cut waste by using the whole plant. Coriander stems and roots go into curry pastes and marinades, parsley stems go into stocks, and rosemary stalks can be used as skewers or burned under the grill for aroma. A kitchen that uses stems and trimmings can often reduce its herb purchasing cost noticeably.

Describe benefits honestly. If a menu mentions health, keep the wording modest and accurate, such as “traditionally used to soothe digestion”. Overclaiming damages trust and can create legal problems, while a truthful, specific description builds credibility.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do kitchen herbs really have health benefits?

Yes, in a realistic sense. Herbs contain plant compounds that show interesting activity in laboratory research, and they make healthy food more appealing while reducing the need for salt. The strongest, best-documented example on the poster is the vitamin K content of parsley. Other links, such as coriander and the kidneys, or thyme and immunity, are more traditional than proven.

Can herbs replace medicine?

No. Herbs are a flavourful part of a healthy diet and not a substitute for medical treatment. If you have a medical condition, are pregnant, or take medication such as blood thinners or diabetes drugs, speak to your doctor before using concentrated herbal teas, extracts or supplements.

What is the difference between peppermint and spearmint?

Peppermint is high in menthol, which gives a strong cooling sensation and is the compound linked to digestive comfort. Spearmint is dominated by carvone, so it is milder and sweeter, and it is the usual choice for cooking and cocktails.

Final Thoughts

The Power of Kitchen Herbs poster is a useful starting point, because it reminds us that the cheapest ingredients in the kitchen are often the most interesting. Tulsi, coriander, parsley, rosemary, thyme and mint each bring a recognisable flavour and a signature compound, and each has a long tradition of use beyond the plate. Some of the benefits attached to them are well supported, some are promising and some are still folklore.

My advice, after a career spent in kitchens around the world, is simple. Buy herbs fresh, store them correctly, add them at the right moment, and enjoy them generously as part of a varied and balanced diet.
