13 Types of Menus Used in Food & Beverage Service

By Nigel A. Thomas • Hospitality Executive & Corporate Trainer • CHS, ServSafe FPM

Why the Right Menu Format Matters

Choosing the right menu type is one of the most important decisions in food and beverage service. I've opened properties where the menu format was decided in a boardroom with a spreadsheet, and I've opened properties where it was decided on the back of a napkin at 11 PM before a soft launch — and the difference always shows up on the floor within the first week.

Every menu is designed to meet different operational goals, guest expectations, and dining experiences. Get the format wrong, and even brilliant food struggles: kitchens over-produce, service slows down, and guests leave confused about what they actually paid for. Get it right, and the menu quietly does half the selling for you before a server ever reaches the table.

A menu is not a list of dishes. It's an operational contract between the kitchen, the front of house, and the guest — and each format below writes that contract differently.

The Core Five: How Guests Order and Pay

These five formats decide the single biggest question in any dining concept: does the guest choose everything, or does the house decide most of it for them?

1À la Carte

Each dish is listed and priced separately, allowing guests to order individual items exactly as they like them.

Best for: Fine dining and casual restaurants.

2Table d'Hôte (Set Menu)

A complete meal with a fixed number of courses offered at one set price, with little or no choice per course.

Best for: Hotels, banquets, and events.

3Prix Fixe Menu

A multi-course meal offered at one fixed price, often with a choice of dishes for each course.

Best for: Fine dining restaurants and special occasions.

4Tasting Menu (Dégustation)

A series of small courses showcasing the chef's creativity and signature dishes, usually in a set sequence.

Best for: Fine dining restaurants.

5Buffet Menu

Guests serve themselves from a variety of prepared dishes offered at a fixed price.

Best for: Hotels, weddings, and large events.
Know the difference: Table d'Hôte menus are more fixed with limited or no choices, while Prix Fixe menus usually offer choices within each course.

Menus Built Around Volume and Structure

The next set exists to solve a different problem entirely — not guest choice, but operational repeatability. These formats keep a kitchen consistent across hundreds of covers a day, or years of contract catering.

6Banquet Menu

A pre-arranged menu designed for large groups or special functions, agreed in advance with the client.

Best for: Conferences, weddings, and corporate events.

7Cycle Menu

A menu that repeats over a fixed period — commonly 7, 14, or 28 days — before starting again.

Best for: Hospitals, schools, and staff cafeterias.

8Static Menu

The same menu is offered every day with few or no changes, built for speed and predictability.

Best for: Fast food and chain restaurants.

9Seasonal Menu

A menu that changes based on seasonal ingredients and their availability, refreshed a few times a year.

Best for: Farm-to-table and premium restaurants.

10Du Jour Menu

Features dishes or specials that change daily, often built around what came in fresh that morning.

Best for: Restaurants, cafés, and bistros.

Additional Menu Types (Optional but Common)

These aren't standalone concepts — they're supporting menus that ride alongside one of the ten above, and they quietly do a lot of work for guest satisfaction and check average.

11Children's Menu

Smaller portions and simple dishes designed for children, usually priced lower and served faster.

Best for: Family restaurants.

12Beverage Menu

A separate menu featuring alcoholic and non-alcoholic drinks, often the single biggest margin driver on the floor.

Best for: Restaurants, bars, and hotels.

13Dessert Menu

A dedicated menu offering desserts, pastries, and sweet dishes, presented after the main meal.

Best for: Restaurants and cafés.

Quick Reference: Choosing the Right Format

If your priority is...Consider this menu type
Maximum guest flexibilityÀ la Carte
Predictable food cost per coverTable d'Hôte or Prix Fixe
Showcasing chef creativityTasting Menu
High volume, self-serviceBuffet
Contracted institutional feedingCycle Menu

Ingredient-led storytellingSeasonal or Du Jour Menu

Why This Matters on the Floor, Not Just on Paper

I've trained enough F&B teams to know that menu type dictates almost everything downstream — prep schedules, staffing ratios, portion control, and even how a server is trained to sell. A static menu team can run lean because tomorrow looks like today. A du jour team needs a pre-shift briefing every single day, because the server has to sell a dish they may not have tasted yet. A banquet menu succeeds or fails entirely on what was agreed weeks in advance — there is no room for improvisation once 200 covers are plated. Understanding these formats isn't academic; it's the difference between a kitchen that runs in control and one that's constantly firefighting.

Numbers tell you what happened. People tell you why. The right menu format is what lets both sides — kitchen and guest — actually deliver on their end of the deal.

Final Thought

A well-designed menu is more than a list of food and drinks — it is a powerful tool for improving guest satisfaction, increasing profitability, and creating memorable dining experiences. Whether you're opening a fine dining room built around a tasting menu or running a 500-cover banquet on a fixed Table d'Hôte, the format you choose sets the ceiling for what your team can deliver consistently. Know all thirteen, and you'll never again default to "à la carte" just because it's familiar — you'll choose the format that actually fits the operation in front of you.
