FSSAI Kitchen Inspection Guide: What Inspectors Check and How to Be Ready

By Nigel A. Thomas, Hospitality Executive & Corporate Trainer • Commercial Kitchen Operations Academy • Updated October 2026

An FSSAI inspection is not something a kitchen can prepare for in the last hour. Inspectors form a view of your operation within minutes of walking in: the state of the floor, whether the hand-wash basin has soap, whether the fridge thermometer reads what the log says, and whether your staff can answer a simple question about what they do and why. A kitchen that runs to its own standards every day passes calmly. A kitchen that cleans up on the morning of the visit usually shows it.

This guide is for hotel and restaurant kitchens, caterers, canteens and cloud kitchens. It explains what inspectors look at under Schedule 4 of the licensing regulations, what changed for licensed food businesses in 2026, which records you should be able to produce, and how to run a simple routine that keeps you ready all year. It is a training guide, not legal advice; the checklist at the end tells you what to verify against the current FSSAI text.

What Changed for Licensed Food Businesses in 2026

FSSAI notified the Food Safety and Standards (Licensing and Registration of Food Businesses) Amendment Regulations, 2026 in the Gazette on 10 March 2026. An implementation order dated 13 March 2026 applied the revised turnover thresholds from 1 April 2026. For a kitchen operator, the practical points are these.

  • No more periodic renewal. Licences and registrations no longer carry an expiry date that has to be renewed.
  • Annual fee and return still matter. The licence or registration is deemed suspended if the annual fee or the annual return is not filed, and a business cannot operate while it is suspended. This is the first thing to check, because a suspended licence is a far bigger problem than a dirty shelf.
  • New turnover categories. Basic registration now applies up to ₹1.5 crore annual turnover, a State licence from ₹1.5 crore to ₹50 crore, and a Central licence above ₹50 crore. Existing operators move categories through the FoSCoS portal: by a modification application when upgrading, or by surrendering the licence and registering afresh when moving down.
  • Wider “petty food business operator” definition. It now covers street vendors, hawkers, food trucks and temporary stalls, with instant registration certificates on submission of documents.
The coverage reviewed for this guide describes changes to licence validity, fees and categories. It does not describe a rewrite of the Schedule 4 hygiene requirements, which inspectors continue to apply. Always read the current consolidated text on the FSSAI website, because further amendments are being notified through 2026.

Which Rules Apply to a Commercial Kitchen

Licensed food service businesses work to Schedule 4 of the Food Safety and Standards (Licensing and Registration of Food Businesses) Regulations, 2011. Part II sets the general hygiene and sanitary requirements for licensed operators. Part V adds the specific requirements for catering and food service establishments such as hotels, restaurants and canteens. Between them they cover premises and layout, equipment, water, storage, food handling, personal hygiene, training, pest control, waste and record keeping. Treat Part II and Part V as one checklist: an inspector will.

The Inspection Walk-Through, Area by Area

1. Premises and layout

Floors, walls and ceilings should be smooth, sound, easy to clean and free of cracks, flaking paint and damp. Doors and windows should keep pests out. The layout should let food move from raw to cooked without crossing back. Staff toilets and changing areas should be separate from food preparation and service areas, and should not open directly into them. Lighting and ventilation should be adequate, and exhaust hoods should be clean.

2. Equipment and utensils

Food-contact surfaces should be food-grade and in good repair. Wooden chopping surfaces, chipped plastic boards and rusted racks attract attention. Separate boards and knives for raw meat, vegetables and cooked food, ideally colour-coded, are expected practice. Equipment should be cleaned and sanitised before and after use, and a cleaning schedule should show who does what and when.

3. Receiving and storage

Raw materials should come from reliable, licensed or registered suppliers, and you should keep supplier records. Check deliveries for temperature and condition before accepting them. In the fridge, keep raw meat apart from other foods and below ready-to-eat food, and keep vegetarian items separate from non-vegetarian items. Dry goods go off the floor, in clean, covered containers, and chemicals are stored away from food. Use first in, first out, and label everything with the date.

4. Preparation, cooking and holding

Inspectors watch for cross-contamination more than anything else: a knife moving from raw chicken to salad, a cloth used for everything, an unwashed hand touching a ready-to-eat item. Frozen food should be thawed hygienically. Cooked food should reach a safe core temperature, be held hot or chilled, and not sit in between. A probe thermometer should be on hand and used.

5. Personal hygiene and health

Food handlers should wear clean uniforms, keep nails trimmed, cover hair and wash hands properly at the start of work, after the toilet, after handling raw food and after touching waste. Smoking, chewing and spitting are prohibited in food areas. Schedule 4 expects food handlers to have an annual medical examination by a registered medical practitioner, and the certificates should be on file. A handler who is unwell should be taken off food duties.

6. Water, waste and pest control

Water in contact with food must be potable, and you should keep records of any testing you do. Waste should be segregated, bins should have lids and be emptied often, and the waste area should be kept clean and away from food. A licensed pest control programme should be in place with service records, and there should be no evidence of rodents, cockroaches or flies. Droppings, gnaw marks and gaps under doors are the usual findings.

7. Training and supervision

All food handlers should be trained in food hygiene and personal hygiene relevant to their work, with periodic checks that the training works. Licensed businesses are also expected to have at least one FoSTaC-certified Food Safety Supervisor for every 25 food handlers, or part of 25, on each premises. That means one supervisor for 1–25 handlers, two for 26–50, and so on. The supervisor trains other handlers and keeps the records an auditor may ask for.

These are the figures most often cited from Schedule 4 and FSSAI training material. Check the clause numbers and figures against the current text, and write your own house standard, with a margin, on your temperature logs.

Documents to Have Ready

Keep these in one folder, or one shelf, so any manager can produce them in minutes.

  • FSSAI licence or registration certificate, displayed on the premises, and the licence number on bills and invoices as the current rules require
  • Proof that the annual fee and annual return are filed, and that your licence category matches your current turnover
  • Medical fitness certificates for all food handlers, dated within the year
  • FoSTaC certificates for your Food Safety Supervisors, and training records for every handler
  • Supplier list with FSSAI numbers, and purchase invoices
  • Temperature logs for fridges, freezers, hot holding and cooking or reheating
  • Cleaning schedules and signed completion records
  • Pest control contract, service reports and treatment records
  • Water test reports, if applicable, and equipment maintenance records
  • Your food safety management plan and HACCP documents, if you operate one

A Routine That Keeps You Ready

Every shift

Check and log fridge, freezer and hot-holding temperatures. Check hand-wash basins for soap, water and paper towels. Check uniforms and nails at the start of the shift. Label and date new food. Sanitise surfaces and boards between tasks.

Every week

Walk the kitchen with a checklist as if you were the inspector: floors and drains, under equipment, behind the fryers, the back store, the bin area. Review the logs for gaps. Check the pest control station and report any activity. Check stock rotation and discard anything out of date.

Every month

Deep clean extraction hoods, filters, ice machines and storage areas. Verify thermometer accuracy. Refresh training for new staff and revisit one topic with the whole team. Check certificate and medical expiry dates, and confirm your licence record on FoSCoS.

Every year

Arrange medical examinations, review turnover against your licence category, file your annual return and fee on time, renew training and certificates, and update your food safety plan.

The Most Common Non-Compliances and How to Fix Them

  • Missing or incomplete logs. Make logging part of opening and closing, and have a supervisor sign each day.
  • Expired or missing medical certificates. Keep a simple tracker with expiry dates and renew before they lapse.
  • Raw and cooked food stored together. Fix the shelf order, label racks, and train the team on it.
  • Single chopping board and cloth for everything. Use colour-coded boards and separate cloths, and set a rule for each colour.
  • No soap or towels at hand-wash stations. Check every shift, and keep spare stock at hand.
  • Food on the floor or against walls in the store. Install racks at least a hand-span off the floor and keep goods clear of walls.
  • Uncovered bins and poor waste handling. Use lidded, foot-operated bins and a fixed emptying schedule.
  • Pest activity or gaps. Seal gaps, fit door sweeps, screen windows and act on pest control reports.
  • No certified supervisor. Count your food handlers, work out how many supervisors you need, and book FoSTaC training.
  • Licence issues. Check turnover against your category, and confirm that the annual fee and return have been filed.

On the Day of the Inspection

Greet the officer politely, ask to see identification, and let the person in charge accompany the inspection. Do not hide problems or rush to rearrange things; officers notice. Answer questions honestly and simply. If you do not know, say you will find out and follow up. Produce documents quickly from your folder. If the officer takes samples, ask for the sampling forms and keep your copies. Note down every observation made, so that you can answer in writing. Staff should be calm, know where the key records are, and be able to explain their own work, because training is tested by asking handlers directly.

After the Inspection

Read any report or notice carefully before signing, and keep your copy. If the inspection finds deficiencies, the Food Safety and Standards Act allows an officer to issue an improvement notice with a deadline. Respond in writing within the time given, say what you changed, and attach evidence such as photographs, receipts, training records and updated logs. Failure to act on a notice can lead to suspension or cancellation of a licence. Treat each finding as a process failure to fix, not a one-off to patch, and add it to your weekly walk-through.

Quick pre-inspection check: Is the licence valid and displayed? Are the annual fee and return filed? Are medical and FoSTaC certificates current? Are today’s temperature logs complete? Are raw and cooked foods separated? Is there soap at every hand-wash station? Are bins covered and the store off the floor? Is the pest control record up to date? If you can answer yes to every question, you are in good shape.
