Types of Tequila and the Top 10 Tequila Brands: A Guide for Bar Professionals

By Nigel A. Thomas, Hospitality Executive · September 20, 2026 · 11 min read · Bar & Beverage Series

Walk into any good bar today and the back shelf tells a story. Where a single bottle of gold tequila once sat beside the salt and lime, there are now blancos, reposados, añejos and bottles with hand-painted ceramic decanters. Guests ask better questions, and they expect the team to have better answers. This guide gives you those answers: what tequila is, how it is made, the six styles you need to know, and ten of the best-known brands on the world’s back bars.

At the bottom of the page you will find a black-and-gold infographic that puts the types and the ten brands on one poster, ready to download for your bar, your training room or your staff notice board.

What Is Tequila?

Tequila is a spirit made from the fermented juice of the blue Weber agave. It has a protected designation of origin, which means that real tequila can only be produced in Mexico, in the state of Jalisco and in specific municipalities of Guanajuato, Michoacán, Nayarit and Tamaulipas. The name comes from the town of Tequila in Jalisco, and the industry is overseen by the Consejo Regulador del Tequila (CRT).

The most important distinction on any label is 100% agave versus mixto. A 100% agave tequila is made only from blue Weber agave sugars. A mixto must contain at least 51% agave sugars, and the rest can come from other sources. Mixto tequila is not illegal or dishonest, but for anyone who cares about flavour, the words “100% de agave” are where to start.

A Short History of Tequila

Agave has been part of life in Mexico for thousands of years, and long before distillation arrived, indigenous communities fermented agave sap into a drink called pulque. Distilling came with the Spanish in the sixteenth century, and by the seventeenth century agave spirits were being produced around the town of Tequila in Jalisco. Over the following centuries local families built distilleries and shipped their spirit further afield, and one of the best-known early names, the Cuervo family, traces its licence to produce tequila commercially back to 1795.

Tequila’s status as a protected product is more recent. Mexico declared a denomination of origin for tequila in 1974, and the Consejo Regulador del Tequila was formed in 1994 to oversee the rules on where and how tequila can be made. Today those protections are recognised in many countries, which is why a bottle labelled tequila means something specific.

How Tequila Is Made

The agave plant takes many years to mature, commonly six to eight, so every bottle carries a long wait. The journey from field to glass follows six main stages.

  1. Harvest. Skilled field workers called jimadores cut away the long leaves with a sharp tool called a coa, leaving the heart, or piña, which looks like a large pineapple.
  2. Cooking. The piñas are cooked to turn starch into fermentable sugars. Methods include traditional brick ovens, pressure cookers called autoclaves and industrial diffusers. Slower, gentler cooking is often linked with sweeter, more rounded flavours.
  3. Milling. The cooked agave is crushed to release its juice, either with a traditional stone wheel called a tahona or with a mechanical roller mill.
  4. Fermentation. Yeast turns the sugars into alcohol in open or closed tanks. Some producers use natural yeast and others use cultivated strains.
  5. Distillation. Tequila is distilled at least twice, in pot stills or column stills, to concentrate flavour and alcohol.
  6. Ageing and bottling. Blanco goes straight to bottle, while other types rest in oak. The spirit is then diluted and bottled at between 35% and 55% alcohol by volume.

The Six Types of Tequila

The type of tequila is decided by how long it rests in oak, and a few special styles sit beside the main ageing categories. Learn these six and you can guide any guest to the right glass.

TypeAgeingCharacterBest for
Blanco (Plata / Silver)Unaged, or rested up to 2 monthsFresh, crisp, peppery, citrus and pure agaveMargaritas, Palomas, ceviche pairings
Joven (Oro / Gold)Blend of unaged and aged tequilaSoft and sweet; some are mixto with added colourMixing
Reposado2 to 12 months in oakSmooth, balanced, vanilla, caramel, light spiceNeat or premium cocktails
Añejo1 to 3 years in oak barrels of up to 600 LRich, mellow, oak, caramel, dried fruitSipping neat or over a large cube
Extra AñejoMore than 3 yearsDeep, complex, very smoothSipping neat, like a fine cognac
CristalinoAged, then filtered to remove colourClear, light, slightly sweetNeat, chilled or in a light cocktail

Blanco is the purest expression of the agave and the best way to judge a distillery. Joven, sometimes sold as oro or gold, is a blend of unaged and aged tequila, though some gold labels are mixto tequilas coloured with caramel. Reposado means “rested”, and it is the easy bridge between the fresh blanco and the richer aged styles. Añejo means “aged” and shows the influence of oak clearly. Extra añejo was created as an official category in 2006 for tequilas that spend more than three years in wood. Cristalino takes an aged tequila and filters it, often through charcoal, so that it keeps the smoothness of ageing but looks as clear as a blanco.

One useful rule of thumb: the longer tequila rests in oak, the darker and softer it becomes, and the more the barrel flavours sit alongside the agave. That does not make an older tequila better; it makes it different, and the best choice depends on how the guest wants to drink it.

Highlands vs Lowlands

Tequila regions matter, and knowing them lets you talk about flavour with confidence. Agave grown in the red, iron-rich soils of the Highlands (Los Altos) around Arandas tends to make tequila that is sweeter, fruitier and more floral. Agave from the Lowlands valley around the town of Tequila tends to give earthier, more herbal and peppery flavours. Neither is superior, and many brands are proud of a particular terroir.

Top 10 Tequila Brands

The list below is a selection of well-known brands that guests ask for by name. The numbering follows the poster and is for reference, not a quality ranking, and the product lines mentioned are examples rather than a complete range.

  1. Don Julio. A premium name known for refined consistency. Its 1942 bottling honours the year its founder, Don Julio González, began making tequila. Top products: Don Julio Blanco, Reposado and 1942.
  2. Patrón. A widely recognised, smooth and balanced brand, launched in 1989 and now part of Bacardi. Top products: Patrón Silver, Reposado and El Cielo.
  3. Casamigos. Co-founded by George Clooney and Rande Gerber, and now owned by Diageo. Smooth, slightly sweet and very approachable. Top products: Casamigos Blanco, Reposado and Añejo.
  4. Jose Cuervo. One of the world’s best-selling brands, with a heritage that goes back to 1795. Top products: Cuervo Especial Silver, Tradicional and Reserva de la Familia.
  5. Tequila Ocho. Celebrated for single-estate, terroir-driven production, where each release comes from a specific field and harvest. Top products: Ocho Plata (Blanco), Reposado and Añejo.
  6. Fortaleza. Made in the town of Tequila with traditional methods, including a stone tahona and brick ovens, and known for being additive-free. Top products: Fortaleza Blanco, Reposado and Still Strength.
  7. Espolòn. A bold, affordable option with a distinct agave flavour, made in the Highlands. Top products: Espolòn Blanco, Reposado and Añejo.
  8. Clase Azul. A high-end luxury brand famous for hand-painted ceramic decanters. Top products: Clase Azul Reposado, Gold and Añejo.
  9. 1800. A versatile, popular brand that bartenders trust for cocktails. Top products: 1800 Silver, Reposado and Cristalino.
  10. Teremana. A modern, smooth crowd-pleaser launched in 2020 by actor Dwayne Johnson. Top products: Teremana Blanco, Reposado and Añejo.

If you run a bar programme, do not try to stock all ten. Choose one dependable blanco for the well, one or two premium bottles for guests who want to trade up, and a reposado and añejo for sipping. Staff who can describe why each bottle earns its place sell more tequila than staff who only recite prices.

Common Tequila Myths

Every bar team meets the same misunderstandings, and a confident correction earns guests’ trust.

  • “Tequila is made from cactus.” Agave is a succulent, not a cactus, and it is more closely related to asparagus than to a prickly pear.
  • “There is a worm in the bottle.” That is a marketing tradition linked to some mezcals. It is not part of tequila.
  • “Gold means aged.” Not necessarily. Many gold tequilas are mixto tequilas coloured with caramel, so check the type and the 100% agave statement.
  • “Older is always better.” Ageing changes the flavour, but a fresh, well-made blanco can be more exciting than an over-oaked añejo.
  • “Tequila must be shot.” Good tequila is meant to be sipped, and it is one of the best-value neat spirits on a modern back bar.

Building a Tequila Back Bar

A well-planned range serves every guest without tying up too much stock. Think in tiers. At the base, a reliable 100% agave blanco or reposado works as the house pour for cocktails. Above it, one or two recognised premium brands give guests something to trade up to. At the top, a small selection of añejo, extra añejo or a single-estate bottling rewards the enthusiasts and supports a tasting flight. Review the list every season, keep your best-sellers well stocked, and train the team so that each bottle has a short, honest story to tell.

Tequila vs Mezcal

Guests often use the two words as if they mean the same thing. Both are Mexican agave spirits, but each has its own rules, regions and character.

How to Read a Tequila Label

A tequila label holds more information than most guests realise, and your team can use it to guide the conversation.

  • 100% de agave. No other sugars. If these words are missing, the tequila may be mixto with as little as 51% agave.
  • Type. Blanco, reposado, añejo, extra añejo or cristalino tells you how it has aged.
  • NOM number. This four-digit code identifies the distillery that made the tequila, even when several brands share it.
  • Region. Highlands tends to be sweeter and fruitier, Lowlands earthier and more peppery.
  • CRT mark. Tequila is a protected designation of origin certified in Mexico, so look for the CRT and the words “Hecho en México”.

Labels rarely tell you whether a tequila is additive-free, so if a guest asks, the honest answer is to check the producer’s own information rather than guess.

Tequila Cocktails Every Bar Should Master

A dependable tequila list does not need to be long. These five drinks cover most guests and show off the range of the spirit.

  • Margarita. Tequila, orange liqueur and fresh lime juice, shaken and served up or on the rocks. A salt rim is optional and should always be offered rather than assumed.
  • Paloma. Tequila, lime and grapefruit soda in a tall glass over ice. Simple, refreshing and hugely popular in Mexico.
  • Tequila Sunrise. Tequila and orange juice with grenadine sinking to the bottom for the sunrise effect.
  • Tommy’s Margarita. A modern classic that swaps the orange liqueur for agave syrup, which lets the tequila take centre stage.
  • Oaxaca Old Fashioned. A spirit-forward drink built on reposado tequila with a little mezcal, agave syrup and bitters, for guests who like a smoky, stirred cocktail.

Tequila also pairs well with food. Blanco works with ceviche, fresh salsas and seafood, reposado with grilled meats and tacos, and añejo with desserts, dark chocolate and cheese. Suggesting a pairing is one of the easiest ways to turn a drink order into a memorable experience.

Serving Tequila Like a Professional

Great tequila can be spoiled by careless service. These habits make the difference.

  • Match the type to the drink. Use a blanco for Margaritas and Palomas, where the fresh agave and citrus flavours shine. Save reposado and añejo for sipping or for spirit-forward cocktails.
  • Use 100% agave in cocktails. The flavour is cleaner, and guests will notice the difference in a simple drink like a Margarita or a Paloma.
  • Choose the right glass. Serve sipping tequila neat in a tulip or copita glass, not a shot glass, so that the aroma can gather.
  • Offer a flight. Blanco, reposado and añejo side by side is a low-effort way to teach guests and to increase spend.
  • Skip the salt-and-lime ritual for premium bottles. It hides the flavour that guests are paying for. A small glass of sangrita or a slice of orange is a more traditional companion.
  • Store it well. Keep bottles upright, sealed and out of direct sunlight. Tequila does not improve once it is in the bottle.
  • Serve responsibly. Know your house pour size and your local licensing rules, and train the team to pace service for guests who are drinking quickly.

Key Takeaways

  • Real tequila is made from blue Weber agave in designated regions of Mexico.
  • Look for “100% de agave” on the label.
  • The six types are blanco, joven, reposado, añejo, extra añejo and cristalino.
  • Ageing in oak changes colour and flavour; it does not automatically mean better.
  • Highlands tequila tends to be sweeter, Lowlands earthier.
  • Tequila and mezcal are related but not the same.
  • Serve sipping tequila in a tulip or copita glass, and pair each type with the right drink.

Tequila rewards curiosity. Once your team understands the agave, the ageing and the region behind each bottle, they can recommend with confidence instead of guessing, and guests feel the difference at once. Use the poster below as a quick reference on the back bar, and encourage every new team member to taste a blanco, a reposado and an añejo side by side before their first shift.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the main types of tequila?

The six styles are blanco, joven, reposado, añejo, extra añejo and cristalino. Blanco is unaged, reposado rests 2 to 12 months, añejo 1 to 3 years and extra añejo more than 3 years. Cristalino is aged tequila filtered to remove colour.

Is 100% agave tequila better?

It is made only from blue Weber agave sugars, which most bartenders prefer for flavour, especially in cocktails and for sipping. Mixto tequila can contain up to 49% other sugars.

What is the difference between tequila and mezcal?

Tequila is made only from blue Weber agave in designated regions. Mezcal can be made from many agave species, mainly in Oaxaca, and is often cooked in earthen pits, which gives it a smoky character.

Should tequila be served with salt and lime?

That ritual began as a way to soften lower-quality tequila. With a good 100% agave bottle, sip it neat from a tulip or copita glass and enjoy the flavour.

Which tequila is best for a Margarita?

A good blanco or a light reposado. Blanco keeps the drink fresh and citrus-forward, while reposado adds a touch of vanilla and oak.
