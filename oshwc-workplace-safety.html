Hospitality Safety & Compliance

OSHWC & Workplace Safety

A practical workplace-safety guide for Hotel General Managers, F&B Managers, Restaurant Managers, Executive Chefs, Kitchen Managers, Supervisors and hospitality operators in India.

The objective is simple: make workplace safety part of everyday operations rather than something that appears only when an inspection, accident or complaint occurs.

Manager's principle: Workplace safety is not only a compliance department responsibility. In a hotel, restaurant, resort, bakery, café or institutional food operation, the operating manager is one of the people best placed to identify hazards before somebody gets injured.

1. What is the OSHWC Code?

The Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 is one of India's Labour Codes. It brings together provisions relating to occupational safety, health and working conditions across covered establishments and categories of workers.

For hospitality managers, the important question is not simply whether the organisation has a copy of the Code. The practical question is whether the workplace is being operated in a way that identifies hazards, protects workers, provides appropriate working conditions, maintains records and responds properly to incidents.

Regulatory requirements can depend on the establishment, workforce, activity, state, local authority and applicable rules. Always verify the current requirement with the relevant government authority or qualified professional before making a compliance decision.

2. Why Workplace Safety is Different in Hospitality

Hospitality workplaces operate continuously around people, food, heat, electricity, gas, chemicals, machinery, wet floors, sharp equipment, manual handling, contractors, guests and time pressure.

Kitchen

  • Hot oil, steam and cooking surfaces
  • Knives, slicers and food-processing equipment
  • Wet and greasy floors
  • LPG and other cooking-fuel risks
  • Heavy stock and manual handling

Restaurant & Bar

  • Glass breakage
  • Wet floors and spills
  • Electrical equipment
  • Manual handling and service trolleys
  • Customer interaction during emergencies

Housekeeping

  • Chemical exposure
  • Wet-floor hazards
  • Manual handling
  • Cleaning machinery
  • Working at height for certain tasks

Engineering & Maintenance

  • Electrical systems
  • Plant and machinery
  • Hot work
  • Gas and fuel systems
  • Contractor activity

3. The Manager's Workplace Safety System

A practical safety system can be built around five questions:

  1. What can hurt someone? Identify hazards.
  2. Who could be affected? Employees, contractors, guests and visitors.
  3. How serious could it be? Assess the risk.
  4. What controls are already in place? Check whether they actually work.
  5. What needs to change? Assign an owner and completion date.

4. Build a Hazard Register

Every operational department should be able to identify its major workplace hazards. A simple hazard register is often more useful to an operating manager than a large document that nobody updates.

Area Hazard Potential consequence Control Owner
Kitchen Wet floor Slip/fall injury Immediate clean-up, signage, drainage and housekeeping checks Kitchen/Stewarding
Kitchen Hot oil Burn Training, PPE, controlled handling and equipment inspection Chef
Housekeeping Chemical exposure Skin/eye injury Correct dilution, labels, PPE and storage HK Manager
Engineering Electrical work Shock/fire Isolation, competent personnel and inspection Chief Engineer

5. Kitchen and Restaurant Workplace Safety

The kitchen should be treated as a high-activity work environment. Speed is important, but speed must not become an excuse for unsafe work.

  • Keep floors clean, dry and free of unnecessary obstruction.
  • Store knives safely and maintain sharp equipment correctly.
  • Keep handles positioned so they cannot easily be struck or caught.
  • Control access to hot oil, steam and high-temperature equipment.
  • Inspect cooking equipment and extraction systems.
  • Keep emergency exits and access routes clear.
  • Do not allow damaged electrical equipment to remain in service.
  • Train new employees before assigning hazardous tasks.

6. Housekeeping and Chemical Safety

Cleaning chemicals can create serious hazards when incorrectly stored, mixed, diluted or transferred into unlabelled containers.

  • Maintain clearly identified chemical containers.
  • Follow manufacturer instructions for dilution and use.
  • Keep incompatible chemicals separated.
  • Provide appropriate PPE for the task.
  • Train employees before assigning chemical-cleaning duties.
  • Keep chemical storage away from food and food-contact materials.
  • Maintain access to relevant safety information.

7. Manual Handling and Ergonomics

Hospitality employees repeatedly move boxes, beverage crates, food containers, linen, furniture, luggage and equipment. Repeated lifting can cause injury even when there is no dramatic accident.

Reduce the load

Use smaller containers or divide heavy loads where practical.

Improve the route

Keep corridors, stairs and delivery routes clear.

Use equipment

Use trolleys, carts and mechanical assistance where appropriate.

Train employees

Teach safe handling techniques and recognise early signs of strain.

8. Working Conditions Managers Should Inspect

A manager's safety walk should look beyond fire extinguishers. Examine the actual working environment.

  • Lighting
  • Ventilation
  • Temperature and heat exposure
  • Noise where relevant
  • Cleanliness and housekeeping
  • Sanitation and welfare facilities
  • Drinking water arrangements
  • Access and egress
  • Emergency routes
  • Condition of floors, stairs and handrails

9. Personal Protective Equipment

PPE should not be treated as a substitute for safe equipment, engineering controls or proper procedures. Where PPE is required, employees should know what is required, why it is required, how to use it and how to report defective equipment.

Task Possible PPE consideration
Kitchen heat-related tasks Task-appropriate protective equipment
Chemical cleaning Gloves, eye/face protection as appropriate
Maintenance Task-specific PPE based on hazard assessment
Waste handling Appropriate gloves and protective equipment

10. Electrical, Gas and Maintenance Safety

Hospitality properties contain a large number of electrical appliances, refrigeration systems, cooking equipment, pumps, motors and other services. Kitchens may also operate gas systems.

  • Do not use visibly damaged electrical equipment.
  • Keep electrical panels and isolation points accessible.
  • Use competent personnel for electrical and technical work.
  • Maintain equipment inspection and service records.
  • Control hot work and other high-risk maintenance activities.
  • Check gas systems, connections and associated safety arrangements.
  • Keep combustible materials away from heat sources.

11. Contractors and Temporary Workers

Contractors can introduce risks that are not present during normal hotel operations. A contractor safety process should therefore exist before work starts.

  • Verify contractor identity and authorisation.
  • Explain site safety rules.
  • Identify the work area and access route.
  • Control high-risk activities.
  • Confirm required PPE.
  • Maintain appropriate permits or work controls where applicable.
  • Inspect the work area after completion.

12. Emergency Preparedness

An emergency plan is useful only if employees know what to do. Managers should regularly test whether employees understand alarms, evacuation routes, assembly points, emergency contacts and their assigned responsibilities.

Ask: If an emergency happened right now, could the employee on the floor explain what to do without waiting for the manager to arrive?

13. Accident, Incident and Near-Miss Management

A near miss is an opportunity to correct a hazard before somebody is injured. Managers should encourage reporting rather than creating a culture where employees hide mistakes because they fear blame.

  • Record what happened.
  • Identify the immediate cause.
  • Look for the underlying cause.
  • Take corrective action.
  • Assign responsibility.
  • Set a completion date.
  • Verify that the corrective action actually worked.

14. Training and Competency

Safety training should be connected to the actual job. A generic induction is not enough for someone operating a fryer, slicer, compactor, chemical-cleaning system, gas appliance or maintenance tool.

  • New employee induction
  • Department-specific training
  • Equipment-specific training
  • Emergency training
  • Refresher training
  • Supervisor verification of competency
  • Training records

15. Employee Welfare and Working Conditions

Managers should regularly inspect employee welfare arrangements rather than assuming that they remain adequate because they were acceptable when the property opened.

  • Drinking water
  • Sanitation and toilets
  • Rest and welfare arrangements where applicable
  • Clean employee areas
  • Safe access to work areas
  • Appropriate working conditions
  • Mechanisms for reporting safety concerns

16. Daily, Weekly and Monthly Manager Routine

Every Day

  • Walk key operating areas.
  • Check exits and access routes.
  • Look for slips, trips and unsafe equipment.
  • Correct immediate hazards.

Every Week

  • Review open safety actions.
  • Inspect high-risk equipment.
  • Review incidents and near misses.
  • Speak with employees.

Every Month

  • Review the hazard register.
  • Review training records.
  • Audit contractor controls.
  • Check documentation and evidence.

After an Incident

  • Make the area safe.
  • Provide appropriate assistance.
  • Report and document as required.
  • Investigate and correct the cause.

17. Workplace Safety Compliance File

A practical compliance file should allow a manager to demonstrate that safety is actively managed.

  • Safety policy and procedures
  • Risk assessments / hazard registers
  • Training records
  • Equipment inspection and maintenance records
  • Incident and near-miss records
  • Emergency procedures and drill records
  • Contractor records
  • PPE issue or control records where applicable
  • Corrective-action tracking
  • Relevant licences, certificates and statutory records

18. What an Inspector Should See

Inspection readiness should not mean creating paperwork the day before an inspection. The workplace should visibly demonstrate that safety is part of the management system.

  • Employees know basic safety procedures.
  • Hazards are identified and corrected.
  • Equipment is maintained.
  • Emergency routes are accessible.
  • Records are organised.
  • Managers can explain their safety process.
  • Corrective actions are tracked to completion.

19. The 30-Minute GM Safety Walk

A General Manager can conduct a focused safety walk without waiting for a formal audit.

Minutes Focus
0–5 Entrances, exits, corridors and emergency routes
5–12 Kitchen, cooking equipment, floors and storage
12–17 Restaurant, bar and guest-facing work areas
17–22 Housekeeping, chemical storage and service areas
22–27 Engineering, electrical and maintenance areas
27–30 Speak to employees and assign corrective actions

20. Questions Every Manager Should Ask

What is the most dangerous task in this department?

If the supervisor cannot answer this, the department probably needs a better hazard identification process.

What happened during the last near miss?

A mature safety culture learns from near misses instead of waiting for an injury.

Which employee is doing a hazardous task without recent training?

Training needs should be identified from actual operations and changes in equipment.

What safety corrective action is overdue?

Open actions should have a named owner and a realistic completion date.

21. Contractor and Outsourced Operation Checks

Outsourced services do not automatically remove operational responsibility for maintaining a safe workplace. Managers should understand who is responsible for what and ensure that contractors follow the property's safety controls.

  • Cleaning contractors
  • Pest-control contractors
  • Kitchen-equipment technicians
  • Lift and escalator contractors
  • Electrical contractors
  • Fire-system contractors
  • Gas-system contractors
  • Construction and maintenance contractors

22. Linking OSHWC With Wider Hospitality Compliance

Workplace safety does not operate in isolation. A hotel or restaurant manager may simultaneously deal with food safety, fire safety, electrical safety, environmental requirements, labour requirements, legal metrology, licensing, public health and local authority requirements.

That is why a practical compliance system should use one central calendar, one document-control process and clearly assigned departmental responsibilities.

For food-safety operations, see the FSSAI Kitchen Inspection Guide. For the wider hospitality framework, see the Safety & Compliance hub.

23. Central, State and Local Requirements

Hospitality businesses should not assume that one national reference answers every compliance question. Labour, workplace, fire, municipal, building, environmental, public-health and licensing requirements can involve different authorities.

Managers should therefore identify the competent authority for their property and verify current requirements before relying on a checklist or historical practice.

25. Final Workplace Safety Checklist

  • Hazards have been identified.
  • High-risk activities have appropriate controls.
  • Employees receive relevant training.
  • PPE requirements are understood and controlled.
  • Equipment is inspected and maintained.
  • Emergency arrangements are understood.
  • Access and emergency routes are clear.
  • Incidents and near misses are recorded.
  • Corrective actions are tracked.
  • Contractors are controlled.
  • Employee welfare facilities are inspected.
  • Safety documentation can be produced when required.
  • Managers regularly walk the workplace.
  • Current legal and regulatory requirements are verified.

26. Final Operational Message

The strongest workplace-safety system is not the thickest manual. It is the system that employees understand, supervisors use and managers regularly verify.

In hospitality, safety is built into the operation: how food is prepared, how chemicals are stored, how equipment is maintained, how employees move stock, how contractors work and how emergencies are handled.

A good manager does not wait for an accident or inspection to discover a hazard. The manager walks the operation, asks questions, listens to employees, corrects unsafe conditions and follows the action through to completion.
