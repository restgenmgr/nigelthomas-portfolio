What Is EBITDA? A Practical Guide for Restaurant & F&B Leaders

By Nigel A. Thomas · July 2026

EBITDA at a glance — how it's calculated, where it's used, and what it doesn't tell you.

Infographic reference: Oana Labes, MBA, CPA · oanalabes.com

What EBITDA Actually Means

EBITDA stands for Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization. It is one of the most widely used measures of operating performance — and one of the most widely misunderstood.

In simple terms, EBITDA shows how profitable your operations are before financing decisions, tax treatment, and non-cash accounting adjustments get layered on top. It strips the noise away and leaves you with a clean read on operating profit.

The core idea: EBITDA is a measure of operating profit before financing, tax, and non-cash accounting decisions — not a measure of how much cash the business actually generated.

Two Ways to Calculate EBITDA

EBITDA can be built from the top of the P&L down, or from the bottom up. Both should land on the same number.

Top-Down: Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold − Operating Expenses (excluding D&A, interest, and other financing items)
Bottom-Up: Net Income + Taxes + Interest Expense + Depreciation & Amortization

Why EBITDA Matters in Restaurants and F&B

EBITDA helps you understand:

  • How efficiently your restaurant generates profit from operations
  • Your ability to cover fixed costs like rent and salaries
  • Your overall performance when comparing outlets or branches

For example, two restaurants can post identical revenue, yet the one with tighter cost control — food, labour, waste — will show a stronger EBITDA. Revenue tells you how much came in the door; EBITDA tells you how much of it the operation actually kept.

EBITDA isn't purely a finance metric — it's driven by what happens on the floor and in the kitchen every day:

  • Revenue — sales performance, menu pricing, upselling
  • COGS — food cost control, supplier management, waste reduction
  • Operating expenses — labour efficiency, scheduling, utilities

Every shift, every order, and every waste decision moves EBITDA — whether or not anyone is watching the number that day.

Where EBITDA Is Used

  • M&A valuation — a common basis for pricing a business sale
  • Loan sizing — lenders use it to judge how much debt an operation can carry
  • Covenant setting — loan agreements are often built around EBITDA thresholds
  • Performance benchmarking — comparing outlets, brands, or competitors on a like-for-like basis
  • Executive compensation — bonus structures frequently reference EBITDA targets
  • Capital allocation — deciding where to reinvest across a portfolio

The Reality: What EBITDA Does NOT Show

This is where many managers get it wrong. EBITDA is a powerful number — but it is an incomplete one.

EBITDA is not cash flow. Treat it as a starting point, never as a conclusion.
  • It ignores CapEx — strong EBITDA can mask heavy cash reinvestment needs for equipment and renovations.
  • It excludes working capital changes — receivables growth, inventory build-up, and payables timing have no place in EBITDA.
  • It masks debt burden — interest is stripped out, so a business can look healthy on EBITDA while carrying significant debt.
  • It's easy to manipulate — add-backs and adjustments can push EBITDA well beyond true cash earnings.
  • There's no standardized definition — EBITDA has no GAAP definition, so calculations and "adjusted EBITDA" quality can vary widely between businesses.

A restaurant can show strong EBITDA on paper and still struggle to make payroll. That gap between the reported number and the cash in the bank is exactly why EBITDA needs to be read alongside cash flow, not instead of it.

EBITDA vs. Operational Truth

In F&B, it's entirely possible to see:

  • A healthy EBITDA number
  • Alongside poor kitchen efficiency
  • High labour cost
  • Slow service

This means the operation looks profitable on paper but is weak in reality. EBITDA is a lagging summary of what already happened financially — it won't tell you why, and it won't tell you what's about to go wrong on the floor.

How Strong Leaders Use EBITDA

Smart F&B leaders don't stop at EBITDA. They connect it to the operational metrics that actually drive it:

  • Food cost %
  • Labour cost %
  • Contribution margin per dish
  • Table turnover
  • Average check

EBITDA is the result, not the driver. It's a useful indicator of operational performance — but only a starting point for a proper diagnosis.

Final Thought

In Food & Beverage:

  • Operations drive EBITDA.
  • Leadership controls operations.
  • Systems protect profitability.
Bottom line: If you focus only on EBITDA, you manage numbers. If you focus on operations, you control the business.
