Wine Terminology: 16 Essential Terms for Hospitality Professionals

Sixteen essential wine terms explained in plain language, with examples and service notes for hospitality teams.

By Nigel A. Thomas, Hospitality Executive • Beverage Series • October 2026

Introduction

Wine vocabulary is a shared language. It connects the guest, the server, the sommelier and the buyer, and when it is used well, it builds confidence on both sides of the table. A guest who hears a clear, accurate description of a wine feels looked after and is far more likely to order a second bottle. A server who stumbles through jargon, or avoids it completely, loses an easy chance to add value. That is why wine terminology is one of the first topics I cover in beverage training for hospitality teams.

This guide explains sixteen essential terms, the same sixteen shown on the free poster at the bottom of this page. They cover body, vintage, appearance, aroma, structure, faults, service and origin. After more than thirty years in hotels, resorts and cruise lines across India, the Middle East and the USA, I have found that these are the words that come up most often in real service, and the ones that make the biggest difference when staff understand them properly.

Why wine language matters in service

Terminology is a tool, not a performance. The aim is never to impress the guest with difficult words. It is to translate a complex product into simple, accurate language that helps the guest decide. A guest who says they want something smooth is describing tannin and acidity without knowing it. A good server hears that and recommends a medium-bodied red with soft tannins. Learn the technical term for yourself, and then speak in the guest's words.

Good wine service also depends on consistency. If every member of the team uses the same words in the same way, guests receive the same quality of advice at every table and in every outlet. A shared glossary, a short pre-shift tasting and regular practice with real bottles will do more for a wine programme than any list of expensive labels. Use the poster on this page as a quick reference on the wall of the training room or the back of house.

16 wine terms explained

Full-Bodied

Describing the wine • Body

Body describes the weight and fullness of a wine in the mouth. Many professionals compare it with milk: skimmed milk is light, whole milk is medium and cream is full. A full-bodied wine feels rich, weighty and mouth-filling. Alcohol is the biggest influence, because higher alcohol adds a sense of viscosity, but tannin, residual sweetness and flavour concentration also play a part. Warm climates, very ripe grapes and oak ageing tend to produce fuller wines. Typical examples include Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah, Malbec and oaked Chardonnay. These wines suit grilled steak, braised meats and aged cheeses, where the richness of the food balances the weight of the wine. Full-bodied does not mean better. It is a style, and it can overpower delicate dishes such as poached fish.

Service note: Suggest it with robust dishes, and ask the guest whether they enjoy wines that feel rich and bold.

Medium-Bodied

Describing the wine • Body

Medium-bodied wines sit between the light and full styles, with moderate weight, alcohol and texture. The term is relative, so it only makes sense when you compare one wine with another. Merlot, Sangiovese and many Grenache wines are often described this way, and some Chenin Blanc falls into the same group depending on how it is made. Because medium-bodied wines are neither thin nor heavy, they are the most flexible partners at the table. They work with roast chicken, pork, pasta in tomato sauce and many vegetarian dishes. For a hospitality professional they are also the safest suggestion when a guest is unsure, or when a table orders a mix of dishes and wants one bottle to cover them all.

Service note: Offer a medium-bodied bottle when one table is ordering different dishes.

Light-Bodied

Describing the wine • Body

A light-bodied wine feels delicate on the palate. It usually has lower alcohol, firm acidity and a refreshing, almost juicy character. Cool climates and early picking tend to produce this style. Pinot Grigio, Pinot Noir and Gamay, the grape of Beaujolais, are common examples, and many dry Rieslings also feel light in weight. These wines suit salads, seafood, light starters and warm-weather menus, where a heavier wine would feel tiring. They are often served a little cooler than fuller wines, which keeps them lively. Do not confuse light with simple. A light-bodied wine can still be complex and elegant, and some of the most prized wines in the world are built on finesse rather than power.

Service note: Recommend it for starters, seafood and long lunches, and serve it well chilled.

Vintage

Reading the label • Label

Vintage is the year in which the grapes were harvested, not the year the wine was bottled. It tells you something about the growing season: a warm, dry summer, a wet spring or an early frost can all change the character of the wine. In regions with variable weather, such as Bordeaux and Burgundy, vintage matters a great deal, and guests who follow wine closely will often ask for it. Some wines are sold as non-vintage, which means they blend wines from several years. This is standard for Champagne, because blending allows the house to keep a consistent style. A good vintage is not a guarantee of quality, and the producer and the vineyard still matter more than the year alone. On a wine list, accuracy is essential, because a wrong vintage is a wrong product.

Service note: Read the vintage aloud when presenting the bottle and confirm it matches the list.

Legs

Looking at the wine • Appearance

Legs, also called tears, are the streaks that form on the inside of the glass after you swirl the wine. They appear because alcohol evaporates faster than water, which changes the surface tension and pulls the liquid into droplets that slide back down. Thick, slow legs usually point to higher alcohol or residual sugar, which is why dessert and fortified wines often show them. What legs do not show is quality. A cheap, strong wine and a great one can produce the same pattern. This is one of the most common wine myths, and it is worth correcting politely. Used properly, legs are just a visual clue about body, and a pleasant moment of theatre at the table.

Service note: Use legs to explain body. Never use them to promise quality.

Swirl

Looking at the wine • Tasting

Swirling means gently rotating the wine in the glass. It does two things. It brings the wine into contact with oxygen, and it increases the surface area, so more of the volatile aroma compounds escape into the glass where you can smell them. Good technique is simple. Fill the glass to roughly a third, hold it by the stem or base, keep the base on the table at first and move in small circles. Then bring the glass to the nose. Swirl before smelling, not after, and stop if the wine begins to climb the sides. Sparkling wines rarely need much swirling, because the bubbles already carry aroma upward, and vigorous movement only flattens the mousse.

Service note: Pour a third of a glass so the guest has room to swirl.

Aroma

Smelling the wine • Smell

Aroma is used in two ways. In everyday speech it means any smell that comes from the wine. In stricter tasting language it refers to the fresh scents that come directly from the grape and from fermentation, such as citrus, apple, blackcurrant, rose or fresh herbs. Different grapes have recognisable aromatic signatures. Sauvignon Blanc often shows grass and passion fruit, Riesling shows lime and apple, and Gewurztraminer is known for lychee and rose petals. Most of what we call taste is really smell, which is why nose and palate are linked. Training your team to identify a handful of common aromas improves their descriptions and gives guests the vocabulary to choose a wine they will enjoy.

Service note: Describe young wines by their aromas, using two or three simple fruit or floral words.

Bouquet

Smelling the wine • Smell

Bouquet refers to the more complex scents that develop as wine is made and matured. Oak barrels can add vanilla, toast and spice. Time in the bottle can bring leather, mushroom, tobacco, forest floor and dried fruit. These notes appear slowly, so bouquet is the word most often used for mature wines. Many people use aroma and bouquet interchangeably, and in casual service that is acceptable, but the distinction is useful for training. Aroma is the freshness of the fruit, and bouquet is the depth that comes from winemaking and age. When a guest says an older wine smells earthy and savoury rather than fruity, that is bouquet at work, and it is usually a sign that the wine has evolved well.

Service note: Use bouquet for older wines and aromas for young, fruity ones.

Acidity

Structure • Structure

Acidity is the freshness in a wine, the quality that makes your mouth water and keeps the flavours lively. It comes from natural acids in the grape, mainly tartaric and malic acid. Cooler climates generally produce wines with higher acidity, and grapes such as Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc and Chenin Blanc are known for it. Acidity balances sweetness and richness, so it is essential in dessert wines and in creamy dishes. It also makes wine a good companion to food, because it cuts through fat and oil in the same way that a squeeze of lemon does. Without enough acidity a wine tastes flat or heavy, and with too much it can taste sharp. The skill lies in balance.

Service note: Match high-acid wines with oily fish, fried dishes and salads with dressing.

Tannin

Structure • Structure

Tannins are natural compounds found in grape skins, seeds and stems, and they can also come from oak barrels. They create the dry, grippy, slightly astringent feeling on your gums and cheeks, similar to strong black tea. Tannin is mostly a feature of red wines, because they ferment with their skins, and it gives them structure and the ability to age. Cabernet Sauvignon, Nebbiolo and Syrah are well known for it. With time, tannins soften and the wine feels smoother. Tannin binds with proteins, which is why a tannic red wine tastes softer next to a juicy steak. It can clash with very spicy food or delicate fish, where it may taste harsh or metallic.

Service note: Explain tannin as a drying feeling, similar to strong tea, and pair it with protein-rich dishes.

Finish

Tasting • Tasting

The finish is what remains after you swallow or spit the wine. It covers both flavour and sensation, such as lingering fruit, spice, oak, warmth or a gentle grip of tannin. Professionals describe the finish as short, medium or long. A long finish, where pleasant flavours last for many seconds, is one of the signs of a quality wine, together with balance, complexity and concentration. A short finish disappears quickly and leaves little behind. The finish is also the final impression a guest takes away, which makes it important in a tasting or a pairing dinner. Encourage your team to wait a few seconds after each sip and notice what is still present on the palate before they describe the wine.

Service note: Describe the finish as short, medium or long, then name one flavour that stays.

Corked

Faults and service • Fault

A corked wine is one affected by cork taint, caused by a compound called TCA, short for 2,4,6-trichloroanisole. It smells of damp cardboard, wet newspaper or a musty cellar, and the fruit seems muted or absent. The wine is not dangerous, but it tastes unpleasant, and it will not improve. Cork taint affects only a small share of bottles sealed with natural cork, and it cannot be seen from outside. Screw caps and synthetic closures do not cause it. Corked is often confused with cork crumbs in the glass, which are harmless, and with other faults such as oxidation. Staff should know the difference and be confident in handling a guest who believes a bottle is faulty.

Service note: Accept the guest's concern politely, replace the bottle without argument and follow your supplier credit procedure.

Decanting

Faults and service • Service

Decanting means pouring wine from its bottle into a decanter. It has two purposes. For older red wines, it separates the clear liquid from sediment that has formed over the years. For young, powerful reds, it exposes the wine to air, which can soften tannins and open up aromas. Technique matters. Stand the bottle upright beforehand, pour slowly in one steady motion and watch the neck of the bottle against a light. Stop when the sediment approaches. Very old and delicate wines should be decanted gently and only shortly before service, because too much air can flatten them. A decanter also brings a sense of occasion to the table, which suits fine dining.

Service note: Stop pouring when sediment reaches the neck of the bottle.

Sediment

Faults and service • Service

Sediment is the natural deposit that can form in a bottle, especially in older red wines and in wines bottled without heavy filtering. It may look like dark flakes, fine grit or a coating on the bottom of the bottle. White wines can show tiny crystals, called tartrates or wine diamonds, which are harmless crystals of tartaric acid. Neither type is a fault. They are a sign that the wine has not been stripped of its character. The problem is simply texture, because sediment in the glass tastes gritty and looks unattractive. Guests who are unfamiliar with it may think something is wrong, so a short, calm explanation prevents a complaint. Careful handling and decanting leave the sediment behind.

Service note: Stand older bottles upright for several hours before opening them.

Ullage

Faults and service • Storage

Ullage is the empty space between the wine and the closure in a bottle. Every bottle has a little, which is normal. In older bottles, however, the fill level matters, because wine can slowly evaporate or seep past the cork over time. Cellar records describe fill levels such as into the neck, high shoulder, mid shoulder and low shoulder. A level that is unusually low suggests that air has been in contact with the wine, which raises the risk of oxidation. It may also point to poor storage, such as heat or dry conditions. Buyers and sommeliers check ullage before purchasing a rare bottle or accepting one into the cellar, because the fill level affects both condition and value.

Service note: Inspect and record the fill level of older bottles when receiving them.

Terroir

Origin • Origin

Terroir is a French term for the combination of natural factors that give a wine its sense of place. It includes soil, climate, altitude, slope and the direction a vineyard faces. Many people also include the traditions and practices of the local growers. The idea is that the same grape grown in two different sites will produce two different wines, and that these differences are worth protecting. Terroir is the thinking behind appellation systems in France and Italy, and it explains why Burgundy values individual vineyards so highly. Not everyone agrees on how much terroir matters compared with the winemaker, but the concept remains a powerful way to tell a story and to explain why a wine tastes the way it does.

Service note: Give the guest the story of the place in a single sentence when you recommend a bottle.

Faults, storage and safe service

Several of these terms are really about quality control. Corked, sediment and ullage all help a team judge the condition of a bottle before it reaches the guest. When opening a bottle, the server should check the cork for damage or seepage, smell it for obvious faults and pour a small taste for the host. If the wine smells of damp cardboard, it is probably corked and should be replaced. If it looks cloudy in a way that is not sediment, or smells like vinegar or bruised apple, it may be oxidised or spoiled and should also be replaced.

Storage protects everything else. Bottles with natural cork should be kept on their sides in a cool, stable place away from light and vibration, so that the cork stays moist. Heat is the greatest enemy, because it accelerates ageing and can push the cork out. For safe service, serve alcohol responsibly, follow local licensing law, check identification when required and watch for guests who have had enough. Allergen and sulphite questions are common, so train your team to say honestly what they know and to check with the kitchen or the label when they do not.

Putting the terms to work

Here is how the terms work together in a real tasting note. This is a full-bodied red from an older vintage. The aroma is dark fruit, and the bouquet shows leather and tobacco. The acidity is fresh, the tannin is firm but softening and the finish is long. There is a little sediment in the bottle, so it should be decanted. Every word in that description helps the guest imagine the wine, and every term maps to something the server can check, explain and recommend a dish to match.

For menu design, group wines by style and use plain descriptors alongside the technical terms. A short line such as light-bodied, crisp and citrusy tells the guest more than a long list of grape varieties. Train staff to ask one question, such as whether the guest prefers lighter or fuller wines, and then offer two options instead of ten. That approach respects the guest, speeds up service and increases the average spend on wine.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between aroma and bouquet?

Aroma usually describes the fresh scents from the grape and fermentation. Bouquet describes the more complex scents that develop through oak and ageing, so it is most often used for mature wines.

Do wine legs show quality?

No. Legs show alcohol and sugar levels, which relate to body. Many ordinary wines produce long legs and many excellent wines produce short ones.

Is sediment a wine fault?

No. Sediment is a natural deposit and is harmless. It is usually separated by standing the bottle upright and decanting carefully.

What does vintage versus non-vintage mean?

A vintage wine comes from grapes harvested in a single year, which is printed on the label. A non-vintage wine blends several years, a method used widely in Champagne to keep a consistent house style.
