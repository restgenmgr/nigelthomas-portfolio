Commercial Kitchen Operations Academy • Module 5

Kitchen Safety & Workplace Safety

A practical guide to preventing injuries, controlling workplace hazards and creating a safer commercial kitchen environment.

For chefs, kitchen managers, supervisors, stewards, food handlers and hospitality professionals.

Operational principle: Kitchen safety is not a poster on a wall. It is a daily management system built into preparation, production, service, cleaning, maintenance, supervision and staff behaviour.

1. Why Kitchen Safety Matters

Commercial kitchens combine heat, sharp tools, electricity, gas, chemicals, wet floors, heavy equipment and fast-moving teams. During busy service, these hazards can become more serious because people are working quickly and communication can deteriorate.

A professional kitchen therefore needs safety controls that are practical, visible and consistently followed. Managers should identify hazards before they cause an incident, correct unsafe conditions and make sure employees understand the correct method of working.

2. The Main Kitchen Safety Hazards

Sharp Objects

Knives, slicers, mandolines, graters and other cutting equipment can cause serious injuries when used, stored or cleaned incorrectly.

Heat and Burns

Ovens, ranges, fryers, steam, hot oil, pans and heated equipment create significant burn and scald hazards.

Slips and Falls

Water, oil, food debris, grease and poorly stored equipment can create slip and trip hazards.

Electrical Hazards

Damaged cords, overloaded connections, wet electrical equipment and poor maintenance can create shock or fire risks.

Gas and Fuel

Gas appliances and fuel systems require appropriate installation, inspection, ventilation and emergency procedures.

Chemicals

Cleaning chemicals can cause burns, poisoning, respiratory irritation or dangerous reactions if incorrectly stored or mixed.

3. Knife Safety

Knives are among the most frequently used tools in a professional kitchen. Good knife safety begins with selecting the correct knife for the task and maintaining it properly.

  • Use the correct knife for the job.
  • Keep knives clean and in good condition.
  • Use a stable cutting board.
  • Keep the cutting area organised.
  • Cut away from the body where appropriate.
  • Keep fingers away from the cutting path.
  • Do not leave knives hidden under food, cloths or in sinks.
  • Carry knives with the point controlled and blade facing safely.
  • Never attempt to catch a falling knife.
  • Store knives in a designated rack, block or other controlled storage system.

4. Safe Use of Slicers and Cutting Equipment

Mechanical slicers, processors, mixers and other cutting equipment require specific operating procedures. Employees should be trained before using equipment and should never bypass guards or safety devices.

  • Read and follow the equipment manufacturer's instructions.
  • Use guards and protective devices correctly.
  • Keep hands away from moving blades.
  • Switch off equipment before cleaning or clearing blockages.
  • Disconnect or isolate equipment where required by the procedure.
  • Never improvise a repair while the equipment is operating.

5. Burns, Scalds and Hot Surfaces

Burns are common kitchen injuries. Employees should be trained to recognise hot surfaces, steam hazards, hot oil and hot containers before handling them.

  • Use appropriate dry heat-resistant protection when handling hot equipment.
  • Keep pan handles positioned safely and away from traffic routes where appropriate.
  • Open steam-producing equipment carefully and stand clear of escaping steam.
  • Warn other employees when carrying hot liquids or equipment.
  • Use suitable containers for hot food.
  • Do not overfill containers with hot liquids.
  • Keep hot equipment areas organised.
Important: Never use an improvised treatment for a serious burn. Follow the workplace first-aid procedure and seek appropriate medical assistance for injuries requiring professional assessment.

6. Fryer and Hot-Oil Safety

Deep fryers combine high temperatures and oil, making them a particularly important safety area.

  • Only trained employees should operate commercial fryers.
  • Keep water away from hot oil.
  • Do not overfill fryer baskets.
  • Lower food carefully to reduce splashing.
  • Follow the manufacturer's operating and cleaning instructions.
  • Allow oil and equipment to cool before cleaning according to procedure.
  • Keep fryer surroundings free from unnecessary combustible materials.

7. Slips, Trips and Falls

A wet or greasy floor can become dangerous within seconds. Slip prevention must therefore be part of normal kitchen work rather than an occasional cleaning exercise.

  • Clean spills immediately.
  • Use appropriate warning signage or barriers when required.
  • Keep walkways clear.
  • Store boxes, equipment and utensils in designated areas.
  • Keep floors maintained and in suitable condition.
  • Wear appropriate slip-resistant footwear where required by the workplace.
  • Do not run in the kitchen.

8. Electrical Safety

Commercial kitchens use many electrical appliances. Electricity and water create a serious hazard combination, so electrical controls must be taken seriously.

  • Do not operate electrical equipment with damaged cords or plugs.
  • Keep electrical connections away from water.
  • Do not overload sockets or extension systems.
  • Keep electrical equipment clean according to manufacturer instructions.
  • Never use damaged equipment simply because service is busy.
  • Repairs should be performed by appropriately qualified personnel.
  • Report sparks, burning smells, shocks or abnormal operation immediately.

9. Gas and LPG Safety

Gas systems require competent installation, maintenance and inspection. Kitchen employees should know the site's approved operating and emergency procedures rather than improvising.

  • Know the location of approved gas shut-off controls.
  • Keep ventilation systems functioning.
  • Report suspected leaks immediately.
  • Do not create ignition sources around a suspected gas leak.
  • Never attempt unauthorised gas repairs.
  • Keep gas equipment clean and maintained.
  • Follow the site's emergency evacuation procedure when required.
Gas emergency: If a gas leak is suspected, follow the property's approved emergency procedure immediately. Do not use switches, flames or other possible ignition sources in an area where gas may be present. Evacuate and contact the appropriate emergency or qualified service personnel according to site procedure.

10. Fire Prevention

Fire prevention is a management responsibility shared by every employee. Commercial kitchens contain ignition sources, cooking oils, electrical equipment, packaging and other combustible materials.

  • Keep cooking equipment clean and properly maintained.
  • Prevent grease accumulation.
  • Keep combustible materials away from heat sources.
  • Maintain required clearance around equipment.
  • Keep emergency exits and access routes clear.
  • Know the location of site-approved fire safety equipment.
  • Do not obstruct fire doors, extinguishers or emergency equipment.
  • Follow the property's fire safety and evacuation plan.

11. Fire Extinguisher Awareness

Employees should receive training appropriate to their workplace and local fire-safety requirements. A fire extinguisher should never be treated as a substitute for evacuation, emergency notification or professional fire response.

Remember: Only attempt to control a small fire if you are trained, the correct equipment is available, the situation is within the property's approved procedure and a safe escape route remains available. Otherwise evacuate and raise the alarm.

12. Chemical Safety

Cleaning chemicals are essential to kitchen hygiene but can create serious risks if handled incorrectly.

  • Keep chemicals in labelled containers.
  • Follow manufacturer instructions and workplace procedures.
  • Never mix chemicals unless the manufacturer specifically permits it.
  • Store chemicals away from food and food-contact items.
  • Use required personal protective equipment.
  • Keep safety information available as required by the workplace.
  • Report spills or exposure immediately.

13. Personal Protective Equipment

PPE should be selected according to the task and workplace risk assessment. PPE is one layer of protection and should not replace safer work methods, engineering controls or proper training.

Task or HazardPossible Control
Cutting and food preparationAppropriate knife practices and task-specific protective equipment where required.
Heat and hot equipmentSuitable heat-resistant protection and safe handling procedures.
Chemical cleaningAppropriate gloves, eye protection or other PPE specified by the chemical and workplace procedure.
Wet workSuitable footwear and protective equipment according to workplace requirements.

14. Manual Handling and Lifting

Commercial kitchens regularly move food boxes, beverage containers, cooking equipment and waste. Poor lifting technique or excessive loads can cause musculoskeletal injuries.

  • Assess the load before lifting.
  • Use carts or mechanical assistance where available.
  • Ask for assistance with heavy or awkward loads.
  • Keep the load controlled.
  • Avoid twisting while carrying heavy loads.
  • Keep pathways clear before moving equipment or supplies.
  • Store heavy items in locations that reduce unnecessary lifting risk.

15. Safe Equipment Operation

Every piece of commercial kitchen equipment should have a clear operating method. New employees should not be expected to learn by trial and error.

  • Provide equipment-specific training.
  • Use manufacturer instructions.
  • Keep guards and safety devices in place.
  • Report faults immediately.
  • Do not operate equipment known to be unsafe.
  • Use isolation procedures where required by the workplace.
  • Keep maintenance records according to the property's system.

16. Safe Cleaning Practices

Cleaning activities can create additional hazards through wet floors, chemicals, steam, hot equipment and temporary obstruction of work areas.

  • Follow the approved cleaning SOP.
  • Use appropriate signage or barriers for wet floors.
  • Do not clean hot equipment unless the manufacturer's procedure permits it.
  • Use chemicals according to instructions.
  • Store cleaning equipment safely after use.
  • Keep cleaning activity coordinated with production and service.

17. Emergency Procedures

Every commercial kitchen should have site-specific emergency procedures covering foreseeable incidents. Employees should know how to raise an alarm, who to contact and where to assemble.

Every Employee Should Know

  • Emergency contact procedures.
  • Emergency exit routes.
  • Assembly point location.
  • Location of relevant emergency equipment.
  • Who the designated supervisors are.
  • How to report an injury or dangerous condition.
  • When to evacuate rather than attempt to control an incident.

18. Incident and Near-Miss Reporting

A professional safety system learns from both injuries and near misses. A near miss is an event that could have caused harm but did not. Recording these events can reveal hazards before a serious incident occurs.

Manager Response

  1. Make the immediate area safe.
  2. Ensure injured employees receive appropriate assistance.
  3. Notify the responsible manager according to procedure.
  4. Record the incident or near miss.
  5. Identify the contributing hazard or unsafe condition.
  6. Apply corrective action.
  7. Review whether the control actually prevents recurrence.

19. Daily Kitchen Safety Inspection

  • Floors clean, dry and free from unnecessary obstruction.
  • Walkways and exits clear.
  • Knives stored safely.
  • Cooking equipment operating normally.
  • Electrical cords and plugs visually satisfactory.
  • Gas equipment and ventilation appear normal.
  • Fire safety equipment accessible.
  • Chemicals labelled and stored correctly.
  • Waste containers controlled and positioned safely.
  • Emergency access routes clear.
  • Employees wearing required PPE.
  • Unsafe equipment removed from service where necessary.
  • Maintenance issues reported.
  • Any incidents or near misses recorded.

20. Kitchen Opening Safety Checklist

  • Inspect floors, walkways and work areas.
  • Check that exits and emergency routes are clear.
  • Check equipment for visible damage.
  • Confirm cooking equipment is safe to operate.
  • Check required ventilation systems.
  • Check that chemicals are stored correctly.
  • Check knives and cutting equipment.
  • Confirm required PPE is available.
  • Review outstanding maintenance or safety issues.
  • Brief the team on significant hazards or operational changes.

21. Kitchen Closing Safety Checklist

  • Cooking equipment shut down according to procedure.
  • Relevant gas controls handled according to site procedure.
  • Electrical equipment switched off where required.
  • Hot equipment allowed to cool safely.
  • Floors cleaned and hazards removed.
  • Knives and equipment stored safely.
  • Chemicals returned to approved storage.
  • Waste removed according to procedure.
  • Emergency routes remain clear.
  • Safety incidents and maintenance issues handed over.

22. Kitchen Manager Safety Responsibilities

The kitchen manager sets the standard. Safety cannot be delegated entirely to the safety department or maintenance team.

  • Identify workplace hazards.
  • Provide practical training.
  • Enforce approved safety procedures.
  • Correct unsafe behaviour.
  • Remove unsafe equipment from service.
  • Follow up maintenance issues.
  • Review incidents and near misses.
  • Conduct regular inspections.
  • Maintain required safety records.
  • Lead by example.

23. Safety During Busy Service

The busiest period is often when safety controls are most likely to fail. Managers should actively protect safety during peak service rather than accepting shortcuts because the kitchen is busy.

Communication

Use clear calls and warnings when moving hot pans, knives, trays or equipment through shared spaces.

Organisation

Keep stations stocked and organised so employees are not forced to improvise during service.

Traffic Flow

Maintain predictable movement routes and avoid unnecessary congestion around pass, fryer and cooking areas.

Immediate Correction

Correct dangerous behaviour immediately rather than waiting until the end of service.

24. Safety Culture

A strong safety culture exists when employees understand that production targets never justify knowingly unsafe work. Supervisors should encourage employees to report hazards without fear of being ignored or punished for raising legitimate concerns.

Good safety leadership is visible. Managers should wear the required PPE, follow procedures, correct hazards and demonstrate that safety is part of professional performance.

26. Common Kitchen Safety Failures

  • Wet floors left unattended.
  • Knives placed in sinks.
  • Damaged electrical equipment still being used.
  • Blocked exits or fire equipment.
  • Chemicals stored beside food.
  • Employees using equipment without training.
  • Unsafe lifting of heavy containers.
  • Gas concerns ignored because service is busy.
  • Near misses not reported.
  • Safety inspections completed only on paper.
  • Managers accepting unsafe shortcuts.

27. Manager's Quick Safety Questions

  • What are the three biggest hazards in my kitchen today?
  • Has anything changed since the last shift?
  • Is any equipment unsafe or awaiting repair?
  • Are exits and emergency routes clear?
  • Are employees using required PPE?
  • Do new employees understand the equipment they are using?
  • Have there been any near misses?
  • Are corrective actions actually being completed?
  • Am I demonstrating the safety standards I expect from the team?

28. Kitchen Safety Implementation Checklist

  • Identify major kitchen hazards.
  • Create equipment-specific safe operating procedures.
  • Train employees before assigning hazardous tasks.
  • Provide appropriate PPE.
  • Maintain emergency procedures.
  • Keep exits and emergency equipment accessible.
  • Conduct daily safety inspections.
  • Report incidents and near misses.
  • Track corrective actions.
  • Review safety performance regularly.
  • Update procedures when equipment, processes or risks change.

29. Final Principles for Kitchen Safety

  1. Prevent hazards before they become incidents.
  2. Never sacrifice safety for speed.
  3. Train employees before assigning hazardous tasks.
  4. Keep work areas organised.
  5. Remove unsafe equipment from service.
  6. Report and learn from near misses.
  7. Keep emergency routes accessible.
  8. Use the correct PPE and equipment.
  9. Make safety part of every shift briefing.
  10. Managers must lead by example.
Academy takeaway: A safe kitchen is an organised kitchen. Good workflow, clean work areas, proper equipment maintenance, effective training and strong supervision reduce risk while improving operational performance.

30. Important Safety Disclaimer

It does not replace applicable occupational health and safety legislation, fire codes, electrical regulations, gas regulations, building requirements, emergency plans, manufacturer instructions, workplace risk assessments or competent professional advice. Requirements vary by location, property and equipment. Always follow the current requirements and approved procedures applicable to your workplace.
