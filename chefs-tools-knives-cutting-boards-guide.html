ðŸ”§ Honing Steel (Sharpening Steel)

Daily maintenance Edge alignment

Description: A long (25-35cm) cylindrical or oval rod made from hardened steel, ceramic, or diamond-coated material, fitted with a handle and guard.

Function & Use: A honing steel does not sharpen a knife â€” it realigns the microscopic edge of the blade that folds over with regular use. Used before every service or every 15-20 minutes of heavy use. Hold the steel vertically with the tip on a stable surface, and draw each side of the blade down at a 20-degree angle in smooth, controlled strokes. A well-honed knife feels significantly sharper. Ceramic and diamond steels also remove small amounts of metal and provide a degree of actual sharpening.

Professional Standard: A chef who steels their knife before every use produces cleaner, more precise cuts with less effort and significantly less risk of injury.

ðŸ”§ Whetstone (Sharpening Stone)

Description: A rectangular stone of varying grit â€” coarse (120-400 grit for damaged or very dull blades), medium (800-1200 grit for regular sharpening), and fine (2000-8000 grit for finishing and polishing the edge).

Function & Use: Whetstones are the only tools that truly sharpen a knife by removing metal to create a new edge. The blade is drawn across the wet stone at a consistent 15-20 degree angle. Japanese knives use a single-bevel sharpening technique; Western knives use a double-bevel. Regular whetstone sharpening (once weekly in a professional kitchen) keeps knives at their optimal performance.

ðŸŽ¨ The Color-Coded Cutting Board System

The color-coded cutting board system is one of the most important food safety protocols in any professional kitchen. Mandated by HACCP (Hazard Analysis Critical Control Points) food safety standards and required by health inspectors globally, the system prevents cross-contamination between different food categories â€” particularly between raw proteins, ready-to-eat foods, and allergen-containing ingredients.

Each color board is designated for a specific food category and must never be used for any other purpose. Staff must be trained on this system from day one â€” a violation is a food safety incident.

RED BOARD

ðŸ”´ Red â€” Raw Red Meat

Use for: Raw beef, lamb, pork, veal, venison, and all raw red meats. Any raw protein that is red in color uses this board.

Why separate: Raw red meat carries Salmonella, E. coli O157:H7, Campylobacter, and other pathogens that can cause severe foodborne illness if transferred to other foods â€” particularly ready-to-eat items.

YELLOW BOARD

ðŸŸ¡ Yellow â€” Raw Poultry

Use for: Raw chicken, turkey, duck, goose, quail, and all raw poultry and game birds.

Why separate: Raw poultry â€” particularly chicken â€” is the highest-risk protein for Campylobacter and Salmonella contamination. Keeping poultry on its own dedicated yellow board and ensuring thorough cleaning after every use is critical. Many foodborne illness outbreaks in commercial kitchens trace back to poultry cross-contamination.

BLUE BOARD

ðŸ”µ Blue â€” Raw Fish & Seafood

Use for: All raw fish (salmon, tuna, sea bass, cod), shellfish (prawns, lobster, crab), and all other raw seafood.

Why separate: Raw seafood carries Vibrio, Listeria, and other marine pathogens. Seafood also has strong flavors and aromas that transfer easily to other foods. The blue board keeps seafood prep entirely isolated â€” particularly important for operations serving raw preparations like sashimi, ceviche, and oysters.

GREEN BOARD

ðŸŸ¢ Green â€” Fruits & Vegetables

Use for: All fresh fruits, salad leaves, vegetables, and herbs. Both raw (for salads and garnishes) and those to be cooked.

Why separate: Produce must never contact surfaces that have been used for raw proteins. Cross-contamination from raw meat or poultry boards onto salad vegetables â€” which will be eaten without cooking â€” is a direct route to foodborne illness. The green board is typically the most-used board in a vegetarian or garde manger section.

WHITE BOARD

â¬œ White â€” Dairy, Bread & Bakery

Use for: Cheese, butter, dairy products, bread, pastry, and all bakery items.

Why separate: Dairy products are highly absorbent and will pick up flavors, odors, and pathogens from surfaces that have been used for other foods. The white board is also the standard board for general pastry work â€” rolling pastry, cutting dough, and handling baked goods. In many establishments, the white board is also used for ready-to-eat foods that require no further cooking.

BROWN BOARD

ðŸŸ¤ Brown â€” Cooked Meats

Use for: All cooked meats â€” roasted beef, cooked chicken, smoked meats, cured meats like salami and prosciutto, and all meats that have already been through a heat process.

Why separate: Cooked meats must never go back onto a surface that has been used for raw proteins â€” this is called recontamination and can transfer dangerous pathogens onto food that will not be cooked again. The brown board is essential in the charcuterie and cold kitchen sections.

PURPLE BOARD

ðŸŸ£ Purple â€” Allergen Use

Use for: Preparing dishes for guests with severe food allergies â€” gluten-free preparation, nut-free dishes, or any allergen-specific preparation that requires a completely clean, dedicated surface.

Why separate: For guests with severe allergies, even trace amounts of allergens (gluten, nuts, shellfish, dairy) transferred from a shared board can cause anaphylactic reactions. The purple board is dedicated solely to allergen-controlled preparation and should be stored separately, washed separately, and clearly labeled. This is the newest addition to the standard color system and is now recognized internationally.

  • Wash, rinse, and sanitize boards between EVERY use â€” not just at end of shift
  • Replace boards when they develop deep grooves and cut marks â€” bacteria harbor in grooves
  • Plastic boards are dishwasher-safe and preferred in commercial kitchens over wood
  • Never use the same cloth or sponge to clean different colored boards
  • Store boards vertically in a dedicated rack â€” flat storage traps moisture and bacteria

ðŸ”¨ Meat Mallet (Meat Tenderizer)

Tenderizing Flattening Two-sided head

Description: A heavy hammer-shaped tool with a handle and a weighted head, typically made from stainless steel or aluminum. Most mallets are double-sided: one side has a flat smooth surface; the other has a textured, pyramid-studded surface (toothed face).

Function & Use:

  • Flat side: Used to pound and flatten cuts of meat â€” escalopes, chicken breasts, veal cutlets â€” to an even thickness for uniform cooking. A chicken breast pounded flat becomes a paillard; a veal cutlet becomes a scaloppine or schnitzel. Even thickness ensures the entire piece cooks at the same rate, preventing overcooked edges and undercooked centers.
  • Textured (toothed) side: Used to physically break down the tough muscle fibers and connective tissue in tougher cuts of meat â€” round steak, chuck, flank steak. The pyramid studs pierce and tear the fibers, making the meat more tender and allowing marinades to penetrate more deeply.

Professional Use: Place meat between two sheets of cling film or in a zip-lock bag before pounding â€” this prevents the meat from tearing and keeps your mallet and board clean. Always pound with controlled, even strokes from the center outward.

Function & Use: Used in pastry for driving pastry rings and molds into place, breaking up blocks of chocolate, cracking lobster and crab claws at the table, and general pounding tasks that require firm impact without the tenderizing effect. Description: A handheld device with multiple stainless steel blades or needles arranged in rows. When pressed into the surface of meat, the needles pierce hundreds of tiny holes simultaneously.

Function & Use: Provides deep mechanical tenderization without changing the shape of the meat â€” unlike a mallet, which flattens and spreads the cut. Particularly effective for thick steaks and chops. The needle holes also dramatically increase marinade absorption â€” a needled steak can absorb three times as much marinade as an un-needled one in the same time. Used extensively in banquet kitchens where volume and speed are priorities.
